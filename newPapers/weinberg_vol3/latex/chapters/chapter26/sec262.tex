\subsection{General Superfields}

\providecommand{\gradedbracket}[2]{\left[#1,#2\right\}}
\providecommand{\SuperQ}{\mathcal{Q}}
\providecommand{\SuperD}{\mathcal{D}}

It is straightforward to construct supermultiplets of fields by the direct
technique illustrated in the previous section, but in order to construct
supersymmetric actions we also need to know how to multiply field
supermultiplets to make other supermultiplets. A great deal of work can be
saved by using a formalism invented by Salam and
Strathdee~\hyperref[ch26-ref-1]{[1]}, in which the fields in any
supermultiplet are assembled into a single superfield.

Just as the four-momentum operators $P_\mu$ are defined as the generators of
translations of the ordinary spacetime coordinates $x^\mu$, the four
supersymmetry generators $Q_a$ and $Q_a^\dagger$ may be regarded as the
generators of translations of four fermionic c-number superspace coordinates,
which anticommute with each other and with fermionic fields but commute with
the $x^\mu$ and all bosonic fields. We aim at constructing Lorentz-invariant
Lagrangian densities, so it will be convenient to adopt the four-component
Dirac formalism described in Section 25.2. The supersymmetry generators are
gathered into a four-component Majorana spinor $Q_\alpha$, and
correspondingly, the superspace coordinates are gathered into another
four-component Majorana spinor $\theta_\alpha$. (Various properties of
Majorana spinors are outlined in the appendix to this chapter.) The
supersymmetry generators have non-vanishing anticommutators, so we cannot take
them as simply proportional to the supercoordinate translation operators
$\partial/\partial\theta_\alpha$. Instead, Salam and Strathdee found that the
supersymmetry algebra would be satisfied if we suppose that the commutator or
anticommutator of the supersymmetry generator $Q$ with any bosonic or
fermionic superfield $S(x,\theta)$ is
\begin{equation}
  \gradedbracket{Q}{S}=i\SuperQ S\,,
  \tag{26.2.1}\label{eq:26.2.1}
\end{equation}
where $\SuperQ$ is the superspace differential operator
\begin{equation}
  \SuperQ\equiv
  -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\theta}
  +\gamma^\mu\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.2}\label{eq:26.2.2}
\end{equation}
(As usual $\bar\theta\equiv\theta^\dagger\beta$. All derivatives with respect
to fermionic c-number variables should be understood as
\emph{left}-derivatives, calculated by moving the variable to the left of any
expression before differentiating with respect to it.) For Majorana spinors
$\bar\theta=\theta^T\gamma_5\epsilon$, with the $4\cdot4$ matrix $\epsilon$
given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.3}, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.1} may be expressed more
explicitly as
\begin{equation}
  \SuperQ_\alpha
  =\sum_\gamma(\gamma_5\epsilon)_{\alpha\gamma}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_\gamma}
  +\sum_\gamma\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\gamma}\theta_\gamma
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.3}\label{eq:26.2.3}
\end{equation}
Likewise,
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\SuperQ}_\beta
  =\sum_\gamma\SuperQ_\gamma(\gamma_5\epsilon)_{\gamma\beta}
  =\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_\beta}
  -\sum_\gamma(\gamma_5\epsilon\gamma^\mu)_{\beta\gamma}\theta_\gamma
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.4}\label{eq:26.2.4}
\end{equation}
It is straightforward to calculate that
\begin{equation}
  \{\SuperQ_\alpha,\bar{\SuperQ}_\beta\}
  =(\gamma_5\epsilon\gamma^\mu\gamma_5\epsilon)_{\beta\alpha}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
  +\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.5}\label{eq:26.2.5}
\end{equation}
But Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.35} shows that
$\gamma_\mu^T=-\mathcal{C}\gamma_\mu\mathcal{C}^{-1}$, where $\mathcal{C}$ is
the matrix $\mathcal{C}=-\gamma_5\epsilon$, so both terms on the right-hand
side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.5} are equal, and therefore
\begin{equation}
  \{\SuperQ_\alpha,\bar{\SuperQ}_\beta\}
  =2\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.6}\label{eq:26.2.6}
\end{equation}
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.2.6} and \eqref{eq:26.2.1} together with the generalized
Jacobi identities \eqref{eq:25.1.5} show that
\begin{equation}
  [\{Q_\alpha,\bar Q_\beta\},S]
  =\{\SuperQ_\alpha,\bar{\SuperQ}_\beta\}S
  =2\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\mu S
  =-2i\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}[P_\mu,S]\,,
  \tag{26.2.7}\label{eq:26.2.7}
\end{equation}
in agreement with the anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:25.2.36}.

It is often more convenient to express the commutation and anticommutation
relations \eqref{eq:26.2.1} as transformation rules under infinitesimal
supersymmetry transformations. Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.18},
\eqref{eq:26.2.1}, and \eqref{eq:26.2.2} shows that a supersymmetry
transformation with infinitesimal Majorana spinor parameter $\alpha$ changes a
superfield $S(x,\theta)$ by an amount
\begin{equation}
  \delta S=(\bar\alpha\SuperQ)S
  =-\left(\bar\alpha\frac{\partial S}{\partial\bar\theta}\right)
  +(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)\frac{\partial S}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.8}\label{eq:26.2.8}
\end{equation}
Recall that $\partial/\partial\bar\theta$ here acts on the left of any
expression. In particular, where $M$ is any linear combination of the
matrices $\mathbf{1}$, $\gamma_5\gamma_\mu$, and $\gamma_5$ for which
$\bar\theta M\theta$ does not vanish, we have
$\bar\theta'M\theta''=\bar\theta''M\theta'$, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\theta}
  (\bar\theta M\theta)=2M\theta\,.
  \tag{26.2.9}\label{eq:26.2.9}
\end{equation}

The components of $\theta$ anticommute, so any product of their components
vanishes if two of them are the same component. But $\theta$ has only four
components, so any function of $\theta$ has a power series that terminates
with its quartic term. Furthermore, as shown in the appendix to this chapter,
a product of two $\theta$s is proportional to a linear combination of
$(\bar\theta\theta)$, $(\bar\theta\gamma_\mu\gamma_5\theta)$, and
$(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)$; a product of three $\theta$s is proportional to
$(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)\theta$; and a product of four $\theta$s is
proportional to $(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)^2$. The most general function of
$x^\mu$ and $\theta$ may therefore be expressed as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  S(x,\theta)={}&C(x)-i\bigl(\bar\theta\gamma_5\omega(x)\bigr)
  -\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)M(x)
  -\frac{1}{2}(\bar\theta\theta)N(x)\\
  &+\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\theta)V^\mu(x)
  -i(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta\left[\lambda(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sl{\partial}\omega(x)\right]\right)\\
  &-\frac{1}{4}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)^2
    \left(D(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Box C(x)\right).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.2.10}\label{eq:26.2.10}
\end{equation}
(The terms $\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega$ and $\frac12\Box C(x)$ are separated
from $\lambda(x)$ and $D(x)$, respectively, for later convenience.) If
$S(x,\theta)$ is a scalar then $C(x)$, $M(x)$, $N(x)$, and $D(x)$ are scalar
(or pseudoscalar) fields; $\omega(x)$ and $\lambda(x)$ are four-component
spinor fields; and $V^\mu(x)$ is a vector field. Also, using the reality
properties of bilinear products of Majorana fields given in the appendix to
this chapter, we see that if $S(x,\theta)$ is real, then $C(x)$, $M(x)$,
$N(x)$, $V^\mu(x)$, and $D(x)$ are all real, while $\omega(x)$ and
$\lambda(x)$ are Majorana spinors satisfying the phase convention
$s^*=-\beta\gamma_5\epsilon s$.

Now we must work out the supersymmetry transformation properties of the
component fields in Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.10}. Applying Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.2.8}
and \eqref{eq:26.2.9} to the expansion \eqref{eq:26.2.10} gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta S={}&(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)\frac{\partial C}{\partial x^\mu}
  +i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\omega)
  -i(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta\gamma_5\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x^\mu}\right)\\
  &+i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\theta)M
  -\frac{i}{2}(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \frac{\partial M}{\partial x^\mu}\\
  &+(\bar\alpha\theta)N
  -\frac{1}{2}(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\theta)
    \frac{\partial N}{\partial x^\mu}\\
  &-i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\gamma_\nu\theta)V^\nu
  +\frac{i}{2}(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)
    (\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma_\nu\theta)
    \frac{\partial V^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\\
  &+2i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta\left[\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)
  +i(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha\left[\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)\\
  &-i(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta\partial_\mu
      \left[\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)\\
  &+(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\theta)
    \left[D+\frac{1}{2}\Box C\right].
  \end{aligned}
\]
We need to put each term in the standard form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.10}. For
this purpose, we note that: Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.9} gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  (\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\gamma_5\partial_\mu\omega)
  ={}&-\frac14(\bar\theta\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\sl{\partial}\gamma_5\omega)
  -\frac14(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma^\nu\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\sl{\partial}\gamma_\nu\omega)\\
  &-\frac14(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\sl{\partial}\omega)\,;
  \end{aligned}
\]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.16} gives
\[
  (\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\theta)
  =-(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\gamma_5\theta)
    (\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)\,;
\]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.17} gives
\[
  (\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma_\nu\theta)
  =-(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\gamma_\nu\theta)
    (\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)\,;
\]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.9} gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  &(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta\left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)\\
  &\quad=-\frac14(\bar\theta\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha\gamma_5
      \left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)
  +\frac14(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma^\mu\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha\gamma_\mu
      \left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)\\
  &\qquad-\frac14(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha
      \left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)\,;
  \end{aligned}
\]
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.19} gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  &(\bar\alpha\gamma^\mu\theta)(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta\partial_\mu
      \left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)\\
  &\qquad=-\frac14
    \left(\bar\alpha\sl{\partial}\gamma_5
      \left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]\right)
    (\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)^2.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Using these relations and rearranging terms in order of increasing numbers of
$\theta$ factors, we have
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta S={}&i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\omega)
  +\left(\bar\alpha
    [\sl{\partial}C+i\gamma_5M+N-i\gamma_5\sl{V}]\theta\right)\\
  &-\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha\gamma_5[\lambda+\sl{\partial}\omega]\right)
  +\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha[\lambda+\sl{\partial}\omega]\right)\\
  &+\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma^\mu\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\gamma_\mu\lambda)
  +\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma^\nu\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\partial_\nu\omega)\\
  &+\frac12(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha
      [-i\sl{\partial}M-\gamma_5\sl{\partial}N
       -i\sl{\partial}\sl{V}
       +\gamma_5(D+\tfrac12\Box C)]\theta\right)\\
  &-\frac{i}{4}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)^2
    \left(\bar\alpha\gamma_5
      [\sl{\partial}\lambda+\tfrac12\Box\omega]\right),
  \end{aligned}
\]
or, using the symmetry property \eqref{eq:26.A.7},
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta S={}&i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\omega)
  +\left(\bar\theta
    [-\sl{\partial}C+i\gamma_5M+N-i\gamma_5\sl{V}]\alpha\right)\\
  &-\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha\gamma_5[\lambda+\sl{\partial}\omega]\right)
  +\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\alpha[\lambda+\sl{\partial}\omega]\right)\\
  &+\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma^\mu\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\gamma_\mu\lambda)
  +\frac{i}{2}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\gamma^\nu\theta)
    (\bar\alpha\partial_\nu\omega)\\
  &+\frac12(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)
    \left(\bar\theta
      [i\sl{\partial}M-\gamma_5\sl{\partial}N
       -i\partial_\mu\sl{V}\gamma^\mu
       +\gamma_5(D+\tfrac12\Box C)]\alpha\right)\\
  &-\frac{i}{4}(\bar\theta\gamma_5\theta)^2
    \left(\bar\alpha\gamma_5
      [\sl{\partial}\lambda+\tfrac12\Box\omega]\right).
  \end{aligned}
\]
If we now compare this with the terms up to second order in $\theta$ in the
expansion \eqref{eq:26.2.10}, we find the transformation rules:
\begin{equation}
  \delta C=i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\omega)\,,
  \tag{26.2.11}\label{eq:26.2.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta\omega
  =(-i\gamma_5\sl{\partial}C-M+i\gamma_5N+\sl{V})\alpha\,,
  \tag{26.2.12}\label{eq:26.2.12}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta M=-(\bar\alpha[\lambda+\sl{\partial}\omega])\,,
  \tag{26.2.13}\label{eq:26.2.13}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta N=i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5[\lambda+\sl{\partial}\omega])\,,
  \tag{26.2.14}\label{eq:26.2.14}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta V_\mu=(\bar\alpha\gamma_\mu\lambda)
  +(\bar\alpha\partial_\mu\omega)\,.
  \tag{26.2.15}\label{eq:26.2.15}
\end{equation}
The terms of third and fourth order in $\theta$ give
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta\left[\lambda+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega\right]
  &=\frac12\left[
    -\sl{\partial}M-i\gamma_5\sl{\partial}N
    +\partial_\mu\sl{V}\gamma^\mu
    +i\gamma_5\left(D+\frac12\Box C\right)\right]\alpha,\\
  \delta\left[D+\frac12\Box C\right]
  &=i\left(\bar\alpha\gamma_5
    \left[\sl{\partial}\lambda+\frac12\Box\omega\right]\right).
  \end{aligned}
\]
Combining the latter two transformation rules with the transformation rules
\eqref{eq:26.2.11} and \eqref{eq:26.2.12} for $C$ and $\omega$ yields much
simpler transformation rules for $\lambda$ and $D$:
\begin{equation}
  \delta\lambda
  =\left(\frac12[\partial_\mu\sl{V},\gamma^\mu]
    +i\gamma_5D\right)\alpha\,,
  \tag{26.2.16}\label{eq:26.2.16}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta D=i(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\sl{\partial}\lambda)\,.
  \tag{26.2.17}\label{eq:26.2.17}
\end{equation}
It is to achieve this simplification that the terms
$\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega$ and $\frac12\Box C$ were separated from
$\lambda$ and $D$ in the expansion \eqref{eq:26.2.10}.

The whole point of the superfield formalism is to simplify the task of making
supermultiplets out of other supermultiplets. Given two superfields $S_1$ and
$S_2$ that both satisfy the transformation rule \eqref{eq:26.2.8}, their
product $S\equiv S_1S_2$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta S&=[(\bar\alpha Q),S_1S_2]
  =(\delta S_1)S_2+S_1(\delta S_2)\\
  &=\bigl((\bar\alpha\SuperQ)S_1\bigr)S_2
    +S_1\bigl((\bar\alpha\SuperQ)S_2\bigr)
  =(\bar\alpha\SuperQ)S\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.2.18}\label{eq:26.2.18}
\end{equation}
and is therefore also a superfield. A straightforward calculation using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.A.7}, \eqref{eq:26.A.16}, \eqref{eq:26.A.18}, and
\eqref{eq:26.A.19} gives its components as
\begin{equation}
  C=C_1C_2\,,
  \tag{26.2.19}\label{eq:26.2.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \omega=C_1\omega_2+C_2\omega_1\,,
  \tag{26.2.20}\label{eq:26.2.20}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  M=C_1M_2+C_2M_1+\frac{i}{2}(\bar\omega_1\gamma_5\omega_2)\,,
  \tag{26.2.21}\label{eq:26.2.21}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  N=C_1N_2+C_2N_1-\frac12(\bar\omega_1\omega_2)\,,
  \tag{26.2.22}\label{eq:26.2.22}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  V^\mu=C_1V_2^\mu+C_2V_1^\mu
  -\frac{i}{2}(\bar\omega_1\gamma_5\gamma^\mu\omega_2)\,,
  \tag{26.2.23}\label{eq:26.2.23}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \lambda={}&C_1\lambda_2+C_2\lambda_1
  -\frac12\gamma^\mu\omega_1\partial_\mu C_2
  -\frac12\gamma^\mu\omega_2\partial_\mu C_1
  +\frac{i}{2}\sl{V}_1\gamma_5\omega_2
  +\frac{i}{2}\sl{V}_2\gamma_5\omega_1\\
  &+\frac12(N_1-i\gamma_5M_1)\omega_2
  +\frac12(N_2-i\gamma_5M_2)\omega_1\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.2.24}\label{eq:26.2.24}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  D={}&-\partial_\mu C_1\partial^\mu C_2+C_1D_2+C_2D_1
  +M_1M_2+N_1N_2\\
  &-\left(\bar\omega_1
    \left[\lambda_2+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega_2\right]\right)
  -\left(\bar\omega_2
    \left[\lambda_1+\frac12\sl{\partial}\omega_1\right]\right)
  -V_{1\mu}V_2^\mu\,.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.2.25}\label{eq:26.2.25}
\end{equation}
It is trivial that linear combinations of superfields are superfields, in the
same sense, and that spacetime derivatives and complex conjugates of
superfields are superfields. But multiplying a superfield by some function of
$\theta$ or differentiating it with respect to $\theta$ does not in general
yield a superfield. (For instance, $\theta$ itself is evidently not a
superfield, because $\theta$ is a fermionic c-number and therefore commutes
with $\bar\alpha Q$, while $\SuperQ\theta\neq0$.) There is, however, a way of
combining a derivative of a superfield with respect to $\theta$ and
multiplying it by a factor $\theta$ which does yield another superfield.

Consider the superspace differential operator $\SuperD_\alpha$ defined by
\begin{equation}
  \SuperD\equiv
  -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\theta}
  -\gamma^\mu\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,,
  \tag{26.2.26}\label{eq:26.2.26}
\end{equation}
or more explicitly
\begin{equation}
  \SuperD_\alpha
  =\sum_\gamma(\gamma_5\epsilon)_{\alpha\gamma}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_\gamma}
  -\sum_\gamma\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\gamma}\theta_\gamma
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.27}\label{eq:26.2.27}
\end{equation}
The only difference between the definitions of $\SuperD$ and $\SuperQ$ is a
change of sign of the terms involving the spacetime derivative. In
consequence of this change of sign, in the anticommutator of $\SuperD_\beta$
with $\SuperQ_\alpha$, instead of getting two equal terms like those in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.5} with the same sign, we get them with opposite signs, so
that they cancel:
\begin{equation}
  \{\SuperD_\beta,\SuperQ_\alpha\}=0\,.
  \tag{26.2.28}\label{eq:26.2.28}
\end{equation}
Since $\alpha$ is fermionic, it follows that $(\bar\alpha\SuperQ)$ commutes
with $\SuperD_\beta$, so if $S(x,\theta)$ is a superfield, then
\begin{equation}
  \delta\SuperD_\beta S
  \equiv-i[(\bar\alpha Q),\SuperD_\beta S]
  =-i\SuperD_\beta[(\bar\alpha Q),S]
  =\SuperD_\beta(\bar\alpha\SuperQ)S
  =(\bar\alpha\SuperQ)\SuperD_\beta S\,,
  \tag{26.2.29}\label{eq:26.2.29}
\end{equation}
so that $\SuperD_\beta S$ is also a superfield.
\emph{Thus an arbitrary polynomial function of superfields $S$ and their
superderivatives $\SuperD_\beta S$, $\SuperD_\beta\SuperD_\gamma S$, etc.\
is also a superfield.}

It is not necessary to add here that in constructing a superfield out of other
superfields we can also include their spacetime derivatives, because these can
be obtained from second superderivatives. Since the only difference between
$\SuperD_\beta$ and $\SuperQ_\beta$ is in the sign of the term involving
$\partial_\mu$, the anticommutators of the $\SuperD$s are the same as those of
the $\SuperQ$s, except for a change of sign:
\begin{equation}
  \{\SuperD_\alpha,\bar{\SuperD}_\beta\}
  =-2\gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,.
  \tag{26.2.30}\label{eq:26.2.30}
\end{equation}

Now let us consider how to construct a supersymmetric action out of
superfields. There is no such thing as a supersymmetric Lagrangian density,
because the anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:26.2.6} shows that if
$\delta\mathcal{L}=0$ then $\mathcal{L}$ must be a constant. Even if the
Lagrangian density is not supersymmetric, the action will still be
supersymmetric if $\delta\mathcal{L}(x)$ is a derivative, which would not
contribute to $\delta\int\mathcal{L}\,d^4x$. In general, the Lagrangian
density $\mathcal{L}$ can be written as a sum of terms, each of which is some
component of a superfield that is constructed out of elementary superfields
and their superderivatives. Inspection of the transformation rules
\eqref{eq:26.2.11}--\eqref{eq:26.2.17} for the individual components shows
that in the absence of any special conditions on a general superfield, the
only component of such a superfield whose variation is a derivative is the
$D$-component. Also, for the $D$-component of any superfield to be a scalar,
the superfield itself must be a scalar. Therefore, unless there are special
conditions on the individual superfields from which the Lagrangian density is
constructed, a supersymmetric action can only be the integral of the
$D$-term of a scalar superfield $\Lambda$:
\begin{equation}
  I=\int d^4x\,[\Lambda]_D\,.
  \tag{26.2.31}\label{eq:26.2.31}
\end{equation}
But in fact no action of this sort would be physically satisfactory without
special conditions on the superfields from which it is constructed. For a
general superfield $S(x,\theta)$, the only sort of supersymmetric kinematic
action $I_0$ that is bilinear in $S$ and $S^*$ and involves no more than two
derivatives of the component fields is of the form
\begin{equation}
  I_0\mathrel{\propto}\int d^4x\,[S^*S]_D\,.
  \tag{26.2.32}\label{eq:26.2.32}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.25}, we see that $S^*S$ has the $D$-component
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  [S^*S]_D={}&-\partial_\mu C^*\partial^\mu C
  -\frac12(\bar\omega\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\omega)
  +\frac12((\partial_\mu\bar\omega)\gamma^\mu\omega)\\
  &+C^*D+D^*C-(\bar\omega\lambda)-(\bar\lambda\omega)\\
  &+M^*M+N^*N-V_\mu^*V^\mu\,.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.2.33}\label{eq:26.2.33}
\end{equation}
The terms quadratic in $C$ or $\omega$ look promising as the kinematic
Lagrangians for massless fields of spins zero and $1/2$; the final three terms
are harmless; but the terms involving $D$ or $\lambda$ have the disastrous
effect in path integrals of constraining $C$ and $\omega$ to vanish.
Fortunately, as we shall see in the following section, there are constrained
superfields from which we can construct physically sensible actions. The
introduction of these constrained superfields will also open up ways of
constructing supersymmetric terms in the action that are not the
$D$-components of functions of superfields.

If parity is conserved, then the space inversion properties of the component
fields of a superfield will be related by supersymmetry. To work out this
relation, we apply the parity operator $P$ to the commutation/anticommutation
relation \eqref{eq:26.2.1} and use the transformation property
\eqref{eq:25.3.16} of supersymmetry generators, which gives
\begin{equation}
  i\beta\gradedbracket{Q}{P^{-1}S(x,\theta)P}
  =\SuperQ P^{-1}S(x,\theta)P\,.
  \tag{26.2.34}\label{eq:26.2.34}
\end{equation}
The solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.34} for a scalar superfield is of the form
\begin{equation}
  P^{-1}S(x,\theta)P
  =\eta S(\Lambda_Px,-i\beta\theta)\,,
  \tag{26.2.35}\label{eq:26.2.35}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is some phase (the intrinsic parity of the superfield) and
$\Lambda_Px\equiv(-\mathbf{x},+x^0)$. (To check that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.35} satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.34}, note that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.35} gives the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.34} as
\[
  i\eta\beta\left(
    -\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{(-i\beta\theta)}}
    +\gamma^\mu(-i\beta\theta)
      \frac{\partial}{\partial(\Lambda_Px)^\mu}
    \right)S(\Lambda_Px,\theta)
  =\eta\SuperQ S(\Lambda_Px,-i\beta\theta)\,,
\]
in agreement with what Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.35} gives for the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.34}.) Using the expansion \eqref{eq:26.2.10} in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.35} then gives the space inversion properties of the
component fields:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  P^{-1}C(x)P&=\eta C(\Lambda_Px),\\
  P^{-1}\omega(x)P&=-i\eta\beta\omega(\Lambda_Px),\\
  P^{-1}M(x)P&=-\eta M(\Lambda_Px),\\
  P^{-1}N(x)P&=\eta N(\Lambda_Px),\\
  P^{-1}V^\mu(x)P&=-\eta(\Lambda_P)^\mu{}_\nu V^\nu(\Lambda_Px),\\
  P^{-1}\lambda(x)P&=i\eta\beta\lambda(\Lambda_Px),\\
  P^{-1}D(x)P&=\eta D(\Lambda_Px).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.2.36}\label{eq:26.2.36}
\end{equation}

\(\ast\qquad\ast\qquad\ast\)
\end{center}

The general real superfield $S$ involves four real spinless fields $C$, $M$,
$N$, and $D$, plus one real four-vector field $V_\mu$, for a total of eight
independent bosonic field components. For comparison, there are two
four-component Majorana spinor fields $\omega$ and $\lambda$, also for a total
of eight independent field components. The equality of the numbers of
independent bosonic and fermionic field components holds in general not only
for the unconstrained general superfield studied in this section, but also for
all superfields obtained from the general superfield by imposing
supersymmetric constraints, such as the chiral and other constrained
superfields discussed in the next section.

To see this in general, suppose that we have a representation of the
supersymmetry algebra provided by $N_B$ linearly independent real bosonic
field operators $b_n(x)$ and $N_F$ linearly independent fermionic field
operators $f_k(x)$. We will assume that these fields satisfy only non-trivial
field equations, so that no linear combination of the $b_n$ or $f_k$ with
non-vanishing coefficients satisfies a homogeneous linear field equation.
Consider a real supersymmetry generator $Q(u)$, defined as
\begin{equation}
  Q(u)\equiv(\bar uQ)=(\bar Qu)\,,
  \tag{26.2.37}\label{eq:26.2.37}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is some ordinary numerical Majorana spinor (\emph{not} an
anticommuting c-number). (For extended supersymmetry, in place of $Q_\alpha$
we could use any one of the $Q_{r\alpha}$, say $Q_{1\alpha}$.) In order for
the $b_n$ and $f_k$ to furnish a representation of the supersymmetry algebra,
we must have
\begin{equation}
  [Q(u),b_n]=i\sum_k q_{nk}(\partial)f_k\,,
  \tag{26.2.38}\label{eq:26.2.38}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q(u),f_k\}=\sum_n p_{kn}(\partial)b_n\,,
  \tag{26.2.39}\label{eq:26.2.39}
\end{equation}
for some matrix differential operators $q(\partial)$ and $p(\partial)$.
Taking the anticommutator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.38} and the commutator of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.39} with $Q(u)$ gives
\begin{equation}
  [Q^2(u),b_n]
  =i\sum_m\bigl(q(\partial)p(\partial)\bigr)_{nm}b_m\,,
  \tag{26.2.40}\label{eq:26.2.40}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  [Q^2(u),f_k]
  =i\sum_\ell\bigl(p(\partial)q(\partial)\bigr)_{k\ell}f_\ell\,.
  \tag{26.2.41}\label{eq:26.2.41}
\end{equation}
The anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:25.2.36} or \eqref{eq:25.2.38} gives
the square of $Q(u)$ as $Q^2(u)=-iP_\mu(\bar u\gamma^\mu u)$. Hence the
square matrices $p(\partial)q(\partial)$ and $q(\partial)p(\partial)$ must
both be non-singular, because if there were any non-vanishing coefficients
$c_n(\partial)$ or $d_k(\partial)$ for which
$\sum_n c_n(\partial)(q(\partial)p(\partial))_{nm}=0$ or
$\sum_k d_k(\partial)(p(\partial)q(\partial))_{k\ell}=0$ then $b_n$ or $f_k$
would satisfy the homogeneous linear field equations
\[
  (\bar u\gamma^\mu u)\partial_\mu
    \sum_n c_n(\partial)b_n=0
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  (\bar u\gamma^\mu u)\partial_\mu
    \sum_k d_k(\partial)f_k=0\,,
\]
in contradiction with our assumption that the fields do not satisfy such
field equations. In order for $qp$ to be non-singular we must have
$N_F\geq N_B$, and in order for $pq$ to be non-singular we must have
$N_B\geq N_F$, so we can conclude that $N_B=N_F$. Also the square matrices
$q$ and $p$ must both be non-singular, so the complex conjugate of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.38} tells us that $f^*=q^{*-1}qf$, so that the number of
independent fermion fields is $N_F$ rather than $2N_F$, and is therefore
equal to the number $N_B$ of independent boson fields, as was to be shown.
