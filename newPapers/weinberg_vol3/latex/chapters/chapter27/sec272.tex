\subsection{Gauge-Invariant Action for Abelian Gauge Superfields}

We must now consider how to construct a gauge-invariant supersymmetric
action for the gauge superfields \(V^A(x,\theta)\) that contain the gauge
fields \(V_\mu^A(x)\). In order to motivate this construction we will first
consider the case of a single Abelian gauge field (dropping the superscript
\(A\)), and then return to the general case in the next section.

In an Abelian gauge theory like quantum electrodynamics, the gauge-invariant
field constructed from \(V_\mu(x)\) is the familiar field-strength tensor
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mu\nu}(x)
  =\partial_\mu V_\nu(x)-\partial_\nu V_\mu(x).
  \tag{27.2.1}\label{eq:27.2.1}
\end{equation}
The supersymmetry transformation rule for \(f_{\mu\nu}(x)\) is then given
by the transformation rule \eqref{eq:26.2.15} for \(V_\mu(x)\) as
\begin{equation}
  \delta f_{\mu\nu}
  =\bigl(\bar\alpha(\partial_\mu\gamma_\nu
    -\partial_\nu\gamma_\mu)\lambda\bigr).
  \tag{27.2.2}\label{eq:27.2.2}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.16} gives the transformation rule for \(\lambda(x)\) as
\begin{equation}
  \delta\lambda
  =\left(-\frac14 f_{\mu\nu}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]
    +i\gamma_5D\right)\alpha,
  \tag{27.2.3}\label{eq:27.2.3}
\end{equation}
while Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.17} gives the transformation rule for \(D(x)\):
\begin{equation}
  \delta D=i\bigl(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr).
  \tag{27.2.4}\label{eq:27.2.4}
\end{equation}
None of this depends on whether or not the superfield \(V^A(x,\theta)\) is
taken to be in Wess--Zumino gauge. We see that the fields
\(f_{\mu\nu}(x)\), \(\lambda(x)\), and \(D(x)\) form a complete
supersymmetry multiplet.

It is not hard to construct a suitable kinematic Lagrangian density for
the fields of this supermultiplet. The only Lorentz-invariant, parity
conserving, and gauge-invariant functions of these fields with
dimensionality four are \(f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}\),
\(\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda\), and \(D^2\). We can make \(V^\mu\) a
conventionally normalized vector field by taking the coefficient of
\(f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}\) to be \(-1/4\), so we may tentatively take the
kinematic Lagrangian density as
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}
  =-\frac14 f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}
  -c_\lambda\bigl(\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr)
  -c_DD^2,
\]
with coefficients \(c_\lambda\) and \(c_D\) to be determined from the
condition that \(\int\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}d^4x\) is supersymmetric.
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:27.2.2}--\eqref{eq:27.2.4}, an infinitesimal
supersymmetry transformation changes the operators in the Lagrangian by
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta\bigl(f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}\bigr)
  &=2f^{\mu\nu}
    \bigl(\bar\alpha(\gamma_\nu\partial_\mu
      -\gamma_\mu\partial_\nu)\lambda\bigr),
  \\
  \delta\bigl(\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr)
  &=2\left(
    \bar\alpha\left[-\frac14f_{\mu\nu}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]
      +i\gamma_5D\right]\sl{\partial}\lambda
  \right),
  \\
  \delta D^2&=2iD\bigl(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr),
  \end{aligned}
\]
in which we drop derivative terms that do not contribute to the variation
of the action. In order to see how these terms cancel, it is necessary to
use an identity for gamma matrices\footnote{To derive this, use the fact
that any \(4\cdot4\) matrix may be expressed as a linear combination of
the 16 independent covariant matrices described in Section 5.4, which in
our case is limited by Lorentz invariance and space inversion invariance
to the terms shown here. The coefficients of these terms can be calculated
by giving \(\mu\nu\rho\) the values 121 and 123.}
\begin{equation}
  [\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]\gamma^\rho
  =-2\eta^{\mu\rho}\gamma^\nu
  +2\eta^{\nu\rho}\gamma^\mu
  -2i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\gamma_\sigma\gamma_5.
  \tag{27.2.5}\label{eq:27.2.5}
\end{equation}
The term
\(-i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}f_{\mu\nu}
(\bar\alpha\gamma_\sigma\gamma_5\partial_\rho\lambda)\)
does not contribute to \(\int d^4x\,\delta\mathcal{L}\), because
integrating by parts yields a contribution proportional to
\(\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho f_{\mu\nu}\), which vanishes
for an \(f_{\mu\nu}\) of the form \eqref{eq:27.2.1}. This identity then
allows us to rewrite the variation of the \(\lambda\)-term as
\[
  \delta\bigl(\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr)
  =-f^{\mu\nu}
    \bigl(\bar\alpha(\gamma_\nu\partial_\mu
      -\gamma_\mu\partial_\nu)\lambda\bigr)
  +2iD\bigl(\bar\alpha\gamma_5\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr).
\]
The cancellation of terms proportional to \(f^{\mu\nu}\lambda\) requires
that \(c_\lambda=1/2\), while the cancellation of terms proportional to
\(D\lambda\) requires that \(c_D=-c_\lambda\), so the supersymmetric
Lagrangian density takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}
  =-\frac14f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}
  -\frac12\bigl(\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr)
  +\frac12D^2.
  \tag{27.2.6}\label{eq:27.2.6}
\end{equation}
This shows that with \(V^\mu\) canonically normalized, the field
\(\lambda\) related to \(V^\mu\) by the transformation rules
\eqref{eq:27.2.2} and \eqref{eq:27.2.3} is also canonically normalized.

In addition, for Abelian gauge theories there is a superrenormalizable
term, known as a \textit{Fayet--Iliopoulos term}%
~\hyperref[ch27-ref-2]{[2]}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FI}}=\xi D,
  \tag{27.2.7}\label{eq:27.2.7}
\end{equation}
with \(\xi\) an arbitrary constant. Its variation under a supersymmetry
transformation is shown by Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.4} to be a derivative, so
that it yields another supersymmetric term in the action. As we will see
in Section 27.5, the presence of such a term can provide a mechanism for
the spontaneous breakdown of supersymmetry.

Both for its own interest and as a tool in constructing supersymmetric
interactions involving the fields \(f_{\mu\nu}\), \(\lambda\), and \(D\),
it is interesting to ask what sort of superfield has these as component
fields. Somewhat surprisingly, it turns out to be a \textit{spinor
superfield} \(W_\alpha(x)\), with component fields (in the notation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.10}) given by
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  C_{(\alpha)}(x)&=\lambda_\alpha(x),
  \\
  \omega_{(\alpha)\beta}(x)
  &=\frac{i}{2}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\epsilon)_{\alpha\beta}
    f_{\mu\nu}(x)+(\gamma_5\epsilon)_{\alpha\beta}D(x),
  \\
  V_{(\alpha)\mu}(x)
  &=-i\partial_\mu\bigl(\gamma_5\lambda(x)\bigr)_\alpha,
  \\
  M_{(\alpha)}(x)
  &=-i\bigl(\sl{\partial}\gamma_5\lambda(x)\bigr)_\alpha,
  \qquad
  N_{(\alpha)}(x)=-\bigl(\sl{\partial}\lambda(x)\bigr)_\alpha,
  \\
  \lambda_{(\alpha)\beta}(x)&=D_{(\alpha)}(x)=0.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.8}\label{eq:27.2.8}
\end{equation}
(The subscript \(\alpha\) on these component fields is put inside
parentheses to emphasize that it labels the whole superfield.) It is
straightforward to use Eqs.~\eqref{eq:27.2.2}--\eqref{eq:27.2.4} to
check directly that the superfield components given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.8} do transform as in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.2.11}--\eqref{eq:26.2.17}.

Inserting the component fields \eqref{eq:27.2.8} in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.10}, and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.5}, we find that the
superfield \(W_\alpha\) takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  W_\alpha(x,\theta)=\Bigl[&
    \lambda(x)
    +\frac12\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\theta f_{\mu\nu}(x)
    -i\gamma_5\theta D(x)
    -\frac12(\theta^T\epsilon\theta)
      \sl{\partial}\gamma_5\lambda(x)
  \\
  &+\frac12(\theta^T\epsilon\gamma_5\theta)
      \sl{\partial}\lambda(x)
    +\frac12(\theta^T\epsilon\gamma^\mu\theta)
      \gamma_5\partial_\mu\lambda(x)
  \\
  &-\frac14(\theta^T\epsilon\theta)
      \gamma_5\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\sigma\theta\,
      \partial_\sigma f_{\mu\nu}(x)
  \\
  &+\frac{i}{2}(\theta^T\epsilon\theta)
      \gamma^\sigma\theta\,\partial_\sigma D(x)
    -\frac18(\theta^T\epsilon\theta)^2\Box\lambda(x)
  \Bigr]_\alpha.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.9}\label{eq:27.2.9}
\end{equation}
As we showed in Section 26.3, a superfield like this with zero
\(\lambda\)- and \(D\)-components is \textit{chiral}---that is, it is the
sum of left-chiral and right-chiral superfields
\begin{equation}
  W(x,\theta)=W_L(x,\theta)+W_R(x,\theta).
  \tag{27.2.10}\label{eq:27.2.10}
\end{equation}
Here the left- and right-chiral superfields are simply the projections of
\(W\) on the subspaces with \(\gamma_5=+1\) and \(\gamma_5=-1\),
respectively:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  W_L(x,\theta)
  &=\frac12(1+\gamma_5)W(x,\theta)
  \\
  &=\lambda_L(x_+)
    +\frac12\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\theta_L f_{\mu\nu}(x_+)
    +(\theta_L^T\epsilon\theta_L)
      \sl{\partial}\lambda_R(x_+)
    -i\theta_LD(x_+).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.11}\label{eq:27.2.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  W_R(x,\theta)
  &=\frac12(1-\gamma_5)W(x,\theta)
  \\
  &=\lambda_R(x_-)
    +\frac12\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\theta_R f_{\mu\nu}(x_-)
    -(\theta_R^T\epsilon\theta_R)
      \sl{\partial}\lambda_L(x_-)
    -i\theta_RD(x_-),
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.12}\label{eq:27.2.12}
\end{equation}
with \(x_\pm^\mu\) given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.3.23}.

As we saw in Section 26.3, we can construct suitable Lagrangian densities
from the \(\mathcal{F}\)-term of any scalar function of a left-chiral
superfield, plus its Hermitian adjoint. The simplest scalar function of the
left-chiral superfield \eqref{eq:27.2.11} is
\(\sum_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}W_{L\alpha}W_{L\beta}\).
To calculate the \(\mathcal{F}\)-term, we note that, when expressed as a
function of \(\theta_L\) and \(x_+\), the term in
\(\sum_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}W_{L\alpha}W_{L\beta}\) of
second order in \(\theta_L\) is
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  -\left[
    \sum_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
      W_{L\alpha}W_{L\beta}
  \right]_{\theta_L^2}
  ={}&(\theta_L^T\epsilon\theta_L)
    \left[
      -2\bigl(\lambda_L^T(x)\epsilon
        \sl{\partial}\lambda_R(x)\bigr)+D^2(x)
    \right]
  \\
  &+\frac1{16}
    \bigl(\overline{\theta_L}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]
      [\gamma^\rho,\gamma^\sigma]\theta_L\bigr)
    f_{\mu\nu}(x)f_{\rho\sigma}(x).
  \end{aligned}
\]
(The argument of the fields here can be taken as \(x^\mu\) rather than
\(x_+^\mu\) because the difference would yield terms that contain at least
three factors of \(\theta_L\), which therefore vanish.) Lorentz invariance
together with the fact that
\((\bar{s}[\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]s)\) and
\((\bar{s}[\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]\gamma_5s)\) vanish for any Majorana
fermion \(s\) tell us that the bilinear
\((\overline{\theta_L}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]
[\gamma^\rho,\gamma^\sigma]\theta_L)\) must be proportional to a linear
combination of
\((\overline{\theta_L}\theta_L)
(\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}-\eta^{\mu\sigma}\eta^{\nu\rho})\)
and \((\overline{\theta_L}\theta_L)\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\).
We can find the coefficients by giving \(\mu\nu\rho\sigma\) the values
1212 or 1230, and in this way we find that
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  &\bigl(\overline{\theta_L}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]
    [\gamma^\rho,\gamma^\sigma]\theta_L\bigr)
  \\
  &\qquad
  =4(\overline{\theta_L}\theta_L)
    \left[
      -\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}
      +\eta^{\mu\sigma}\eta^{\nu\rho}
      +i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
    \right].
  \end{aligned}
\]
The \(\mathcal{F}\)-term is the coefficient of
\((\overline{\theta_L}\theta_L)\), so
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  -\left[
    \sum_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
      W_{L\alpha}W_{L\beta}
  \right]_{\mathcal{F}}
  ={}&-2\bigl(\overline{\lambda_R}\sl{\partial}\lambda_R\bigr)
    -\frac12f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}
  \\
  &+\frac{i}{4}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
    f_{\mu\nu}f_{\rho\sigma}+D^2.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.13}\label{eq:27.2.13}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.21} shows that
\((\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda)\) is real, while
\((\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\gamma_5\lambda)\) is imaginary, so the real
part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.13} yields the Lagrangian
\eqref{eq:27.2.6} for the gauge and gaugino fields
\begin{equation}
  -\frac12\operatorname{Re}\left[
    \sum_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
      W_{L\alpha}W_{L\beta}
  \right]_{\mathcal{F}}
  =-\frac12\bigl(\bar\lambda\sl{\partial}\lambda\bigr)
  -\frac14f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu}
  +\frac12D^2.
  \tag{27.2.14}\label{eq:27.2.14}
\end{equation}
The physical significance of the imaginary part will be discussed in a
more general context in the next section.

There is another way to derive the form of the spinor superfield, which
will turn out to provide a more convenient way of deriving the components
of the gauge superfield in non-Abelian gauge theories. A tedious but
straightforward calculation shows that the gauge-invariant superfield
\eqref{eq:27.2.9} may be expressed in terms of the gauge superfield
\eqref{eq:27.1.16} as
\begin{equation}
  W_\alpha(x,\theta)
  =\frac{i}{4}(\SuperD^T\epsilon\SuperD)
    \SuperD_\alpha V(x,\theta),
  \tag{27.2.15}\label{eq:27.2.15}
\end{equation}
where \(\SuperD_\alpha\) is the superderivative introduced in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.26}:
\[
  \SuperD_\alpha
  \equiv\sum_\beta(\gamma_5\epsilon)_{\alpha\beta}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_\beta}
    -(\gamma^\mu\theta)_\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
  =-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\theta_\alpha}
    -(\gamma^\mu\theta)_\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}.
\]
This result might have been obtained (aside from the normalization factor)
by noting that the function \eqref{eq:27.2.15} has the desired property of
being a gauge-invariant chiral spinor superfield. First, note that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.15} is a superfield, because it is formed by acting on
the superfield \(V\) with superderivatives. Also, it follows from the
anticommutation of the \(\SuperD\)s that the product of any three or more
\(\SuperD_L\)s or three or more \(\SuperD_R\)s vanishes, so that
\begin{equation}
  (\SuperD^T\epsilon\SuperD)\SuperD
  =(\SuperD_L^T\epsilon\SuperD_L)\SuperD_R
  +(\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R)\SuperD_L.
  \tag{27.2.16}\label{eq:27.2.16}
\end{equation}
Because
\(\SuperD_L(\SuperD_L^T\epsilon\SuperD_L)
=\SuperD_R(\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R)=0\), the superfield
\eqref{eq:27.2.15} is chiral, with
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  W_{L\alpha}(x,\theta)
  &=\frac{i}{4}(\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R)
    \SuperD_{L\alpha}V(x,\theta),
  \\
  W_{R\alpha}(x,\theta)
  &=\frac{i}{4}(\SuperD_L^T\epsilon\SuperD_L)
    \SuperD_{R\alpha}V(x,\theta).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.17}\label{eq:27.2.17}
\end{equation}
Finally, we can show that \eqref{eq:27.2.15} is invariant under the
generalized gauge transformation \eqref{eq:27.1.13}, which for a single
Abelian gauge superfield is simply
\begin{equation}
  V(x,\theta)\longrightarrow
  V(x,\theta)+\frac{i}{2}
  \left[\Omega(x,\theta)-\Omega^*(x,\theta)\right],
  \tag{27.2.18}\label{eq:27.2.18}
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega(x,\theta)\) is an arbitrary left-chiral superfield. Since
\(\SuperD_L\Omega^*=0\), the change in \(W_{L\alpha}\) is proportional to
\((\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R)\SuperD_{L\alpha}\Omega\). But
\(\SuperD_R\Omega=0\) and
\[
  \left[(\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R),\SuperD_{L\alpha}\right]
  =-2\left[(1+\gamma_5)\sl{\partial}\SuperD_R\right]_\alpha,
\]
so the change in \(W_{L\alpha}\) vanishes. A similar argument shows that
\(W_{R\alpha}\) is also gauge-invariant. (The work of checking
Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.15} is greatly reduced by using this gauge-invariance
property to put \(V(x,\theta)\) in Wess--Zumino gauge.)

The chiral superfields \eqref{eq:27.2.11} and \eqref{eq:27.2.12} are
evidently not of the most general form for left- and right-chiral
superfields. To put the constraints satisfied by these superfields in a
manifestly supersymmetric form, we note by using the anticommutation
relation \eqref{eq:26.2.30} that
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\SuperD_{L\alpha}
    (\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R)\SuperD_{L\beta}
  ={}&-2\SuperD_{R\alpha}\SuperD_{L\beta}
    \bigl(\epsilon(1+\gamma_5)\sl{\partial}\bigr)_{\beta\alpha}
  \\
  &+(\SuperD_R^T\epsilon\SuperD_R)
    (\SuperD_L^T\epsilon\SuperD_L)
  \\
  ={}&\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\SuperD_{R\alpha}
    (\SuperD_L^T\epsilon\SuperD_L)\SuperD_{R\beta}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{27.2.19}\label{eq:27.2.19}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.17} it follows then that \(W_L\) and \(W_R\) are
related by the constraint
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\SuperD_{L\alpha}W_{L\beta}
  =\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\SuperD_{R\alpha}W_{R\beta}.
  \tag{27.2.20}\label{eq:27.2.20}
\end{equation}
It is straightforward to show that the most general chiral spinor
superfields satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.20} are of the form
\eqref{eq:27.2.11} and \eqref{eq:27.2.12}, with \(f_{\mu\nu}\) constrained
to satisfy the `Bianchi' identities
\(\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho f_{\mu\nu}=0\).
