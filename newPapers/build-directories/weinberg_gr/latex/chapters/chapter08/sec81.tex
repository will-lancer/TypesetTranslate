\subsection{The General Static Isotropic Metric}\label{sec:8.1}

For the moment we put aside Einstein's equations, and consider what is the
most general metric tensor that can represent a static isotropic
gravitational field. By ``static and isotropic'' we mean that it must be
possible to find a set of ``quasi-Minkowskian'' coordinates
\(x^0=t,x^1,x^2,x^3\), such that the invariant interval
\(ds^2=g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu\) does not depend on \(t\), and depends on
\(\mathbf{x}\) and \(d\mathbf{x}\) only through the rotational invariants
\(d\mathbf{x}^2\), \(\mathbf{x}\cdot d\mathbf{x}\), and
\(\mathbf{x}^2\). The most general interval is then
\begin{equation}
  ds^2
  =
  -F(r)\,dt^2
  +2E(r)\,dt\,\mathbf{x}\cdot d\mathbf{x}
  +D(r)(\mathbf{x}\cdot d\mathbf{x})^2
  +C(r)\,d\mathbf{x}^2,
  \tag{8.1.1}\label{eq:8.1.1}
\end{equation}
where \(F,E,D\), and \(C\) are unknown functions of
\[
  r\equiv(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x})^{1/2}.
\]
(Scalar products of three-vectors are throughout this chapter defined as
usual, e.g.,
\(\mathbf{x}\cdot d\mathbf{x}
=x^1dx^1+x^2dx^2+x^3dx^3\), etc.) A deeper derivation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.1} will be given in Chapter~13; for the present we can
regard Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.1} as a definition of what we mean by a static
isotropic metric, or alternatively as an ansatz that allows us to find some
solutions of the field equations.

It is convenient to replace \(\mathbf{x}\) with spherical polar coordinates
\(r,\theta,\varphi\), defined as usual by
\[
  x^1=r\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\qquad
  x^2=r\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\qquad
  x^3=r\cos\theta.
\]
The interval \eqref{eq:8.1.1} then becomes
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  ds^2={}&-F(r)\,dt^2+2rE(r)\,dt\,dr+r^2D(r)\,dr^2\\
  &+C(r)\bigl(dr^2+r^2d\theta^2
  +r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr).
  \end{split}
  \tag{8.1.2}\label{eq:8.1.2}
\end{equation}
We are free to reset our clocks by defining a new time coordinate
\[
  t'=t+\Phi(r),
\]
with \(\Phi\) an arbitrary function of \(r\). This allows us to eliminate the
off-diagonal element \(g_{tr}\) by setting
\[
  \frac{d\Phi}{dr}=-\frac{rE(r)}{F(r)}.
\]
The interval \eqref{eq:8.1.2} then becomes
\begin{equation}
  ds^2
  =
  -F(r)\,dt'^2+G(r)\,dr^2
  +C(r)\bigl(dr^2+r^2d\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr),
  \tag{8.1.3}\label{eq:8.1.3}
\end{equation}
where
\[
  G(r)\equiv r^2\left(D(r)+\frac{E^2(r)}{F(r)}\right).
\]
We are also free to redefine the radius \(r\), and thereby impose one
further relation on the functions \(F,G\), and \(C\). For instance, suppose
that we define
\[
  r'^2\equiv C(r)r^2.
\]
Then the interval \eqref{eq:8.1.3} takes what is called the
\emph{standard form}
\begin{equation}
  ds^2
  =
  -B(r')\,dt'^2+A(r')\,dr'^2
  +r'^2\bigl(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr),
  \tag{8.1.4}\label{eq:8.1.4}
\end{equation}
where
\[
  B(r')\equiv F(r),
  \qquad
  A(r')
  \equiv
  \left(1+\frac{G(r)}{C(r)}\right)
  \left(1+\frac{r}{2C(r)}\frac{dC(r)}{dr}\right)^{-2}.
\]
Alternatively, we could define
\[
  r''
  =
  \exp\left[
    \int
    \left(1+\frac{G(r)}{C(r)}\right)^{1/2}\frac{dr}{r}
  \right],
\]
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.3} would then appear in what is called the
\emph{isotropic form},
\begin{equation}
  ds^2
  =
  -H(r'')\,dt'^2
  +J(r'')\bigl(dr''^2+r''^2d\theta^2
  +r''^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr),
  \tag{8.1.5}\label{eq:8.1.5}
\end{equation}
where
\[
  H(r'')\equiv F(r),
  \qquad
  J(r'')\equiv\frac{C(r)r^2}{r''^2}.
\]

We shall do most of our work with a metric of the ``standard'' form:
\begin{equation}
  ds^2
  =
  -B(r)\,dt^2+A(r)\,dr^2
  +r^2\bigl(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr).
  \tag{8.1.6}\label{eq:8.1.6}
\end{equation}
(We drop primes on \(r\) and \(t\) from now on.) The metric tensor has the
nonvanishing components
\begin{equation}
  g_{rr}=A(r),\qquad
  g_{\theta\theta}=r^2,\qquad
  g_{\varphi\varphi}=r^2\sin^2\theta,\qquad
  g_{tt}=-B(r),
  \tag{8.1.7}\label{eq:8.1.7}
\end{equation}
with functions \(A(r)\) and \(B(r)\) that are to be determined by solving
the field equations. Since \(g_{\mu\nu}\) is diagonal, it is easy to write
down all the nonvanishing components of its inverse:
\begin{equation}
  g^{rr}=A^{-1}(r),\qquad
  g^{\theta\theta}=r^{-2},\qquad
  g^{\varphi\varphi}=r^{-2}(\sin\theta)^{-2},\qquad
  g^{tt}=-B^{-1}(r).
  \tag{8.1.8}\label{eq:8.1.8}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, the determinant of the metric tensor is
\begin{equation}
  g\equiv\det(g_{\mu\nu})
  =-r^4A(r)B(r)\sin^2\theta,
  \tag{8.1.9}\label{eq:8.1.9}
\end{equation}
% VERIFY SOURCE: the source calls this the invariant volume element but
% displays the factor per unit coordinate time, omitting \(dt\).
so the invariant volume element is
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{-g}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi
  =
  r^2\sqrt{A(r)B(r)}\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi.
  \tag{8.1.10}\label{eq:8.1.10}
\end{equation}

The affine connection can be computed from the usual formula
\[
  \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}
  =
  \frac12g^{\lambda\rho}
  \left(
    \partial_\nu g_{\rho\mu}
    +\partial_\mu g_{\rho\nu}
    -\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}
  \right).
\]
Its only nonvanishing components are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \Gamma^r{}_{rr}=\frac{A'}{2A},
  \qquad
  \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-\frac{r}{A},
  \qquad
  \Gamma^r{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-\frac{r\sin^2\theta}{A},
  \qquad
  \Gamma^r{}_{tt}=\frac{B'}{2A},
  \\
  \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}
  =\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=\frac1r,
  \qquad
  \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,
  \\
  \Gamma^\varphi{}_{r\varphi}
  =\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi r}=\frac1r,
  \qquad
  \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}
  =\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi\theta}=\cot\theta,
  \\
  \Gamma^t{}_{rt}
  =\Gamma^t{}_{tr}=\frac{B'}{2B}.
  \end{gathered}
  \tag{8.1.11}\label{eq:8.1.11}
\end{equation}

We also need the Ricci tensor. In the curvature convention of this edition it
is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mu\nu}
  =
  \partial_\rho\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\mu}
  -\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\rho\mu}
  +\Gamma^\rho{}_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}
  -\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}.
  \tag{8.1.12}\label{eq:8.1.12}
\end{equation}
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: Weinberg's (8.1.12)--(8.1.13) use the opposite
% curvature sign. Every Ricci component below is therefore the negative of
% the printed component, as required by NOTATION.md.
(Note that despite its appearance, the second term is symmetric in
\(\mu\) and \(\nu\), because Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.7.6} gives
\(\Gamma^\rho{}_{\rho\mu}\) equal to
\(\tfrac12\partial_\mu\ln(-g)\).) Inserting in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.12}
the components of the affine connection given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.11},
we find
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  R_{rr}
  &=
  -\frac{B''}{2B}
  +\frac14\frac{B'}{B}
    \left(\frac{A'}{A}+\frac{B'}{B}\right)
  +\frac1r\frac{A'}{A},
  \\
  R_{\theta\theta}
  &=
  1+\frac{r}{2A}
  \left(\frac{A'}{A}-\frac{B'}{B}\right)-\frac1A,
  \\
  R_{\varphi\varphi}
  &=\sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta},
  \\
  R_{tt}
  &=
  \frac{B''}{2A}
  -\frac14\frac{B'}{A}
    \left(\frac{A'}{A}+\frac{B'}{B}\right)
  +\frac1r\frac{B'}{A},
  \\
  R_{\mu\nu}&=0\qquad(\mu\ne\nu).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{8.1.13}\label{eq:8.1.13}
\end{equation}
(A prime now means differentiation with respect to \(r\).) The results that
\(R_{r\theta}\), \(R_{r\varphi}\), \(R_{\theta\varphi}\),
\(R_{t\varphi}\), and \(R_{t\theta}\) vanish, and that
\(R_{\varphi\varphi}=\sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta}\), are merely
consequences of the rotational invariance of the metric, whereas the result
that \(R_{tr}\) vanishes is because we have set our clocks so that the metric
is invariant under the time-reversal transformation \(t\to-t\).

Neither the standard nor the isotropic coordinates are harmonic, but we can
easily use the results \eqref{eq:8.1.7} and \eqref{eq:8.1.11} for the
metric and affine connection in standard coordinates to construct harmonic
coordinates \(X_1,X_2,X_3,t\). We set
\begin{equation}
  X_1=R(r)\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\qquad
  X_2=R(r)\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\qquad
  X_3=R(r)\cos\theta.
  \tag{8.1.14}\label{eq:8.1.14}
\end{equation}
A straightforward calculation gives then
\[
  \begin{split}
  \Box X_i
  &=
  g^{\mu\nu}
  \left(
    \partial_\mu\partial_\nu X_i
    -\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda X_i
  \right)
  \\
  &=
  \frac{X_i}{AR}
  \left[
    \left(\frac{B'}{2B}+\frac2r-\frac{A'}{2A}\right)R'
    +R''-\frac{2A}{r^2}R
  \right].
  \end{split}
\]
Also, the standard time coordinate \(t\) satisfies
\[
  \Box t=0.
\]
Thus the coordinates \(X_1,X_2,X_3,t\) are harmonic if \(R(r)\) satisfies
the differential equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dr}
  \left(r^2B^{1/2}A^{-1/2}\frac{dR}{dr}\right)
  -2A^{1/2}B^{1/2}R=0.
  \tag{8.1.15}\label{eq:8.1.15}
\end{equation}
In these harmonic coordinates the interval \eqref{eq:8.1.6} becomes
\begin{equation}
  ds^2
  =
  -B\,dt^2+\frac{r^2}{R^2}\,d\mathbf{X}^2
  +\left(\frac{A}{R^2R'^2}-\frac{r^2}{R^4}\right)
  (\mathbf{X}\cdot d\mathbf{X})^2.
  \tag{8.1.16}\label{eq:8.1.16}
\end{equation}
