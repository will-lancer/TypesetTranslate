\subsection{Maximally Symmetric Spaces: Construction}\label{sec:13.3}

Maximally symmetric spaces are essentially unique, so we can learn all about
them by constructing examples with arbitrary curvature \(K\) in any way we
like.

This is one rather obvious way to carry out this construction. (See
Figure~\ref{fig:13.1}.) Consider a flat \((N+1)\)-dimensional space, with
metric given by
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  \equiv
  g_{AB}\,\dd x^A\dd x^B
  =
  C_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x^\mu\dd x^\nu+K^{-1}\dd z^2,
  \tag{13.3.1}\label{eq:13.3.1}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\mu\nu}\) is a constant \(N\times N\) matrix and \(K\) is some
constant. We can embed a non-Euclidean \(N\)-dimensional space in this larger
space by restricting the variables \(x^\mu\) and \(z\) to the surface of a
sphere (or pseudosphere)
\begin{equation}
  K C_{\mu\nu}x^\mu x^\nu+z^2=1.
  \tag{13.3.2}\label{eq:13.3.2}
\end{equation}

\input{figures/chapter13/fig13-01.tex}

On this surface \(\dd z^2\) is given by
\[
  \dd z^2
  =
  \frac{K^2\left(C_{\mu\nu}x^\mu\dd x^\nu\right)^2}
       {1-KC_{\mu\nu}x^\mu x^\nu},
\]
and therefore \eqref{eq:13.3.1} gives
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  C_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x^\mu\dd x^\nu
  +
  \frac{K\left(C_{\mu\nu}x^\mu\dd x^\nu\right)^2}
       {1-KC_{\mu\nu}x^\mu x^\nu}.
  \tag{13.3.3}\label{eq:13.3.3}
\end{equation}
The metric is then
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mu\nu}(x)
  =
  C_{\mu\nu}
  +
  \frac{K}
       {1-KC_{\rho\sigma}x^\rho x^\sigma}
  C_{\mu\lambda}x^\lambda C_{\nu\kappa}x^\kappa.
  \tag{13.3.4}\label{eq:13.3.4}
\end{equation}
A flat space appears here as the special case \(K=0\).

This construction makes it obvious that \eqref{eq:13.3.4} admits an
\(N(N+1)/2\)-parameter group of isometries, for both the
\((N+1)\)-dimensional line element \eqref{eq:13.3.1} and the ``embedding''
condition \eqref{eq:13.3.2} are manifestly invariant under rigid
``rotations'' of the \((N+1)\)-dimensional space, that is, under the
transformations
\begin{equation}
  x^\mu\longrightarrow x'^\mu
  =
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu+\mathcal R^\mu{}_{z}z,
  \tag{13.3.5}\label{eq:13.3.5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  z\longrightarrow z'
  =
  \mathcal R^{z}{}_\mu x^\mu+\mathcal R^{z}{}_{z}z,
  \tag{13.3.6}\label{eq:13.3.6}
\end{equation}
where the \(\mathcal R^A{}_B\) are constants, with
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_\rho\mathcal R^\nu{}_\sigma
  +K^{-1}\mathcal R^{z}{}_\rho\mathcal R^{z}{}_\sigma
  =
  C_{\rho\sigma},
  \tag{13.3.7}\label{eq:13.3.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_\rho\mathcal R^\nu{}_{z}
  +K^{-1}\mathcal R^{z}{}_\rho\mathcal R^{z}{}_{z}
  =
  0,
  \tag{13.3.8}\label{eq:13.3.8}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_{z}\mathcal R^\nu{}_{z}
  +K^{-1}\left(\mathcal R^{z}{}_{z}\right)^2
  =
  K^{-1}.
  \tag{13.3.9}\label{eq:13.3.9}
\end{equation}
It is convenient to distinguish two classes of simple transformations
satisfying \eqref{eq:13.3.7}--\eqref{eq:13.3.9}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{(A)}
  \qquad
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_\nu=\mathscr R^\mu{}_\nu,
  \qquad
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_{z}=\mathcal R^{z}{}_\mu=0,
  \qquad
  \mathcal R^{z}{}_{z}=1,
  \tag{13.3.10}\label{eq:13.3.10}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathscr R^\mu{}_\nu\) is any \(N\times N\) matrix with
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}
  \mathscr R^\mu{}_\rho\mathscr R^\nu{}_\sigma
  =
  C_{\rho\sigma}.
  \tag{13.3.11}\label{eq:13.3.11}
\end{equation}
These are just rigid ``rotations'' about the origin:
\begin{equation}
  x'^\mu=\mathscr R^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu.
  \tag{13.3.12}\label{eq:13.3.12}
\end{equation}
The second class is
\begin{equation}
  \text{(B)}
  \qquad
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_{z}=a^\mu,
  \qquad
  \mathcal R^{z}{}_\mu=-K C_{\mu\nu}a^\nu,
  \qquad
  \mathcal R^{z}{}_{z}
  =
  \left(1-KC_{\rho\sigma}a^\rho a^\sigma\right)^{1/2},
  \tag{13.3.13}\label{eq:13.3.13}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal R^\mu{}_\nu
  =
  \delta^\mu_\nu-bK C_{\nu\rho}a^\rho a^\mu,
  \tag{13.3.14}\label{eq:13.3.14}
\end{equation}
where \(a^\mu\) is arbitrary except that
\(\mathcal R^{z}{}_{z}\) must be real, that is,
\begin{equation}
  K C_{\rho\sigma}a^\rho a^\sigma\leq1,
  \tag{13.3.15}\label{eq:13.3.15}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  b
  \equiv
  \frac{
    1-\left(1-KC_{\rho\sigma}a^\rho a^\sigma\right)^{1/2}
  }{
    KC_{\rho\sigma}a^\rho a^\sigma
  }.
  \tag{13.3.16}\label{eq:13.3.16}
\end{equation}
These are ``quasitranslations,'' with
\begin{equation}
  x'^\mu
  =
  x^\mu
  +
  a^\mu
  \left[
    \left(1-KC_{\rho\sigma}x^\rho x^\sigma\right)^{1/2}
    -bK C_{\rho\sigma}x^\rho a^\sigma
  \right].
  \tag{13.3.17}\label{eq:13.3.17}
\end{equation}
In particular, these transformations take the origin \(x^\mu=0\) into
\(a^\mu\).

The existence of isometries \eqref{eq:13.3.17} that take the origin into any
point (at least within a finite region) means that this space is
\emph{homogeneous}: any point is geometrically like any other point. (Our
coordinate system hides this property, just as a polar projection map of the
earth hides the fact that the curvature of the earth is about the same in
Massachusetts as at the North Pole.) Also, the existence of isometries
\eqref{eq:13.3.10} that include all rigid ``rotations'' about the origin means
that this space is \emph{isotropic} about the origin. Since the metric is
homogeneous, and isotropic about the origin, it is isotropic about every
point, and maximally symmetric.

We can construct the Killing vectors for this metric by letting the finite
transformations \eqref{eq:13.3.5}, \eqref{eq:13.3.6} approach the unit
transformation. First, consider the transformations (A), and let
\[
  \mathscr R^\mu{}_\nu
  =
  \delta^\mu_\nu+\epsilon\Omega^\mu{}_\nu,
  \qquad
  \lvert\epsilon\rvert\ll1,
\]
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\sigma}\Omega^\mu{}_\rho
  +C_{\rho\mu}\Omega^\mu{}_\sigma
  =
  0.
  \tag{13.3.18}\label{eq:13.3.18}
\end{equation}
Comparing with \eqref{eq:13.1.3}, the corresponding Killing vectors are
\begin{equation}
  \xi^\mu_\alpha(x)=\Omega^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu.
  \tag{13.3.19}\label{eq:13.3.19}
\end{equation}
Next, consider the transformations (B), and let
\[
  a^\mu=\epsilon\alpha^\mu,
  \qquad
  \lvert\epsilon\rvert\ll1.
\]
Comparing with \eqref{eq:13.1.3}, the corresponding Killing vectors are
\begin{equation}
  \xi^\mu_\alpha(x)
  =
  \alpha^\mu
  \left(1-KC_{\nu\rho}x^\nu x^\rho\right)^{1/2}.
  \tag{13.3.20}\label{eq:13.3.20}
\end{equation}
The reader may check that \eqref{eq:13.3.19} and \eqref{eq:13.3.20} do
satisfy the Killing conditions \eqref{eq:13.1.5}. There are \(N(N-1)/2\)
independent parameters \(\Omega^\mu{}_\nu\) [that is, \(N^2\) elements
\(\Omega^\mu{}_\nu\), subject to the \(N(N+1)/2\) conditions
\eqref{eq:13.3.18}] and \(N\) parameters \(\alpha^\mu\), so this metric
admits \(N(N+1)/2\) independent Killing vectors, verifying maximal symmetry.

The geodesics of this metric take a remarkably simple form. From
\eqref{eq:13.3.4} we can readily calculate that the affine connection is
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}
  =
  Kx^\mu g_{\nu\lambda},
  \tag{13.3.21}\label{eq:13.3.21}
\end{equation}
so the differential equation for a geodesic is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2x^\mu}{\dd\tau^2}+Kx^\mu=0.
  \tag{13.3.22}\label{eq:13.3.22}
\end{equation}
The solutions are thus linear combinations of
\(\sin(\tau\sqrt K)\) and \(\cos(\tau\sqrt K)\) for \(K>0\), or of
\(\sinh(\tau\sqrt{-K})\) and \(\cosh(\tau\sqrt{-K})\) for \(K<0\).

We can uncover the inner properties of this space by calculating the curvature
tensor; a straightforward computation using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.6.2} and
\eqref{eq:13.3.21} gives the Riemann--Christoffel tensor for the metric
\eqref{eq:13.3.4} as
% VERIFY SOURCE: printed p. 389 cites (13.2.21); the intended equation is
% (13.3.21), the affine connection immediately above.
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  R_{\kappa\nu\rho\sigma}
  ={}&
  -K\left(
    C_{\kappa\sigma}C_{\nu\rho}
    -C_{\kappa\rho}C_{\nu\sigma}
  \right)
  \\
  &-\frac{K^2}{1-KC_{\mu\lambda}x^\mu x^\lambda}
  \left(
    C_{\kappa\sigma}x_\nu x_\rho
    -C_{\kappa\rho}x_\nu x_\sigma
    +C_{\nu\rho}x_\kappa x_\sigma
    -C_{\nu\sigma}x_\rho x_\kappa
  \right),
  \end{aligned}
\]
where \(x_\nu\equiv C_{\nu\mu}x^\mu\), or
\[
  R_{\kappa\nu\rho\sigma}
  =
  K\left(
    g_{\sigma\nu}g_{\kappa\rho}
    -g_{\rho\nu}g_{\kappa\sigma}
  \right),
\]
in agreement with Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.2.9}. Hence the constant \(K\) introduced
in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:13.3.1} and \eqref{eq:13.3.2} is the same as the curvature
constant introduced in the last section.

Since \(K\) is an invariant parameter, we cannot by a coordinate
transformation convert the metric \eqref{eq:13.3.4} into a similar metric
with a different \(K\). In contrast, Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.3.3} makes it obvious
that by a linear transformation
\[
  x^\mu=A^\mu{}_\nu x'^\nu
\]
we can convert the metric \eqref{eq:13.3.4} into a similar metric with the
same \(K\) and with \(C_{\mu\nu}\) changed into
\[
  C'_{\mu\nu}
  =
  A^\rho{}_\mu A^\sigma{}_\nu C_{\rho\sigma}.
\]
Our discussion in Section~\ref{sec:3.6} shows that in this way
\(C_{\mu\nu}\) can be changed into any real symmetric matrix we like, as long
as we do not change the numbers of its positive and negative eigenvalues.
Also, the numbers of eigenvalues of each sign of the matrix \(C_{\mu\nu}\)
are the same as for the matrix \(g_{\mu\nu}\) at the point \(x=0\), and hence
the same everywhere, since all points are equivalent.

An \(N\)-dimensional metric that allows the introduction of locally Euclidean
(as opposed, say, to Minkowskian) coordinate systems will have all its
eigenvalues positive, so for \(K\neq0\) we can take \(C_{\mu\nu}\) as
\(\lvert K\rvert^{-1}\) times the unit matrix, in which case
\eqref{eq:13.3.3} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  K^{-1}
  \left[
    \dd\mathbf{x}^2
    +
    \frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\dd\mathbf{x})^2}{1-\mathbf{x}^2}
  \right]
  \qquad\text{for \(K>0\),}
  \tag{13.3.23}\label{eq:13.3.23}
\end{equation}
or
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  \lvert K\rvert^{-1}
  \left[
    \dd\mathbf{x}^2
    -\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\dd\mathbf{x})^2}{1+\mathbf{x}^2}
  \right]
  \qquad\text{for \(K<0\).}
  \tag{13.3.24}\label{eq:13.3.24}
\end{equation}
For \(K=0\), we take \(C_{\mu\nu}\) as just the unit matrix, and
\eqref{eq:13.3.3} gives
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=\dd\mathbf{x}^2
  \qquad\text{for \(K=0\).}
  \tag{13.3.25}\label{eq:13.3.25}
\end{equation}
(We are using an obvious \(N\)-dimensional vector notation. Also, we have
replaced the source's proper-time notation with a proper length
\(\dd s^2\), because for the moment we are doing geometry rather than
physics.) Let us explore the \emph{global} properties of these spaces.

For \(K>0\), our most convenient approach is to go back to the interpretation
of \eqref{eq:13.3.23} as the metric of the curved space embedded by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.3.2} in the flat space \eqref{eq:13.3.1}; that is,
\eqref{eq:13.3.23} describes the surface
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}^2+z^2=1
  \tag{13.3.26}\label{eq:13.3.26}
\end{equation}
in the flat space with
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  K^{-1}\left(\dd\mathbf{x}^2+\dd z^2\right).
  \tag{13.3.27}\label{eq:13.3.27}
\end{equation}
Obviously this metric simply describes the surface of a sphere of radius
\(K^{-1/2}\) in an \((N+1)\)-dimensional Euclidean space. (To make the
coordinates \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(z\) truly Euclidean, we should define
\(\mathbf{x}'=K^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}\) and \(z'=K^{-1/2}z\), in which case
\eqref{eq:13.3.26} reads
\(\mathbf{x}'^2+z'^2=K^{-1}\).) Indeed, in two dimensions we can introduce
angular coordinates \(\theta,\phi\) by
\[
  x_1=\sin\theta\cos\phi,
  \qquad
  x_2=\sin\theta\sin\phi,
\]
and \eqref{eq:13.3.27} then becomes the familiar line element on a sphere
of radius \(K^{-1/2}\):
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  K^{-1}
  \left(
    \dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2
  \right).
  \tag{13.3.28}\label{eq:13.3.28}
\end{equation}
In general, the range of the variables \(\mathbf{x}\) is
\[
  \mathbf{x}^2\leq1.
\]
However, each \(\mathbf{x}\) actually corresponds to \emph{two} points,
corresponding to the two roots of Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.3.26} for \(z\).
(For instance, in two dimensions the components of \(\mathbf{x}\) are the
coordinates of points on a sphere projected on a tangent plane; in a polar
projection map of the earth, Boston will appear at the same point as San
Carlos de Bariloche, Argentina.) The volume of the \(N\)-dimensional space
described by \eqref{eq:13.3.23} is therefore
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  V_N
  &=
  2\int_{\mathbf{x}^2\leq1}
  \sqrt{g}\,\dd x_1\cdots\dd x_N
  \\
  &=
  2K^{-N/2}
  \int_{\mathbf{x}^2\leq1}
  \frac{\dd x_1\cdots\dd x_N}
       {(1-\mathbf{x}^2)^{1/2}}.
  \end{aligned}
\]
A straightforward calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  V_N
  =
  \frac{2\pi^{(N+1)/2}}
       {\Gamma\!\left((N+1)/2\right)}
  K^{-N/2}.
  \tag{13.3.29}\label{eq:13.3.29}
\end{equation}
For instance, \(V_1=2\pi K^{-1/2}\), which is just the perimeter of a
circle of radius \(K^{-1/2}\), and \(V_2=4\pi K^{-1}\), which is just the
area of a sphere with radius \(K^{-1/2}\). A three-dimensional space of
constant positive curvature has the volume
\[
  V_3=2\pi^2K^{-3/2}.
\]
We can also calculate the circumference of such spaces, using for the
geodesics the solutions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.3.22}, which now reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2\mathbf{x}}{\dd s^2}+K\mathbf{x}=0.
  \tag{13.3.30}\label{eq:13.3.30}
\end{equation}
The solutions that pass through the point \(\mathbf{x}=0\) are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}
  =
  \mathbf{e}\sin\!\left(sK^{1/2}\right),
  \tag{13.3.31}\label{eq:13.3.31}
\end{equation}
where, in order to satisfy \eqref{eq:13.3.23},
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{e}=1.
  \tag{13.3.32}\label{eq:13.3.32}
\end{equation}
As we go out along a geodesic from the ``North pole''
\(\mathbf{x}=0\), we reach the ``equator'' \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}\) at
\(s=\pi K^{-1/2}/2\), we reach the ``South pole'' \(\mathbf{x}=0\) at
\(s=\pi K^{-1/2}\), we reach the opposite point
\(\mathbf{x}=-\mathbf{e}\) of the ``equator'' at
\(s=3\pi K^{-1/2}/2\), and we return to our starting point at
\(s=2\pi K^{-1/2}\). Thus the distance from any point around the whole
space and back to itself along a geodesic is
\begin{equation}
  L=2\pi K^{-1/2}
  \tag{13.3.33}\label{eq:13.3.33}
\end{equation}
for spaces of constant positive curvature and arbitrary dimensionality.
This calculation shows very clearly that the space described by
\eqref{eq:13.3.23} is finite, but it is not bounded: when we come to the
apparent singularity at \(\mathbf{x}^2=1\), we continue right through, but
with \(z\) given by the root of Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.3.26} of opposite sign.

For \(K<0\) the metric \eqref{eq:13.3.24} does not even have an apparent
singularity, and there is nothing to restrict the coordinates
\(\mathbf{x}\) to any finite range. This can be seen even more definitely
by calculating the geodesics, which are now given by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:13.3.30} and \eqref{eq:13.3.24} as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}
  =
  \mathbf{e}\sinh\!\left(s(-K)^{1/2}\right),
  \tag{13.3.34}\label{eq:13.3.34}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{e}=1.
  \tag{13.3.35}\label{eq:13.3.35}
\end{equation}
We can obviously go out along this geodesic an unlimited distance from the
origin. For \(N=2\), this space is just that discovered by Gauss, Bolyai,
and Lobachevski. [See Section~\ref{sec:1.1}. In order to put the metric in
the form \eqref{eq:1.1.9} of Klein's model, it is necessary to introduce a
new set of coordinates \(\mathbf{x}'\), defined by
\(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}'(1-\mathbf{x}'^2)^{-1/2}\).] We see from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:13.3.1} and \eqref{eq:13.3.2} that this geometry describes
the surface
\begin{equation}
  -\mathbf{x}^2+z^2=1
  \tag{13.3.36}\label{eq:13.3.36}
\end{equation}
in a flat space, with
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  \lvert K\rvert^{-1}
  \left(\dd\mathbf{x}^2-\dd z^2\right).
  \tag{13.3.37}\label{eq:13.3.37}
\end{equation}
The minus sign in \eqref{eq:13.3.37} means that this flat space is not
Euclidean. It is therefore understandable that the Gauss--Bolyai--Lobachevski
geometry could not be discovered until mathematicians learned to think of
curved surfaces, not as subspaces of an ordinary Euclidean space, but as
spaces characterized by their own inner metric relations.

Finally, let us return to space-time, and consider the structure of a
four-dimensional maximally symmetric metric with three positive and one
negative eigenvalue. In this case, in the target \((-+++)\) convention, we
can set
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu},
  \tag{13.3.38}\label{eq:13.3.38}
\end{equation}
and the metric is
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  -\dd t^2+\dd\mathbf{x}^2
  +
  \frac{
    K\left(\mathbf{x}\cdot\dd\mathbf{x}-t\,\dd t\right)^2
  }{
    1-K\left(\mathbf{x}^2-t^2\right)
  }.
  \tag{13.3.39}\label{eq:13.3.39}
\end{equation}
For \(K>0\), we can introduce coordinates in which the metric appears
\emph{spatially} flat, by setting
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  t
  &=
  \frac1{\sqrt K}
  \left[
    \frac{K\mathbf{x}'^2}{2}\cosh\!\left(K^{1/2}t'\right)
    +
    \left(1+\frac{K\mathbf{x}'^2}{2}\right)
    \sinh\!\left(K^{1/2}t'\right)
  \right],
  \\
  \mathbf{x}
  &=
  \mathbf{x}'\exp\!\left(K^{1/2}t'\right).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{13.3.40}\label{eq:13.3.40}
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{eq:13.3.39} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  -\dd t'^2
  +\exp\!\left(2K^{1/2}t'\right)\dd\mathbf{x}'^2.
  \tag{13.3.41}\label{eq:13.3.41}
\end{equation}
We can also introduce coordinates in which the metric appears
time-independent, by setting
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  t''
  &=
  t'
  -
  \frac1{2K^{1/2}}
  \ln\!\left[
    1-K\mathbf{x}'^2\exp\!\left(2K^{1/2}t'\right)
  \right],
  \\
  \mathbf{x}''
  &=
  \mathbf{x}'\exp\!\left(K^{1/2}t'\right).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{13.3.42}\label{eq:13.3.42}
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{eq:13.3.41} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  -\left(1-K\mathbf{x}''^2\right)\dd t''^2
  +\dd\mathbf{x}''^2
  +
  \frac{
    K\left(\mathbf{x}''\cdot\dd\mathbf{x}''\right)^2
  }{
    1-K\mathbf{x}''^2
  }.
  \tag{13.3.43}\label{eq:13.3.43}
\end{equation}
This metric was first discussed in this form by de Sitter;%
\hyperref[ch13-ref-2]{\textsuperscript{2}} it will provide the basis for our
treatment of the steady-state cosmology in Chapter~14.

Once again, it should be stressed that the maximally symmetric metric
\eqref{eq:13.3.4}, although derived by an apparently arbitrary procedure,
actually represents the most general possible maximally symmetric metric,
because the uniqueness theorem of the last section tells us that any other
maximally symmetric metric can be converted into the form
\eqref{eq:13.3.4} by a suitable coordinate transformation.
