\subsection{Precession of Orbiting Gyroscopes}\label{sec:9.6}

We saw in Section~\ref{sec:5.1} that the spin \(S_\mu\) of a particle in
free fall precesses according to the equation of parallel transport:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{D S_\mu}{D\tau}=0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \frac{\dd S_\mu}{\dd\tau}
  =
  \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}S_\lambda
  \frac{\dd x^\nu}{\dd\tau}.
  \tag{9.6.1}\label{eq:9.6.1}
\end{equation}
A few years ago Pugh\hyperref[ch9-ref-2]{\textsuperscript{2}} and
Schiff\hyperref[ch9-ref-3]{\textsuperscript{3}} suggested that a gyroscope
might be placed in orbit around the earth, and the precession of its spin
vector be used to measure the fine details of the earth's gravitational
field. Schiff made use of a calculational method developed by
Papapetrou\hyperref[ch9-ref-4]{\textsuperscript{4}} and
Fock\hyperref[ch9-ref-5]{\textsuperscript{5}} in which the motion is first
calculated for an extended spinning body and then evaluated in the limit as
the body size goes to zero. We shall instead treat the gyroscope as a point
particle from the beginning, because for such particles the Principle of
Equivalence tells us that there is a locally inertial frame of reference in
which the spin does \emph{not} precess, and we can use
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.1} as the translation of this statement into a general
coordinate system.

The spin four-vector \(S_\mu\) is defined to remain orthogonal to the
velocity \(\dd x^\mu/\dd\tau\):
\[
  \frac{\dd x^\mu}{\dd\tau}S_\mu=0.
\]
[See Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.9}.] In other words,
\begin{equation}
  S_0=-v^iS_i.
  \tag{9.6.2}\label{eq:9.6.2}
\end{equation}
We set \(\mu=\ii\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.1}, multiply by
\(\dd\tau/\dd t\), and use Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.2} to eliminate \(S_0\);
this gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd S_i}{\dd t}
  =
  \Gamma^j{}_{i0}S_j
  {}-\Gamma^0{}_{i0}v^jS_j
  {}+\Gamma^j{}_{ik}v^kS_j
  {}-\Gamma^0{}_{ik}v^kv^jS_j.
  \tag{9.6.3}\label{eq:9.6.3}
\end{equation}
The post-Newtonian approximation will allow us to evaluate the coefficient
of \(S_j\) on the right-hand side to order \(\bar v^3/\bar r\):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd S_i}{\dd t}
  \simeq
  \left[
    \Gamma^{j(3)}{}_{i0}
    {}-\Gamma^{0(2)}{}_{i0}v^j
    {}+\Gamma^{j(2)}{}_{ik}v^k
  \right]S_j.
  \tag{9.6.4}\label{eq:9.6.4}
\end{equation}
(The last term drops out because there is no
\(\Gamma^{0(1)}{}_{ik}\).) The components of the affine connection are
provided by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:9.1.69}, \eqref{eq:9.1.70}, and
\eqref{eq:9.1.72}. We find
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \frac{\dd\mathbf S}{\dd t}\simeq{}&
  \frac12\mathbf S\times
    (\boldsymbol\nabla\times\boldsymbol\zeta)
  -\mathbf S\,\partial_t\phi
  -2(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf S)\boldsymbol\nabla\phi\\
  &-\mathbf S(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)
  +\mathbf v(\mathbf S\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi).
  \end{split}
  \tag{9.6.5}\label{eq:9.6.5}
\end{equation}

To solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.5}, we use the fact that parallel transport
preserves the value of \(S_\mu S^\mu\), so that [see
Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.10}]
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(g^{\mu\nu}S_\mu S_\nu\right)=0.
  \tag{9.6.6}\label{eq:9.6.6}
\end{equation}
The rate of change of \(\mathbf S\) is seen from Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.4} to
be of order \(S\bar v^3/\bar r\), so we need only keep those terms in
\(g^{\mu\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}\) whose rate of change, as seen by a particle
moving with velocity \(\bar v\), is comparable---that is, terms whose
gradient is of order \(\bar v^2/\bar r\). Hence \(g^{\mu\nu}\) may be
replaced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.6} by
\(\eta^{\mu\nu}+g^{\mu\nu(2)}\). Furthermore, \(S_0^2\) is already of
order \(\bar v^2\) relative to \(\mathbf S^2\), so we need not keep
\(g^{00(2)}\). Thus, to the order needed here,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.5} has the integral
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf S^2+2\phi\mathbf S^2-(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf S)^2
  =\text{constant}.
  \tag{9.6.7}\label{eq:9.6.7}
\end{equation}
This suggests that we should introduce a new spin vector
\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) by
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf S
  =
  (1-\phi)\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}
  +\frac12\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}),
  \tag{9.6.8}\label{eq:9.6.8}
\end{equation}
so that, to order \(\bar v^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}^{\,2}\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.7} reads
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\mathcal S}^{\,2}=\text{constant}.
  \tag{9.6.9}\label{eq:9.6.9}
\end{equation}
To the required order, we can invert Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.8} to read
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\mathcal S}
  =
  (1+\phi)\mathbf S
  -\frac12\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf S).
  \tag{9.6.10}\label{eq:9.6.10}
\end{equation}

The rate of change of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) is given to order
\((\bar v^3/\bar r)S\) by treating \(\mathbf S\) as constant everywhere
it appears with coefficients of order \(\bar v^2\), and setting
\(\dd\mathbf v/\dd t\simeq-\boldsymbol\nabla\phi\). We then find
\[
  \frac{\dd\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}{\dd t}
  =
  \frac{\dd\mathbf S}{\dd t}
  +\mathbf S\left(\partial_t\phi
    +\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi\right)
  +\frac12\boldsymbol\nabla\phi(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf S)
  +\frac12\mathbf v(\mathbf S\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi).
\]
Inserting Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.5}, we find, to order
\((\bar v^3/\bar r)\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\),
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}{\dd t}
  =
  \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal S},
  \tag{9.6.11}\label{eq:9.6.11}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\Omega
  =
  -\frac12\boldsymbol\nabla\times\boldsymbol\zeta
  {}-\frac32\mathbf v\times\boldsymbol\nabla\phi.
  \tag{9.6.12}\label{eq:9.6.12}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:9.6.11} shows that \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) just
precesses at a rate \(|\boldsymbol\Omega|\) around the direction of
\(\boldsymbol\Omega\), with no change in magnitude, thus verifying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.9}.

What does this have to do with the measurement of the precession of a
gyroscope in free fall? The answer, as always, is to be sought by reference
to the method actually used to measure the effect. In the present case the
spin direction of the gyroscope is monitored by measuring, \emph{in the
inertial frame moving with the gyroscope}, the angles \(\theta\) between
the spin \(\mathbf S_g\) of the gyroscope in this frame and the velocity
vectors \(\mathbf u_g\) of light rays from one or more distant stars:
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta
  =
  \frac{\mathbf S_g\cdot\mathbf u_g}
       {|\mathbf S_g|\,|\mathbf u_g|}.
  \tag{9.6.13}\label{eq:9.6.13}
\end{equation}
(This angle can be measured by focusing the star's image on an array of
photoelectric cells fixed to the gyroscope, in such a way that a change in
\(\theta\) causes the image to move over the cells, producing a change in
the photoelectric current.) In the inertial frame of the gyroscope, light
moves with unit velocity,
\[
  |\mathbf u_g|=1.
\]
The time component of \(S_{g\mu}\) vanishes
[see Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.2}],
\[
  S_{g0}=0,
\]
and the vector \(\mathbf S_g\) has constant magnitude
\[
  |\mathbf S_g|=(S_{g\mu}S_g^\mu)^{1/2}.
\]
Thus, the measured angles \(\theta\) can be expressed in the form
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta
  =
  \frac{S_\mu u^\mu}{(S_\mu S^\mu)^{1/2}}.
  \tag{9.6.14}\label{eq:9.6.14}
\end{equation}
This is now an invariant, so we are no longer restricted to the rather
inconvenient inertial coordinate system fixed to the gyroscope, but can use
for \(S_\mu\) and \(u^\mu\) the spin and light-velocity four-vectors in any
convenient coordinate system, such as the reference frame fixed to the
earth. In this frame we have for the velocity four-vector of the starlight
ray
\[
  u^i=u_\infty^i+\delta u^i,
  \qquad
  u^0=1+\delta u^0,
\]
where \(\mathbf u_\infty\) is a fixed unit vector giving the light velocity
far from the earth, and \(\delta u^\mu\) is a correction of order
\(M_\oplus G/r\sim\bar v^2\), arising from the effect of the earth's
gravitational field on the speed and direction of light rays. Also,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:9.6.10} and \eqref{eq:9.6.2} give, to order \(\bar v^2\),
\[
  S_i
  =
  \mathcal S_i-\phi\mathcal S_i
  +\frac12v_i(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal S})
  +\order(\bar v^4),
  \qquad
  S_0=-\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}+\order(\bar v^3).
\]
Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.14} gives the measured angle as
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta
  \simeq
  \widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}\cdot
  \left[
    \mathbf u_\infty-\mathbf v+\delta\mathbf u
    -\phi\mathbf u_\infty
    +\frac12\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u_\infty)
  \right],
  \tag{9.6.15}\label{eq:9.6.15}
\end{equation}
where
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}
\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}/|\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}|\).
The term \(-\mathbf v\) represents the \emph{aberration of starlight}, an
important effect known since the eighteenth century, which certainly must
be taken into account. Apart from this term, \(\cos\theta\) evidently
changes with time because \(\delta\mathbf u,\phi,\mathbf v\), and
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}\) change as the gyroscope revolves
about the earth, and also because \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) precesses
with angular frequency \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). Indeed, the fractional
changes in \(\cos\theta\) produced by the variations in each of
\(\delta\mathbf u,\phi,\mathbf v\), and
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}\) in the course of one revolution are,
aside from aberration, all of order \(\bar v^2\) [see
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:8.5.8} and \eqref{eq:9.6.12}]. Thus, in order to measure
the precession of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) in one revolution of the
gyroscope it would be necessary to measure \(\theta\) to an accuracy of
order \(10^{-10}\) radians, and even then we would have to disentangle the
effect of the bending \(\delta\mathbf u\) of starlight and the other terms
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.15} in order to interpret the result as a spin
precession. Fortunately, there is one property of the spin precession that
distinguishes it from all other effects: it is cumulative. After a large
number \(N\) of revolutions, the spin direction
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}\) will have changed by an amount of
order \(N\bar v^2\), while \(\delta\mathbf u,\phi\), and
\(\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u_\infty)\) will still be of order
\(\bar v^2\), so to a good approximation the change in \(\theta\) will,
after aberration is taken into account, be just given by the change in
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}\):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(\cos\theta)
  \simeq
  \mathbf u_\infty\cdot
  \Delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}.
  \tag{9.6.16}\label{eq:9.6.16}
\end{equation}
Our conclusion then is that the precession \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) of
\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) is a directly measurable effect, provided we
have the patience to wait for the gyroscope to complete many revolutions
about the earth.

Returning now to the problem of calculating \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), if we
regard the earth as a rotating sphere at rest, the fields
\(\boldsymbol\zeta\) and \(\phi\) can be taken from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:9.4.34} and \eqref{eq:9.4.22}:
\[
  \phi=-\frac{GM_\oplus}{r},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol\zeta
  =
  \frac{2G}{r^3}(\mathbf x\times\mathbf J_\oplus).
\]
The precession frequency \eqref{eq:9.6.12} is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\Omega
  =
  3G\frac{\mathbf x(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf J_\oplus)}{r^5}
  -G\frac{\mathbf J_\oplus}{r^3}
  {}+\frac{3GM_\oplus}{2r^3}(\mathbf x\times\mathbf v).
  \tag{9.6.17}\label{eq:9.6.17}
\end{equation}
The last term, which depends only upon the mass of the earth but not its
spin, is called the geodetic
precession;\hyperref[ch9-ref-6]{\textsuperscript{6}} it is essentially just
the Thomas precession caused by gravitation. (See Section~\ref{sec:5.1}.)
The first two terms represent an interaction between the spin and orbital
angular momenta of the earth and the gyroscope, analogous to the hyperfine
interaction of atomic physics. If, for simplicity, we take the gyroscope's
orbit to be a circle of radius \(r\) with unit normal
\(\widehat{\mathbf h}\), then the gyroscope's velocity is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf v
  =
  -\left(\frac{M_\oplus G}{r^3}\right)^{1/2}
  (\mathbf x\times\widehat{\mathbf h}),
  \tag{9.6.18}\label{eq:9.6.18}
\end{equation}
and the precession rate, averaged over a revolution, is
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle\boldsymbol\Omega\right\rangle
  =
  \frac{G\left[
    \mathbf J_\oplus
    -\widehat{\mathbf h}
      (\widehat{\mathbf h}\cdot\mathbf J_\oplus)
  \right]}{2r^3}
  {}+\frac{3(M_\oplus G)^{3/2}}{2r^{5/2}}
  \widehat{\mathbf h}.
  \tag{9.6.19}\label{eq:9.6.19}
\end{equation}
Both terms are maximized by taking \(r\) as small as possible, that is,
about equal to the radius \(R_\oplus\) of the earth. At these low altitudes
the ratio of the first ``hyperfine'' term to the second ``geodetic'' term
is of order
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\text{hyperfine}}{\text{geodetic}}
  \simeq
  \frac{J_\oplus G}
       {3(M_\oplus G)^{3/2}R_\oplus^{1/2}}
  =
  6.5\times10^{-3},
  \tag{9.6.20}\label{eq:9.6.20}
\end{equation}
so the main effect is a precession of the spin around the orbital angular
momentum \(\widehat{\mathbf h}\), with average rate
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle|\boldsymbol\Omega|\right\rangle
  \simeq
  \frac{3(M_\oplus G)^{3/2}}{2r^{5/2}}
  \simeq
  8.4\left(\frac{R_\oplus}{r}\right)^{5/2}
  \frac{\text{sec}}{\text{year}}.
  \tag{9.6.21}\label{eq:9.6.21}
\end{equation}
This should be measurable.\hyperref[ch9-ref-7]{\textsuperscript{7}} In
order to detect the small ``hyperfine'' precession one might direct the spin
axis of the gyroscope along the direction \(\widehat{\mathbf h}\) normal to
the plane of the orbit; in this case the terms in
\(\boldsymbol\Omega\) parallel to \(\widehat{\mathbf h}\) have no effect
[see Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.11}], so the effective precession is just around
\(\mathbf J_\oplus\):
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle\boldsymbol\Omega\right\rangle_{\mathrm{eff}}
  =
  \frac{G\mathbf J_\oplus}{2r^3},
  \tag{9.6.22}\label{eq:9.6.22}
\end{equation}
with magnitude
\begin{equation}
  \left|
    \left\langle\boldsymbol\Omega\right\rangle_{\mathrm{eff}}
  \right|
  =
  0.055\left(\frac{R_\oplus}{r}\right)^3
  \frac{\text{sec}}{\text{year}}.
  \tag{9.6.23}\label{eq:9.6.23}
\end{equation}
In order to maximize the effect of this tiny precession, one would like to
have the spin axis of the gyroscope perpendicular to \(\mathbf J_\oplus\);
since it must also be perpendicular to the plane of the orbit, the best
arrangement would be to place the gyroscope in a polar orbit, with its spin
axis parallel to the equatorial plane of the earth.

As usual, the effect of putting the satellite in an eccentric orbit would
simply be to replace the radius \(r\) everywhere with the semilatus rectum
\(L\). It is also easy to take into account the effect of a possible
departure from Einstein's field equations. The Robertson expansion
\eqref{eq:8.3.1} for a general static spherically symmetric metric in
isotropic coordinates gives (with \(\alpha=1\))
\[
  g_{00}^{(2)}=-2\phi,
  \qquad
  g_{ij}^{(2)}=-2\gamma\phi\delta_{ij},
  \qquad
  g_{i0}^{(3)}=0,
\]
where \(\phi\), as usual, is \(-GM/r\), and \(\gamma\) is a dimensionless
constant that in Einstein's theory would be unity. Referring back to
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:9.1.18}, \eqref{eq:9.1.19}, and \eqref{eq:9.1.21}, we see
that now
\[
  \Gamma^{i(2)}{}_{jk}
  =
  \gamma\left[
    -\partial_k\phi\,\delta_{ij}
    -\partial_j\phi\,\delta_{ik}
    +\partial_i\phi\,\delta_{jk}
  \right],
  \qquad
  \Gamma^{0(2)}{}_{i0}=\partial_i\phi,
  \qquad
  \Gamma^{i(3)}{}_{j0}=0.
\]
Using these in Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.4} gives for the rate of change of spin
\[
  \frac{\dd\mathbf S}{\dd t}
  =
  -(1+\gamma)(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf S)\boldsymbol\nabla\phi
  -\gamma(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)\mathbf S
  +\gamma(\mathbf S\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)\mathbf v.
\]
As before, it is convenient to introduce a spin vector of constant
magnitude, which now is
\[
  \boldsymbol{\mathcal S}
  =
  (1+\gamma\phi)\mathbf S
  -\frac12\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf S).
\]
Again, \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}\) just precesses about a vector
\(\boldsymbol\Omega\),
\[
  \frac{\dd\boldsymbol{\mathcal S}}{\dd t}
  =
  \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal S},
\]
but now \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) is given by
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\Omega
  =
  -\left(\frac12+\gamma\right)
  (\mathbf v\times\boldsymbol\nabla\phi).
  \tag{9.6.24}\label{eq:9.6.24}
\end{equation}
Thus, the effect of a modification of Einstein's field equations on the
geodetic precession is simply to multiply it by a factor
\[
  \frac{1+2\gamma}{3}.
\]
In order to calculate the effects on \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) of a
modification of Einstein's field equations in a system that is not static
and spherically symmetric, it would be necessary to know the details of
the new theory; we shall return to this problem in Section~\ref{sec:9.9}.
