\subsection{Precession of Perihelia}\label{sec:9.5}

We shall now see how the post-Newtonian formalism developed in the last four
sections can be used to calculate the precession of planetary orbits in the
actual solar system, taking into account other planets, solar rotation, solar
oblateness, and so on. The potential \(\phi+\psi\) that determines \(g_{00}\)
[see Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.4.11}] is overwhelmingly dominated by the spherically
symmetric part \(-GM_\odot/r\) of the sun's contribution, so it is
convenient to write
\begin{equation}
  \phi+\psi=-\frac{GM_\odot}{r}+\epsilon(\mathbf x,t),
  \tag{9.5.1}\label{eq:9.5.1}
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon\) includes not only the Newtonian potentials of the other
planets, but also any quadrupole or higher terms in the sun's contribution
to \(\phi+\psi\). Equation~\eqref{eq:9.2.1} for a point particle now reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\mathbf v}{\dd t}
  =
  -\frac{GM_\odot\mathbf x}{r^3}
  +\boldsymbol\eta+\order(\bar v^6),
  \tag{9.5.2}\label{eq:9.5.2}
\end{equation}
with \(\boldsymbol\eta\) a small perturbation,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \boldsymbol\eta={}&
  -\boldsymbol\nabla(\epsilon+2\phi^2)
  -\partial_t\boldsymbol\zeta
  +\mathbf v\times(\boldsymbol\nabla\times\boldsymbol\zeta)\\
  &+3\mathbf v\,\partial_t\phi
  +4\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)
  -v^2\boldsymbol\nabla\phi.
  \end{split}
  \tag{9.5.3}\label{eq:9.5.3}
\end{equation}

By far the most convenient technique for calculating the precession of
perihelia is to compute the rate of change of the Runge--Lenz
vector\hyperref[ch9-ref-1]{\textsuperscript{1}}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf A
  =
  -M_\odot G\frac{\mathbf x}{r}
  +\mathbf v\times\mathbf h.
  \tag{9.5.4}\label{eq:9.5.4}
\end{equation}
Here \(r=|\mathbf x|\), \(\mathbf v=\dd\mathbf x/\dd t\), and
\(\mathbf h\) is the orbital angular momentum per unit mass,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf h\equiv\mathbf x\times\mathbf v.
  \tag{9.5.5}\label{eq:9.5.5}
\end{equation}
If the perturbation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.5.2} were absent, the orbit would
be an ellipse described by
\begin{equation}
  r=\frac{L}{1+e\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)},
  \tag{9.5.6}\label{eq:9.5.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\varphi}{\dd t}
  =
  \frac{\sqrt{LM_\odot G}}{r^2},
  \tag{9.5.7}\label{eq:9.5.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd r}{\dd t}
  =
  e\sqrt{\frac{M_\odot G}{L}}
  \sin(\varphi-\varphi_0),
  \tag{9.5.8}\label{eq:9.5.8}
\end{equation}
with \(e\) the eccentricity and \(L\) the semilatus rectum. (We take the
orbit to lie in the plane \(\theta=\pi/2\), with perihelion at azimuthal
angle \(\varphi_0\).) Then \(\mathbf h\) is a constant vector normal to the
orbit, with magnitude
\begin{equation}
  |\mathbf h|=\sqrt{LM_\odot G},
  \tag{9.5.9}\label{eq:9.5.9}
\end{equation}
and \(\mathbf A\) is a constant vector pointing toward perihelion, with
\begin{equation}
  |\mathbf A|=eM_\odot G.
  \tag{9.5.10}\label{eq:9.5.10}
\end{equation}
Thus the rate of precession caused by any perturbation is the component of
\(\dd\widehat{\mathbf A}/\dd t\), with
\(\widehat{\mathbf A}\equiv\mathbf A/|\mathbf A|\), along the direction
perpendicular to both \(\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf h\):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\varphi_0}{\dd t}
  =
  (\widehat{\mathbf h}\times\widehat{\mathbf A})
  \cdot\frac{\dd\widehat{\mathbf A}}{\dd t}
  =
  (\mathbf h\times\mathbf A)
  \cdot\frac{\dd\mathbf A/\dd t}
  {|\mathbf h|\,|\mathbf A|^2}.
  \tag{9.5.11}\label{eq:9.5.11}
\end{equation}
(If \(\dd\varphi_0/\dd t\) is positive, the precession is in the same
sense as the planet's motion.) A straightforward calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\mathbf A}{\dd t}
  =
  \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf h
  +\mathbf v\times(\mathbf x\times\boldsymbol\eta).
  \tag{9.5.12}\label{eq:9.5.12}
\end{equation}
The result is linear in \(\boldsymbol\eta\), so the precessions produced by
each small term may be added.

The largest term in \(\boldsymbol\eta\) is the part of
\(-\boldsymbol\nabla\epsilon\) arising from the Newtonian potentials of the
other planets. We shall make no attempt to calculate it; the experts tell us
that for Mercury it produces about \(532''\) per century. The next-largest
term is obtained from the relativistic corrections in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.5.3}, setting \(\phi\) and \(\boldsymbol\zeta\) equal to
their values for a spherical nonrotating sun:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_\odot=-\frac{GM_\odot}{r},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol\zeta_\odot=0.
  \tag{9.5.13}\label{eq:9.5.13}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\eta
  =
  -2\boldsymbol\nabla\phi_\odot^2
  +4\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\phi_\odot
  -v^2\boldsymbol\nabla\phi_\odot.
  \tag{9.5.14}\label{eq:9.5.14}
\end{equation}
Writing \(\chi\equiv\varphi-\varphi_0\), substitution in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.5.11} gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \frac{\dd\varphi_0}{\dd t}
  ={}&
  8M_\odot G|\mathbf h|L^{-3}
  (1+e\cos\chi)^3\sin^2\chi\\
  &-M_\odot G e^{-1}|\mathbf h|L^{-3}
  \left[
    7(1+e\cos\chi)^2
    +4(1+e\cos\chi)^3
    +(1+e\cos\chi)^4
  \right]\cos\chi.
  \end{split}
  \tag{9.5.15}\label{eq:9.5.15}
\end{equation}
Since \(\varphi_0\) changes slowly, its change in one revolution can be
determined by integrating with \(\varphi_0\) held fixed and using the
Keplerian formulas:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\varphi_0
  =
  \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\dd\varphi_0}{\dd t}
  \frac{\dd t}{\dd\varphi}\,\dd\varphi
  =
  \frac{L^2}{|\mathbf h|}
  \int_0^{2\pi}
  \frac{\dd\varphi_0}{\dd t}
  [1+e\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)]^{-2}\,\dd\varphi.
  \tag{9.5.16}\label{eq:9.5.16}
\end{equation}
Most terms drop out in the angular integration, leaving
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\varphi_0=6\pi\frac{M_\odot G}{L},
  \tag{9.5.17}\label{eq:9.5.17}
\end{equation}
in agreement with Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.6.11}.

As another small contribution, consider the field produced by the sun's
rotation:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\zeta
  =
  \frac{2G}{r^3}(\mathbf x\times\mathbf J_\odot).
  \tag{9.5.18}\label{eq:9.5.18}
\end{equation}
Its contribution to the acceleration is
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\eta
  =
  \mathbf v\times(\boldsymbol\nabla\times\boldsymbol\zeta)
  =
  6G\mathbf h(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf J_\odot)r^{-5}
  +2G(\mathbf v\times\mathbf J_\odot)r^{-3}.
  \tag{9.5.19}\label{eq:9.5.19}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:9.5.12} then gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \frac{\dd\mathbf A}{\dd t}={}&
  -6G\mathbf h(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x)
   (\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf J_\odot)r^{-5}\\
  &-2G(\mathbf v\times\mathbf J_\odot)
   (\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf v)r^{-3}
  -2G\mathbf v(\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf J_\odot)r^{-3}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{9.5.20}\label{eq:9.5.20}
\end{equation}
For simplicity, take the sun's rotation axis normal to the orbital plane, so
that \(\mathbf J_\odot\) is parallel to \(\mathbf h\). Then
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \frac{\dd\varphi_0}{\dd t}
  =
  \frac{2J_\odot|\mathbf h|^2}{M_\odot L^4e}
  \Bigl\{&
  -(1+e\cos\chi)^2e\sin^2\chi\\
  &-(1+e\cos\chi)^3(e+\cos\chi)
  \Bigr\},
  \end{split}
  \tag{9.5.21}\label{eq:9.5.21}
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.5.16} gives
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\varphi_0
  =
  -\frac{8\pi J_\odot|\mathbf h|}{M_\odot L^2}.
  \tag{9.5.22}\label{eq:9.5.22}
\end{equation}

The sun is generally supposed to have angular momentum
\(J_\odot\simeq1.7\times10^{48}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^2\,s^{-1}}\), and mass
\(M_\odot=1.99\times10^{33}\,\mathrm g\), so in natural units
\[
  \frac{J_\odot}{M_\odot}\simeq0.28\ \mathrm{km}.
\]
Mercury's orbit has \(L=55.5\times10^6\,\mathrm{km}\) and
\(|\mathbf h|=9.03\times10^3\,\mathrm{km}\), giving
\[
  \Delta\varphi_0\simeq-2.06\times10^{-11}
  \ \text{radians/revolution}
\]
or
\[
  \Delta\varphi_0\simeq-17.6\times10^{-4}
  \ \text{arc-sec/century}.
\]
Even if Dicke and Goldenberg are right and the sun's angular momentum is
25 times larger than generally believed, the precession caused by
\(\boldsymbol\zeta\) is still only of order \(0.04''\) per century, too
small to be measured.

The total precession is obtained by adding the Newtonian \(532''\) term,
the Einstein term \eqref{eq:9.5.17}, the
\(\boldsymbol\zeta\)-term \eqref{eq:9.5.22}, Newtonian effects from solar
oblateness, the contribution of the sun's rotation to the anisotropic part
of \(\psi\), and post-Newtonian corrections to the perturbations caused by
other planets. Only the Newtonian terms and the Einstein term
\eqref{eq:9.5.17} are large enough to be measured.
