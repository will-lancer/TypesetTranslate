\subsection{Spin Precession and Mach's Principle\optionalreading}\label{sec:9.7}

The spin precession effects calculated in the last section have a remarkable
interpretation in terms of the ideas of Ernst Mach discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:1.3}. Recall that the spin of a freely falling gyroscope
does \emph{not} precess in an inertial coordinate system that moves along
with the gyroscope; this, after all, is just the meaning of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.1} of parallel transport. Thus, the precession
\(\boldsymbol\Omega\) of a gyroscope in another frame, say one fixed to the
earth, arises entirely from a rotation of the inertial frame carried by the
gyroscope with respect to earth and the distant stars, with angular
frequency \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). This is why \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) does not
depend on the rate of the gyroscope's spin; \emph{any} vector that keeps a
fixed direction in an inertial system will appear to precess in the ``lab''
or earth system with angular frequency \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.12}.

Why should the inertial frame that falls with the gyroscope rotate with
respect to the distant stars? Mach tells us that inertial forces arise from
accelerations, including rotations, with respect to the total matter of the
universe, so a reference frame will be inertial if it is not accelerating
with respect to some average cosmic distribution of matter. Normally this
means that the inertial frames do not rotate with respect to the distant
stars. However, an observer on a gyroscope orbiting the earth sees a mass
distribution consisting not only of the distant stars, but also of a large
sphere called the earth, which appears to revolve around the gyroscope once
every 90 minutes or so, and which also rotates on its own axis. Thus the
inertial frames fixed to the gyroscope have to reach some sort of compromise
between following the distant stars and following the earth; it tries to
rotate in the same direction as the rotation and apparent revolution of the
earth, but lags far behind, the distant stars always winning the struggle.

The rather vague ideas of this sort that are suggested by Mach's Principle
find their concrete expression in detailed calculations based on the
Principle of Equivalence. We saw in Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.6.19} that the
precession of an orbiting gyroscope, and hence the rotation of the inertial
frame it carries, consists of a small ``geodetic'' term parallel to the
orbital angular momentum \(\widehat{\mathbf h}\), and an even smaller
``hyperfine'' term parallel to the component of the earth's spin
\(\mathbf J_\oplus\) perpendicular to \(\widehat{\mathbf h}\). Thus the
rotation and apparent revolution of the earth about the gyroscope do seem
slightly to drag along the inertial frame that falls with the gyroscope.

This effect may be seen more clearly in a thought experiment discussed by
Lense and Thirring\hyperref[ch9-ref-8]{\textsuperscript{8}} shortly after
the advent of general relativity. They considered a hollow spherical shell
that rotates rigidly with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\). According
to Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.4.35}, the metric field \(\boldsymbol\zeta\) inside the
sphere is
\[
  \boldsymbol\zeta=\mathbf x\times\boldsymbol\Omega,
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol\Omega=-\frac{4\phi}{3}\boldsymbol\omega,
\]
with \(\phi\) the constant gravitational potential inside the sphere,
\[
  \phi
  =
  -4\pi G\int_{\mathrm{shell}}
  T^{00(0)}(r')\,r'\,\dd r'.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:9.6.12} then tells us that any inertial frame inside the
sphere rotates with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\).

We note that \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) is parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\),
but smaller by the dimensionless factor \(-4\phi/3\). It is therefore
interesting to ask what would happen if the shell were made so massive that
\(\phi\) approached a value of order \(-3/4\). Would the inertial frames
inside the shell decouple entirely from the distant stars and follow the
shell rotating with frequency \(\omega\)? (We catch an echo of Mach's
remark about Newton's water-bucket experiment quoted in
Section~\ref{sec:1.3}, ``No one is competent to say how the experiment
would turn out if the sides of the vessel increased in thickness and mass
until they were several leagues thick.'') Unfortunately, the
post-Newtonian method breaks down just when this problem becomes
interesting, when \(|\phi|\) is of order unity.

An exact solution of Einstein's equations that \emph{looks} like the metric
outside a spinning sphere has been found by
Kerr;\hyperref[ch9-ref-9]{\textsuperscript{9}} it is of the form
\[
  \begin{split}
  -\dd\tau^2={}&-\dd t^2+\dd\mathbf x^2\\
  &+\frac{2MG\rho}
  {\left[\rho^4+(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf a)^2\right]
   (\rho^2+\mathbf a^2)^2}\\
  &\quad\times
  \Bigl[
    \rho^2\mathbf x\cdot\dd\mathbf x
    +\rho\,\dd\mathbf x\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf x)
    +(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x)(\mathbf a\cdot\dd\mathbf x)\\
  &\hspace{7em}
    +(\rho^2+\mathbf a^2)\rho\,\dd t
  \Bigr]^2,
  \end{split}
\]
where \(\mathbf x\) is a quasi-Euclidean three-vector, \(\mathbf a\) is a
constant vector, scalar products \(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf a\),
\(\mathbf x^2\), and so on are defined as in Euclidean geometry, and
\(\rho\) is defined by
\[
  \rho^4-(r^2-\mathbf a^2)\rho^2
  -(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x)^2=0,
\]
where, as usual, \(r^2\equiv\mathbf x^2\). For \(r\to\infty\), we have
\(\rho\to r\), and the metric coefficients become
\[
  g_{00}\longrightarrow
  -1+\frac{2MG}{r}+\order\left(\frac1{r^2}\right),
\]
\[
  g_{0i}\longrightarrow
  \frac{2MG}{r^2}
  \left[
    x_i+\frac1r(\mathbf a\times\mathbf x)_i
  \right]
  +\order\left(\frac1{r^3}\right),
\]
\[
  g_{ij}\longrightarrow
  \delta_{ij}+\frac{2MG}{r^3}x_ix_j
  +\order\left(\frac1{r^2}\right).
\]
A straightforward calculation, using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.6.22}--\eqref{eq:7.6.24}, shows that the total momentum,
energy, and angular momentum of the system \emph{and its gravitational
field} are
\[
  \mathbf P=0,
  \qquad
  P^0=M,
  \qquad
  \mathbf J=M\mathbf a.
\]

Unfortunately, it has not yet been possible to show that this exact
exterior solution fits smoothly to an exact solution inside a spinning
sphere. Recently Brill and
Cohen\hyperref[ch9-ref-10]{\textsuperscript{10}} have found a solution for
a very thin rotating spherical shell, which is valid both inside and
outside the shell to lowest order in the rotation frequency \(\omega\) but
to all orders in the shell mass \(M\), and which satisfies the correct
continuity conditions across the shell radius \(R\). The solution is
\[
  -\dd\tau^2
  =
  -H(r)\,\dd t^2
  +J(r)\left[
    \dd r^2+r^2\dd\theta^2
    +r^2\sin^2\theta\bigl(\dd\varphi-\Omega(r)\,\dd t\bigr)^2
  \right],
\]
where
\[
  H(r)=
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    \left(\frac{1-2MG/r}{1+2MG/r}\right)^2,
      & r>R,\\[8pt]
    \displaystyle
    \left(\frac{1-2MG/R}{1+2MG/R}\right)^2,
      & r<R,
  \end{cases}
\]
\[
  J(r)=
  \begin{cases}
    (1+2MG/r)^4, & r>R,\\
    (1+2MG/R)^4, & r<R.
  \end{cases}
\]
Inside the sphere the angular velocity \(\Omega(r)\) is a constant,
\[
  \Omega
  =
  \omega\left[
    1+\frac{3(R-2MG)}{4MG(1+\beta)}
  \right]^{-1},
  \qquad r<R,
\]
with \(\beta\) a dimensionless constant that depends on the relative
contributions of \(T^{ii}\) and \(T^{00}\) to the shell's gravitational
mass. We get an inertial coordinate system inside the sphere if we define
new coordinates
\[
  t'=\sqrt H\,t,
  \qquad
  r'=\sqrt J\,r,
  \qquad
  \varphi'=\varphi-\Omega t,
\]
so \(\Omega\) is the rotation frequency (in \(t\) units) of the inertial
frames within the shell with respect to the Minkowski metric at infinity.
When \(MG\) is small and \(\beta\) is small, \(\Omega/\omega\) approaches
the post-Newtonian value \(4MG/3R\), but when \(MG\) is so large that the
Schwarzschild radius \(2MG\) of the shell approaches the shell radius
\(R\), the ratio \(\Omega/\omega\) approaches unity, as Mach might have
expected.
