\subsection{Signs of the Times}\label{sec:3.6}

The relation between the Minkowski metric $\eta_{\rho\sigma}$ and the metric
tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ of the theory of gravitation may be expressed in matrix
notation
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf g=D^T\boldsymbol\eta D,
  \tag{3.6.1}\label{eq:3.6.1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf g$ is the $4\times4$ matrix whose elements are the
$g_{\mu\nu}$, $\boldsymbol\eta$ is the matrix whose elements are the
$\eta_{\rho\sigma}$, and $D$ is the matrix
\begin{equation}
  D_{\rho\mu}\equiv\frac{\partial\xi^\rho}{\partial x^\mu},
  \tag{3.6.2}\label{eq:3.6.2}
\end{equation}
with $D^T$ its transpose,
\[
  (D^T)_{\mu\rho}\equiv D_{\rho\mu}.
\]
It has been tacitly assumed as part of the Principle of Equivalence that the
transformation from laboratory coordinates $x^\mu$ to locally inertial
coordinates $\xi^\rho$ is nonsingular; that is, $\xi^\rho$ is a
differentiable function of $x^\mu$ and $x^\mu$ is a differentiable function
of $\xi^\rho$. It follows that there exists a matrix
\begin{equation}
  (D^{-1})^\mu{}_\rho\equiv
  \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial\xi^\rho},
  \tag{3.6.3}\label{eq:3.6.3}
\end{equation}
which is reciprocal to $D$, that is,
\[
  (D^{-1}D)^\mu{}_\nu
  =\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial\xi^\rho}
  \frac{\partial\xi^\rho}{\partial x^\nu}
  =\delta^\mu_\nu,
\]
so that $D$ must have nonvanishing determinant
\begin{equation}
  \det D\ne0.
  \tag{3.6.4}\label{eq:3.6.4}
\end{equation}
A transformation of the form \eqref{eq:3.6.1} with $D$ having a
nonvanishing determinant is called a \emph{congruence}.

The fact that $g_{\mu\nu}$ is related to $\eta_{\rho\sigma}$ by the
congruence \eqref{eq:3.6.1} does not mean that the eigenvalues of
$g_{\mu\nu}$ are the same as those of $\eta_{\rho\sigma}$, as would be the
case if this were a similarity transformation. (Indeed, there are
\emph{no} invariant functions of the components of the metric tensor,
although there are invariant functions of the $g_{\mu\nu}$ \emph{and} their
derivatives, as shown in Chapter~6.) However, there is a theorem known as
Sylvester's law of inertia~\hyperref[ch3-ref-9]{[9]} that states that the
\emph{numbers} of eigenvalues that are respectively positive, negative, or
zero do not change under such a congruence. We conclude then that the metric
tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ must, like $\eta_{\rho\sigma}$, have three positive
eigenvalues, one negative eigenvalue, and no zero eigenvalues. It is this
property of the metric that distinguishes our familiar $(3+1)$-dimensional
spacetime from 4-dimensional space, or $(2+2)$-dimensional spacetime, or
worse.
