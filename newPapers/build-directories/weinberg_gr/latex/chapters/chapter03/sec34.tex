\subsection{The Newtonian Limit}\label{sec:3.4}

To make contact with Newton's theory, let us consider the case of a particle
moving slowly in a weak stationary gravitational field. If the particle is
sufficiently slow, we may neglect $\dd\mathbf x/\dd\tau$ with respect to
$\dd t/\dd\tau$, and write \eqref{eq:3.2.3} as
\[
  \frac{\dd^2x^\mu}{\dd\tau^2}
  +\Gamma^\mu{}_{00}\left(\frac{\dd t}{\dd\tau}\right)^2=0.
\]
Since the field is stationary, all time derivatives of $g_{\mu\nu}$ vanish,
and therefore
\[
  \Gamma^\mu{}_{00}=-\frac12g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu g_{00}.
\]
Finally, since the field is weak, we may adopt a nearly Cartesian coordinate
system in which
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu},
  \qquad |h_{\mu\nu}|\ll1,
  \tag{3.4.1}\label{eq:3.4.1}
\end{equation}
so to first order in $h_{\mu\nu}$,
\[
  \Gamma^\mu{}_{00}
  =-\frac12\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu h_{00}.
\]
Using this affine connection in the equations of motion then gives
\[
  \frac{\dd^2\mathbf x}{\dd\tau^2}
  =\frac12\left(\frac{\dd t}{\dd\tau}\right)^2\nabla h_{00},
  \qquad
  \frac{\dd^2t}{\dd\tau^2}=0.
\]
The solution of the second equation is that $\dd t/\dd\tau$ equals a
constant (as could also be seen by computing $\dd\tau$ with $h_{\mu\nu}$
neglected), so dividing the equation for $\dd^2\mathbf x/\dd\tau^2$ by
$(\dd t/\dd\tau)^2$, we find
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2\mathbf x}{\dd t^2}=\frac12\nabla h_{00}.
  \tag{3.4.2}\label{eq:3.4.2}
\end{equation}
The corresponding Newtonian result is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2\mathbf x}{\dd t^2}=-\nabla\phi,
  \tag{3.4.3}\label{eq:3.4.3}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the gravitational potential, which at a distance $r$ from
the center of a spherical body of mass $M$ takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \phi=-\frac{GM}{r}.
  \tag{3.4.4}\label{eq:3.4.4}
\end{equation}
Comparing \eqref{eq:3.4.2} with \eqref{eq:3.4.3}, we conclude that
\[
  h_{00}=-2\phi+\text{constant}.
\]
Furthermore, the coordinate system must become Minkowskian at great
distances, so $h_{00}$ vanishes at infinity, and if we define $\phi$ to
vanish at infinity [as in \eqref{eq:3.4.4}], we find that the constant here
is zero, so $h_{00}=-2\phi$, and returning to the metric
\eqref{eq:3.4.1},
\begin{equation}
  g_{00}=-(1+2\phi).
  \tag{3.4.5}\label{eq:3.4.5}
\end{equation}

The gravitational potential $\phi$ is of the order of $10^{-39}$ at the
surface of a proton, $10^{-9}$ at the surface of the earth, $10^{-6}$ at the
surface of the sun, and $10^{-4}$ at the surface of a white dwarf star, so
evidently the distortion in $g_{\mu\nu}$ produced by gravitation is
generally very slight. (In c.g.s.\ units $\phi$ has the dimensions of a
squared velocity; in our units $\phi$ is the c.g.s.\ value divided by the
square of the c.g.s.\ speed of light.)
