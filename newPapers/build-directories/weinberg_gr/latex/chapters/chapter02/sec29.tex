\subsection{Spin}\label{sec:2.9}

One important use we can make of the energy-momentum tensor \(T^{\mu\nu}\) is
to define angular momentum and spin. Consider first an isolated system, for
which the \emph{total} energy-momentum tensor \(T^{\mu\nu}\) is conserved:
\[
  \partial_\nu T^{\mu\nu}=0 .
\]
We can use \(T\) to construct another tensor,
\begin{equation}
  M^{\rho\mu\nu}\equiv x^\mu T^{\rho\nu}-x^\nu T^{\rho\mu},
  \tag{2.9.1}\label{eq:2.9.1}
\end{equation}
and because \(T\) is conserved and symmetric, \(M\) is also conserved:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\rho M^{\rho\mu\nu}
  =T^{\mu\nu}-T^{\nu\mu}=0 .
  \tag{2.9.2}\label{eq:2.9.2}
\end{equation}
We can then form a \emph{total angular momentum}
\begin{equation}
  J^{\mu\nu}=\int \dd^3x\,M^{0\mu\nu}=-J^{\nu\mu}.
  \tag{2.9.3}\label{eq:2.9.3}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.9.2} we see (by following the arguments of the last
section) that \(J^{\mu\nu}\) is constant in time and is a tensor. We further
note that
\[
  J^{ij}=\int \dd^3x\,
  \bigl(x^iT^{j0}-x^jT^{i0}\bigr),
\]
and since \(T^{j0}\) is the density of the \(j\)th component of momentum, we
may regard \(J^{23},J^{31},J^{12}\) as the \(1,2,3\) components of the angular
momentum. The other components of \(J^{\mu\nu}\) are
\[
  J^{0\ii}=tp^i-\int x^iT^{00}\,\dd^3x .
\]
These components have no clear physical significance, and in fact can be made
to vanish if we fix the origin of coordinates to coincide with the ``center of
energy'' at \(t=0\); that is, if at \(t=0\) the moment
\(\int x^iT^{00}\,\dd^3x\) vanishes.

Although a tensor with regard to the homogeneous Lorentz transformations
\(x^\mu\to\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu\), the total angular momentum behaves
peculiarly under the translation \(x^\mu\to x'^\mu=x^\mu+a^\mu\). From
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.9.3} and \eqref{eq:2.8.13} we find that
\begin{equation}
  J^{\mu\nu}\longrightarrow J'^{\mu\nu}
  =J^{\mu\nu}+a^\mu p^\nu-a^\nu p^\mu .
  \tag{2.9.4}\label{eq:2.9.4}
\end{equation}
This is of course because \(J^{\mu\nu}\) includes the orbital angular momentum,
which is always defined with respect to some center of rotation. In order to
isolate the \emph{internal} part of \(J^{\mu\nu}\), it is convenient to define
a \emph{spin four-vector}
\begin{equation}
  S_\mu\equiv\frac12\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
  J^{\nu\rho}U^\sigma ,
  \tag{2.9.5}\label{eq:2.9.5}
\end{equation}
where \(\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\) is the completely antisymmetric tensor
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:2.5}, and
\[
  U^\mu\equiv\frac{p^\mu}{(-p_\nu p^\nu)^{1/2}}
\]
is the four-vector velocity of the system. Because of the antisymmetry of
\(\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\), the translation
\(x^\mu\to x^\mu+a^\mu\), which changes \(J^{\nu\rho}\) according to the
rule \eqref{eq:2.9.4}, does \emph{not} change \(S_\mu\). Furthermore,
\(S_\mu\) is obviously a vector and is constant for a free particle:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd S_\mu}{\dd t}=0 .
  \tag{2.9.6}\label{eq:2.9.6}
\end{equation}
Finally we note that in the center-of-mass frame of the system
\(U^i=0\) and \(U^0=1\), so in this frame
\begin{equation}
  S_1=J^{23},\qquad S_2=J^{31},\qquad
  S_3=J^{12},\qquad S_0=0 .
  \tag{2.9.7}\label{eq:2.9.7}
\end{equation}
This justifies us in regarding \(S_\mu\) as the internal angular momentum of
the system. Even when the velocity \(\mathbf U\) is not zero, \(S_\mu\) really
has only three independent components, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.9.5} gives
\begin{equation}
  U^\mu S_\mu=0 .
  \tag{2.9.8}\label{eq:2.9.8}
\end{equation}
We use these properties of \(S_\mu\) later, when we discuss the precession of
a gyroscope in free fall.
