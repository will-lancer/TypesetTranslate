\subsection{Energy and Momentum}\label{sec:2.4}

The relativistic form \eqref{eq:2.3.1} of Newton's second law immediately
suggests that we define an energy-momentum four-vector
\begin{equation}
  p^\mu\equiv m\frac{\dd x^\mu}{\dd\tau}
  \tag{2.4.1}\label{eq:2.4.1}
\end{equation}
and write the second law as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd p^\mu}{\dd\tau}=f^\mu .
  \tag{2.4.2}\label{eq:2.4.2}
\end{equation}
Recall that
\[
  \dd\tau=(\dd t^2-\dd\mathbf{x}^2)^{1/2}
  =(1-\mathbf v^2)^{1/2}\dd t ,
  \qquad
  \mathbf v\equiv\frac{\dd\mathbf{x}}{\dd t}.
\]
Then the space components of \(p^\mu\) form the momentum vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf p=m\gamma\mathbf v ,
  \tag{2.4.3}\label{eq:2.4.3}
\end{equation}
and its time component is the energy
\begin{equation}
  p^0\equiv E=m\gamma ,
  \tag{2.4.4}\label{eq:2.4.4}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \gamma\equiv\frac{\dd t}{\dd\tau}
  =(1-\mathbf v^2)^{-1/2}.
  \tag{2.4.5}\label{eq:2.4.5}
\end{equation}
For small \(\mathbf v\), these definitions give
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf p=m\mathbf v+\order(v^3),
  \tag{2.4.6}\label{eq:2.4.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  E=m+\frac12m\mathbf v^2+\order(v^4),
  \tag{2.4.7}\label{eq:2.4.7}
\end{equation}
in agreement with the nonrelativistic formulas, except for the term \(m\) in
\(E\). (Recall that in our units \(1\) sec equals \(3\times10^{10}\) cm, so
\(1\) g equals \(9\times10^{20}\) ergs.) Sometimes the factor \(m\gamma\) is
called the relativistic mass \(\widetilde m\), so that
\(\mathbf p=\widetilde m\mathbf v\). I do not follow this custom here; for us,
``mass'' will always mean the constant \(m\).

Why do we call \(\mathbf p\) and \(E\) the relativistic momentum and energy?
We can use these names for anything we like, but if the concepts of momentum
and energy are to be useful they must be reserved for quantities that are
\emph{conserved}. The unique feature of our \(\mathbf p\) and \(E\) is that,
if one observer says that they are conserved in a reaction, then so will any
other observer related to the first by a Lorentz transformation. Note that
\(\dd x^\mu\) is a four-vector whereas \(m\) and \(\dd\tau\) are invariants, so the
\(p^\mu\) for any single particle is a four-vector; that is, it transforms under
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.1.1} like
\[
  p'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu p^\nu .
\]
Since \(\Lambda\) does not depend on anything but the Lorentz transformation
being performed, it follows that in any reaction, the change of the sum of the
\(p^\mu\) of all particles is also a four-vector:
\[
  \Delta\sum_n p_n'^\mu
  =\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,\Delta\sum_n p_n^\nu .
\]
(The sums run over all particles, and \(\Delta\) denotes the difference between
initial and final states.) The conservation of \(\mathbf p\) and \(E\) in the
original inertial frame tells us that \(\Delta\sum_n p_n^\mu\) vanishes, so in
any coordinate system related to the first by a Lorentz transformation they will
still be conserved; that is, \(\Delta\sum_n p_n'^\mu\) will vanish.

[I shall not show here that \(\mathbf p\) and \(E\) are the \emph{only}
functions of velocity whose conservation is Lorentz
invariant.\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-3]{3}} However, it is worth
stressing that \(E\) must be conserved if \(\mathbf p\) is. For suppose that
momentum is conserved in two different coordinate systems related by a Lorentz
transformation, that is,
\[
  \Delta\sum_n\mathbf p_n=0,
  \qquad
  \Delta\sum_n\mathbf p'_n=0 .
\]
Since \(\Delta\sum_n p_n^\mu\) is a four-vector, we have
\[
  \Delta\sum_n p_n'^i
  =\Lambda^i{}_\mu\,\Delta\sum_n p_n^\mu ,
\]
and using momentum conservation in both coordinate systems, this gives
\[
  0=\Lambda^i{}_0\,\Delta\sum_n p_n^0 .
\]
But \(\Lambda^i{}_0\) is not necessarily zero, so \(p^0=E\) is conserved.]

At zero velocity the energy \(E\) has the finite value \(m\). For this reason
we sometimes give the name ``kinetic energy'' to the quantity \(E-m\), which
for small \(v\) is approximately \(\tfrac12mv^2\). If the total mass is
conserved in a reaction (as in elastic scattering), then the kinetic energy is
conserved, but if some mass is destroyed (as in radioactive decay or fusion or
fission) then very large quantities of kinetic energy will be liberated, with
consequences of well-known importance.

The velocity can be eliminated from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.4.3} and
\eqref{eq:2.4.4}, yielding a relation between energy and momentum
\begin{equation}
  E(\mathbf p)=(\mathbf p^2+m^2)^{1/2}.
  \tag{2.4.8}\label{eq:2.4.8}
\end{equation}
This can also be derived by noting from Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.4.1} and the
definition of \(\dd\tau\) that
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\mu\nu}p^\mu p^\nu=-m^2 .
  \tag{2.4.9}\label{eq:2.4.9}
\end{equation}
For a photon or neutrino we must set \(\mathbf v^2=1\) and \(m=0\), so
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.4.3} and \eqref{eq:2.4.4} become indeterminate, but their
ratio gives a relation useful for all particles:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathbf p}{E}=\mathbf v .
  \tag{2.4.10}\label{eq:2.4.10}
\end{equation}
Note that for \(m=0\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.4.8} gives
\[
  E=\lvert\mathbf p\rvert ,
\]
so \(\mathbf v\) is a unit vector, as of course it must be for a massless
particle.
