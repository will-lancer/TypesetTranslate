\subsection{Naive Models: The Olbers Paradox}\label{sec:16.1}

Throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, perhaps a majority of
astronomers would have subscribed to a simple cosmological picture, in which
the universe is supposed to be infinite, eternal, and Euclidean, and the stars
are more or less at rest, with constant average luminosity per unit volume.
Such naive models would seem to be ruled out by the discovery of the general
red shift of distant galaxies, but it is still of some interest to note an
argument against the naive cosmologies, which was offered in 1744 by the Swiss
astronomer J.~P.~L. de Cheseaux,\hyperref[ch16-ref-1]{[1]} and, independently
in 1826, by Heinrich Wilhelm Matthias
Olbers\hyperref[ch16-ref-2]{[2]} (1758--1840). Their argument was based on the
most ancient of all astronomical observations, that the sky grows dark when
the sun goes down.

To see the significance of this observation, note that if absorption is
neglected, the apparent luminosity of a star of absolute luminosity \(L\) at a
distance \(r\) in a naive cosmological model will be \(L/4\pi r^2\). If the
number density of such stars is a constant \(n\), then the number of stars at
distances between \(r\) and \(r+\dd r\) is \(4\pi nr^2\dd r\), so the total
radiant energy density due to all stars is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_s
  =
  \int_0^\infty
  \left(\frac{L}{4\pi r^2}\right)4\pi nr^2\,\dd r
  =
  Ln\int_0^\infty\dd r.
  \tag{16.1.1}\label{eq:16.1.1}
\end{equation}
The integral diverges, leading to an infinite energy density of starlight!

In order to avoid this paradox, both de Cheseaux and Olbers postulated the
existence of an interstellar medium that absorbs the light from the very
distant stars responsible for the divergence of the integral
\eqref{eq:16.1.1}. However, this resolution of the paradox is
unsatisfactory,\hyperref[ch16-ref-3]{[3]} because in an eternal universe the
temperature of the interstellar medium would have to rise until the medium was
in thermal equilibrium with the starlight, in which case it would be emitting
as much energy as it absorbs, and hence could not reduce the average radiant
energy density. The stars themselves are of course opaque, and totally block
out the light from sufficiently distant sources, but if \emph{this} is the
resolution of the Olbers paradox, then every line of sight must terminate at
the surface of a star, so the whole sky should have a temperature equal to that
at the surface of a typical star.

To see how modern cosmological models avoid the Olbers paradox, we note that
according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.4.12}, the apparent luminosity of a star of
absolute luminosity \(L\) at a comoving coordinate \(r_1\) is (now neglecting
absorption)
\[
  l=\frac{L a^2(t_1)}{4\pi a^4(t_0)r_1^2},
\]
where \(t_0\) is the time the star is observed and \(t_1\) is the time the light
was emitted. Also, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.4}, the number of stars of
luminosity between \(L\) and \(L+\dd L\), whose light, observed at time \(t_0\),
was emitted between times \(t_1-\dd t_1\) and \(t_1\), is
\[
  \dd N
  =
  4\pi a^2(t_1)r_1^2 n(t_1,L)\,\dd t_1\,\dd L,
\]
where \(n(t_1,L)\dd L\) is the number density of stars at time \(t_1\) with
luminosity between \(L\) and \(L+\dd L\). The total energy density of starlight
is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{s0}
  =
  \int l\,\dd N
  =
  \int_{-\infty}^{t_0}
  \mathscr L(t_1)
  \left[\frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_0)}\right]^4
  \dd t_1,
  \tag{16.1.2}\label{eq:16.1.2}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathscr L\) is the proper luminosity density
\[
  \mathscr L(t_1)
  \equiv
  \int n(t_1,L)L\,\dd L.
\]

In a ``big-bang'' cosmology, there is obviously no paradox, since the integral
\eqref{eq:16.1.2} is effectively cut off at a lower limit \(t_1=0\), and the
integrand vanishes at \(t_1=0\), roughly like \(a(t_1)\). The question of an
Olbers paradox arises only in models, such as the steady state cosmology, in
which the universe is supposed to have existed for an infinitely long time. In
such models, a necessary condition for avoidance of the Olbers paradox is that
\begin{equation}
  t_1a^4(t_1)\mathscr L(t_1)\longrightarrow0
  \qquad\text{for}\qquad
  t_1\longrightarrow-\infty.
  \tag{16.1.3}\label{eq:16.1.3}
\end{equation}
For neutrinos there is a slightly stronger
condition,\hyperref[ch16-ref-4]{[4]} with \(a^3(t_1)\) in place of
\(a^4(t_1)\), because one of the factors of \(a(t_1)/a(t_0)\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.1.2} arose from the loss of energy by individual red-shifted
photons, and for neutrinos the \emph{number} density as well as the
\emph{energy} density is in principle observable. The only popular cosmology
in which \eqref{eq:16.1.3} is not satisfied is the oscillating model discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:15.11}. In this case, absorption is needed to avoid an
Olbers paradox, but the absorption occurs during the highly contracted era, and
the red shift during the subsequent expansion saves us from an intolerably
bright night sky. From this point of view, the \(2.7\,\mathrm K\) microwave
background appears as the pale image of the fiery furnace with which we were
threatened by de Cheseaux and Olbers.
