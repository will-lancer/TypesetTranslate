\subsection{The Red-Shift Versus Distance Relation}\label{sec:14.6}

We shall now consider how the correlation of red shifts with distances can
be used to gain information about the cosmic scale factor \(a(t)\). For our
present purposes, only luminosity distances will be considered;
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.4.22} and \eqref{eq:14.4.23} show that no new information
can be gained by studying the correlation of red shifts with angular
diameters or proper motions instead of apparent luminosities.

Suppose then that astronomers are able to define some family of objects, like
the brightest E galaxies discussed at the end of the last section, whose
absolute luminosities \(L\) are all known. By measuring their apparent
luminosities, their luminosity distances can be calculated from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.4.13}:
\[
  d_L=\left(\frac{L}{4\pi l}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
Suppose also that the red shifts \(z\) of these objects are measured, so that
an empirical curve for \(d_L(z)\) is known. What does this tell us about
\(a(t)\)?

The observables \(d_L\) and \(z\) are related to the unknown coordinates of
the light source by the theoretical relations \eqref{eq:14.3.1},
\eqref{eq:14.3.6}, and \eqref{eq:14.4.14}:
\[
  \int_{t_1}^{t_0}\frac{\dd t}{a(t)}
  =
  \int_0^{r_1}\frac{\dd r}{\sqrt{1-kr^2}},
\]
\[
  \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}=1+z,
\]
\[
  d_L
  =
  \frac{a^2(t_0)}{a(t_1)}r_1
  =
  a(t_0)(1+z)r_1.
\]
At present, the curve \(d_L(z)\) is tolerably well known only for small
\(z\), so we are primarily concerned with the case where \(t_0-t_1\) and
\(r_1\) are small. The cosmic scale factor \(a(t)\) may then be most
usefully expressed as a power series
\begin{equation}
  a(t)
  =
  a(t_0)
  \left[
    1+H_0(t-t_0)
    -\frac12 q_0H_0^2(t-t_0)^2
    +\cdots
  \right],
  \tag{14.6.1}\label{eq:14.6.1}
\end{equation}
where \(t_0\) is the present moment, and \(H_0\) and \(q_0\) are parameters
known as Hubble's constant and the deceleration parameter:
\begin{equation}
  H_0\equiv\frac{\dot a(t_0)}{a(t_0)},
  \tag{14.6.2}\label{eq:14.6.2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  q_0\equiv-\frac{a(t_0)\ddot a(t_0)}{\dot a^2(t_0)}.
  \tag{14.6.3}\label{eq:14.6.3}
\end{equation}
(Dots denote derivatives with respect to time.) We shall see in the next
chapter that the whole function \(a(t)\) can be calculated by using
Einstein's field equations if we know the values of \(H_0\) and \(q_0\),
with \(k>0\) if \(q_0>\tfrac12\) and \(k<0\) if \(q_0<\tfrac12\). Our
present discussion will therefore be directed to the measurement of these
two critical parameters.

The use of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.1} in \eqref{eq:14.3.6} yields a power series
for the red shift as a function of the time of flight \(t_0-t_1\):
\begin{equation}
  z
  =
  H_0(t_0-t_1)
  +
  \left(1+\frac{q_0}{2}\right)
  H_0^2(t_0-t_1)^2
  +\cdots.
  \tag{14.6.4}\label{eq:14.6.4}
\end{equation}
Inverting this power series, we obtain a formula for the time of flight in
terms of the red shift:
\begin{equation}
  t_0-t_1
  =
  H_0^{-1}
  \left[
    z-\left(1+\frac{q_0}{2}\right)z^2+\cdots
  \right].
  \tag{14.6.5}\label{eq:14.6.5}
\end{equation}
To find \(r_1\), we expand Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.1}:
\begin{align}
  \frac{1}{a(t_0)}
  \int_{t_1}^{t_0}
  \left[
    1+H_0(t_0-t)
    +\left(1+\frac{q_0}{2}\right)H_0^2(t_0-t)^2
    +\cdots
  \right]\dd t
  &=
  r_1+O(r_1^3),
  \notag\\
  \frac{1}{a(t_0)}
  \left[
    t_0-t_1+\frac12H_0(t_0-t_1)^2+\cdots
  \right]
  &=
  r_1+O(r_1^3).
  \tag{14.6.6}\label{eq:14.6.6}
\end{align}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.5} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.6} gives \(r_1\) in
terms of the red shift:
\begin{equation}
  r_1
  =
  \frac{1}{a(t_0)H_0}
  \left[
    z-\frac12(1+q_0)z^2+\cdots
  \right],
  \tag{14.6.7}\label{eq:14.6.7}
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.4.14} then gives the luminosity distance as a power
series:
\begin{equation}
  d_L
  =
  H_0^{-1}
  \left[
    z+\frac12(1-q_0)z^2+\cdots
  \right].
  \tag{14.6.8}\label{eq:14.6.8}
\end{equation}
This can also be written as a formula for apparent luminosity:
\begin{equation}
  l
  =
  \frac{L}{4\pi d_L^2}
  =
  \frac{LH_0^2}{4\pi z^2}
  \left[
    1+(q_0-1)z+\cdots
  \right],
  \tag{14.6.9}\label{eq:14.6.9}
\end{equation}
or equivalently, for the distance modulus:
\begin{align}
  m-M
  ={}&
  25
  -5\log_{10}\!\left[
    H_0\,(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}})
  \right]
  +5\log_{10}\!\left[
    cz\,(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})
  \right]
  \notag\\
  &\quad
  +1.086(1-q_0)z+\cdots.
  \tag{14.6.10}\label{eq:14.6.10}
\end{align}
[One Mpc is \(10^6\,\mathrm{pc}\). Note that
\(100\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\) equals
\((9.78\times10^9\,\mathrm{yr})^{-1}\).] The program is then to compare
either Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.8}, \eqref{eq:14.6.9}, or \eqref{eq:14.6.10}
with astronomical data, and thereby to determine the critical parameters
\(q_0\) and \(H_0\).

In order to measure \(q_0\), we need to go out to large values of \(z\)
(say, \(z>0.1\)), where only brightest cluster galaxies (or possibly
supernovae) can be used as distance indicators. However, it is only the
shape of the curve of \(d_L\), \(l\), or \(m\) versus \(z\) that is needed.

In order to measure \(H_0\), a single object with \(z<0.1\) is all we need,
but we have to know its absolute luminosity as well as its red shift and
apparent luminosity. Also, its red shift must be large enough (say,
\(z>0.01\)) so that its radial velocity reflects the general expansion of
the universe, rather than a local velocity anomaly. Unfortunately, the Virgo
cluster, whose luminosity is known from observation of its brightest stars
and globular clusters (as in ``rung 4'' of the cosmic distance ladder
described in the last section), has a radial velocity of only about
\(1000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), which is not large enough to ensure the
dominance of the cosmological recession. There is a possibility that
``rung 4'' can be employed out to larger red shifts, for example by using the
angular diameters of H\,II regions. However, at present the only way to
extend measurements of \(H_0\) as well as \(q_0\) out to large red shifts is
to use all five rungs of the cosmic distance ladder, taking as distance
indicators the brightest galaxies of rich clusters.

This program is subject to a great many complications. Some, which are now
taken into account by applying well-understood corrections to the data,
include:

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(A) Galactic Rotation.}
The rotation of our galaxy gives the sun a velocity of about
\(215\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). This produces systematic red or blue shifts
in the spectra of distant galaxies, which are routinely subtracted from the
observed red shifts in calculating the ``cosmological'' red shift \(z\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(B) Aperture.}
Since the edges of galaxies fade gradually into the background light of the
sky, it is necessary to refer all measurements of galactic apparent
luminosities to a standard telescope aperture.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(C) \(k\)-Term.}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:14.4}, the red shift will distort the
frequency distribution of light from distant objects, so that their visual
or blue magnitudes reflect their absolute luminosities at higher frequencies
than for near objects. If we know the intrinsic frequency distribution, we
can correct for this effect by using Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.4.35}, with the result
that the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.10} is replaced with
\(m_B-M_B-k_B(z)\), where \(k_B(z)\) is an explicitly known function of
\(z\), calculated by Oke and
Sandage.\hyperref[ch14-ref-39]{\textsuperscript{39}} In an alternative
procedure developed by Baum,\hyperref[ch14-ref-40]{\textsuperscript{40}} the
luminosity distribution is measured directly for each galaxy studied, so
that all apparent magnitudes can be referred to the same emission frequency,
and no \(k\)-term is needed.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(D) Absorption.}
Our galaxy is known to absorb a certain fraction of the light coming to us
from extragalactic objects. Treating our galaxy as an infinite flat slab, the
distance through the galaxy that a light ray must travel on its way to us is
proportional to \(\csc b\), where \(b\) is the angle between the line of
sight and the plane of the galaxy. The light will therefore be dimmed by a
factor \(\exp(-X\csc b)\), with \(X\) some constant. Taking \(X\) from
studies of the nearer extragalactic objects, the result is then that the
left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.10} is replaced with the corrected
distance modulus
\[
  (m-M)_{\mathrm{corr}}
  =
  m_B-M_B-k_B(z)-A_B(b),
\]
where, roughly,
\[
  A_B(b)\simeq0.25\csc b.
\]
(This is somewhat of an oversimplification.
Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-41]{\textsuperscript{41}} first applies an
absorption correction \(A_V(b)=0.18(\csc b-1)\) to the visual magnitudes,
then converts to blue magnitudes, and then applies an additional correction
\(A_B=0.25\).) No correction is generally made for extragalactic
absorption.\hyperref[ch14-ref-42]{\textsuperscript{42}} (See
Section~15.4.)

In addition to the above complications, which are pretty well understood,
there are a number of other possible sources of error, whose status is much
more in doubt.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(E) Uncertainty in \(L\).}
As emphasized in Section~\ref{sec:14.5}, a new correction at any rung of the
cosmic distance ladder, such as a change in the distance to the Hyades or the
Virgo cluster, would require a corresponding correction to the estimated
absolute luminosity of the brightest E galaxies. Inspection of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.9} or \eqref{eq:14.6.10} shows that this would affect the
value of Hubble's constant, but would not change the deceleration parameter
\(q_0\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(F) Scott Effect.}
It was also emphasized in the last section that, if there is no sharp upper
limit to the absolute luminosity of cluster galaxies, then the tendency to
select richer clusters at greater distances would mean that the absolute
luminosities of their brightest galaxies would increase with \(z\).
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.9}, this selection effect would lead to an
overestimate of the deceleration parameter \(q_0\). However, the Scott
effect, if real, would only enter at very great distances, and therefore
would have little effect on the value of \(H_0\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(G) Shear Field.}
De Vaucouleurs\hyperref[ch14-ref-43]{\textsuperscript{43}} has suggested the
existence of a local anisotropy in the galactic velocity field, encompassing
our own local group and the Virgo cluster. If this is true, it could mean
that red shifts with \(cz\) less than about
\(4000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) do not accurately reflect the general
expansion of the universe.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(H) Galactic Evolution.}
As we look further and further out into space, we see galaxies that are
presumably younger and younger. It may be that the luminosity of the
brightest E galaxies is a function \(L(t_1)\) of the time the light was
emitted. Equation~\eqref{eq:14.6.5} tells us then that \(L\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.9} should be replaced with
\[
  L(t_1)
  =
  L(t_0)
  \left[
    1-E_0(t_0-t_1)+\cdots
  \right]
  =
  L(t_0)
  \left[
    1-\frac{E_0z}{H_0}+\cdots
  \right],
\]
where
\begin{equation}
  E_0\equiv\frac{\dot L(t_0)}{L(t_0)}.
  \tag{14.6.11}\label{eq:14.6.11}
\end{equation}
The effect would be that \(q_0\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.9} would be replaced
with an effective deceleration parameter
\begin{equation}
  q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}
  =
  q_0-\frac{E_0}{H_0}.
  \tag{14.6.12}\label{eq:14.6.12}
\end{equation}
Sandage has recently given two different estimates for the rate of change of
\(L\) for brightest E galaxies---in our notation, they
are\hyperref[ch14-ref-44]{\textsuperscript{44}}
\begin{equation}
  E_0
  =
  \frac{0.04\pm0.02}{10^9\,\mathrm{yr}},
  \tag{14.6.13}\label{eq:14.6.13}
\end{equation}
and\hyperref[ch14-ref-45]{\textsuperscript{45}}
\begin{equation}
  E_0
  =
  \frac{0.00\pm0.05}{10^9\,\mathrm{yr}}.
  \tag{14.6.14}\label{eq:14.6.14}
\end{equation}
As we shall see, a rate of evolution \(E_0\) of order
\(0.04/(10^9\,\mathrm{yr})\) would have an important effect on the value of
\(q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}\).

Returning now to the observations of red shifts and luminosity distances, we
must pick up our story where we left it in Section~\ref{sec:14.3}, with
Hubble's 1929 discovery\hyperref[ch14-ref-13]{\textsuperscript{13}} of a
linear relation between \(d_L\) and \(z\). Hubble estimated the distance to
18 nearby galaxies from the apparent magnitudes of their brightest stars,
and plotted the results against Slipher's red shifts for these objects. (The
absolute magnitude of the brightest stars was known from studies of our local
group of galaxies, whose distance was known from observation of their Cepheid
variables, whose \(P\)-\(L\) curve had been calibrated by Shapley from
statistical studies of proper motions and radial velocities. For details,
see the last section.) The most distant galaxies used by Hubble were members
of the Virgo cluster, with a radial velocity of
\(1000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). This is not much greater than the root-mean-
square random galactic velocity, and Hubble's data points were consequently
spread all over the \(d_L\)-versus-\(z\) plot. Nevertheless, he was somehow
able to deduce a ``roughly linear'' relation between \(cz\) and \(d_L\),
with slope
\[
  H_0
  \simeq
  500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
  \simeq
  (2\times10^9\,\mathrm{yr})^{-1}.
\]

At this very time, Milton L. Humason was beginning a program of red-shift
measurements at much greater distances, using the 100-in.\ reflector at
Mount Wilson to study the brightest galaxies in clusters. His first definite
result, a radial velocity \(cz=3779\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) for the galaxy
NGC 7619, was used by Hubble in his 1929
paper\hyperref[ch14-ref-13]{\textsuperscript{13}} to check the linearity of
the relation between \(cz\) and \(d_L\). Assuming this relation to be linear,
with slope \(500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), Hubble could deduce a
distance \(7.8\,\mathrm{Mpc}\) for NGC 7619, so that its apparent magnitude
\(m=11.8\) implied an absolute magnitude \(M=-17.65\). Hubble also
calculated the absolute magnitudes of the 18 galaxies used in his
determination of \(H_0\) (plus six additional members of our local group)
from their distances and apparent magnitudes, and found \(M\) to range from
\(-12.7\) to \(-17.7\). Since NGC 7619, as the brightest galaxy in a
cluster, is presumably brighter than average, this could be considered
reasonably good agreement, indicating that \(cz\) is indeed roughly
proportional to \(d_L\) out to \(z\simeq0.013\).

Continuing their collaboration, Hubble and
Humason\hyperref[ch14-ref-46]{\textsuperscript{46}} by 1931 had verified the
linearity of the relation between \(cz\) and \(d_L\) out to
\(20{,}000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) (\(z=0.067\)), and \(H_0\) was revised to
\(550\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\). The limit of the Mount Wilson
telescope was reached in 1936, when
Humason\hyperref[ch14-ref-47]{\textsuperscript{47}} recorded the radial
velocity of the Ursa Major II cluster as
\(42{,}000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) (\(z=0.14\)). By plotting
\(\log_{10}z\) against the apparent photographic magnitude (with absorption
and \(k\)-term corrections) of the fifth-brightest galaxy in 10 clusters,
ranging from Virgo to Ursa Major II,
Hubble\hyperref[ch14-ref-48]{\textsuperscript{48}} could verify that the
slope was close to \(1/5\), as would be expected [see
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.10}] if the red shift is a linear function of luminosity
distance out to \(z\simeq0.14\). A definite measurement of \(q_0\) had to
wait until completion of the 200-in.\ reflector at Mount Palomar.

Hubble also at this time\hyperref[ch14-ref-48]{\textsuperscript{48}} gave a
new estimate of \(H_0\), using 109 field galaxies, with \(cz\) ranging up to
\(19{,}070\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), as the fifth rung of the cosmic distance
ladder. The absolute magnitudes of these field galaxies were assumed to be
the same as the average, \(M=-15.18\), for 145 resolved galaxies (only 29 of
which belonged to the sample of 109 field galaxies), whose distances could
be determined from the apparent magnitude of their brightest stars. Plotting
\(m-M\) against \(\log_{10}cz\) gave
\(H_0=520\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\). A separate determination,
based directly on the brightest stars in 29 resolved field galaxies, gave
\(H_0=526\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\).

In 1950, Palomar was ready, and Hubble's program was taken up again. As we
saw in the last section, the first consequence of the observations at
Palomar was the recalibration by
Baade\hyperref[ch14-ref-27]{\textsuperscript{27}} of the Cepheid
period--luminosity relation. This immediately doubled the extragalactic
distance scale, and hence halved the value of Hubble's constant, to about
\(260\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\). In 1956, Humason, Mayall, and
Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-49]{\textsuperscript{49}} published an exhaustive
survey of the information then available on red shifts and distances. Under
the assumption that the brightest galaxies in clusters have the same
absolute magnitude as M31, the intercept of the plot of
\(m_V-k_V-A_V\) against \(\log_{10}cz\) for the brightest galaxies in 18
clusters (out to \(z=0.18\)) gave
\(H_0=180\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\). (A separate determination,
based on the average red shift of the Virgo cluster and the apparent
magnitudes of the brightest stars in the Virgo-cluster galaxy NGC 4321, gave
\(H_0=176\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\).) Also, with no evolutionary
correction, the curvature of the graph of \(m_V-k_V-A_V\) versus
\(\log_{10}z\) gave
\[
  q_0=3.7\pm0.8.
\]
A year later, Baum\hyperref[ch14-ref-40]{\textsuperscript{40}} reported a
study of eight clusters, using eight-color photometry to avoid the need for
a \(k\)-term correction. His result was
\[
  q_0=1\pm1.
\]
Next, Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-50]{\textsuperscript{50}} reexamined
Hubble's use of brightest stars at ``rung 4'' of the distance ladder, and in
1958 concluded that some of these ``brightest'' stars are H\,II regions,
which are 1.8 magnitudes brighter than the true brightest stars. The cosmic
distance scale expanded again, and \(H_0\) dropped to
\(75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\). Further analysis led
Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-51]{\textsuperscript{51}} to give a value for
\(H_0\) of \(98\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\) in 1961. Also,
preliminary calculations of galactic evolution led
Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-52]{\textsuperscript{52}} to estimate that
galaxies are decreasing in luminosity, with \(E_0\simeq-0.8H_0\), so that
Baum's value of \(q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}\) led to \(q_0=0.2\pm0.5\).

Meanwhile, red shifts were becoming available for radio galaxies. In 1960,
R. Minkowski discovered that one of these, 3C295, has red shift \(z=0.46\),
the largest yet known for any galaxy.%
\hyperref[ch14-ref-53]{\textsuperscript{53}}
(See Figure~\ref{fig:14.11}.)
Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-34]{\textsuperscript{34}} in 1968 included these
new red shifts, along with the data considered earlier by Humason, Mayall,
and Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-49]{\textsuperscript{49}} and
Baum,\hyperref[ch14-ref-40]{\textsuperscript{40}} in a study of 41
first-ranked cluster members. His data, transformed to the blue magnitude
system, could be well fit by the relation
\begin{equation}
  m_{B,\mathrm{corr}}
  \equiv
  m_B-k_B-A_B
  =
  5\log_{10}(cz)-6.06,
  \tag{14.6.15}\label{eq:14.6.15}
\end{equation}
where \(c=3\times10^5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). (See
Figure~\ref{fig:14.12}.) The dispersion of points about this curve was only
about \(\pm0.3\) magnitudes, indicating that these brightest galaxies really
do have a uniform absolute magnitude \(M_B\). The Hubble constant is thus
reliably given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.6.10} and \eqref{eq:14.6.15} as
\begin{equation}
  5\log_{10}
  \left[
    H_0\,(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}})
  \right]
  =
  M_B+31.06.
  \tag{14.6.16}\label{eq:14.6.16}
\end{equation}

Using globular clusters instead of brightest stars to fix the distance of
the Virgo cluster, Sandage\hyperref[ch14-ref-34]{\textsuperscript{34}}
estimated that brightest E galaxies have \(M_B=-21.68\), so that
\begin{equation}
  H_0
  =
  75.3_{-15}^{+19}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
  =
  \left[
    13.0_{-2.7}^{+3.7}\times10^9\,\mathrm{yr}
  \right]^{-1}.
  \tag{14.6.17}\label{eq:14.6.17}
\end{equation}
[The error quoted here includes uncertainties of \(\pm0.3\) magnitudes in
the apparent magnitude of the brightest globular cluster in M87;
\(\pm0.2\) magnitudes in the apparent magnitude of the first-ranked Virgo
galaxy NGC 4472; and \(\pm0.3\) magnitudes in fitting the data with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.15}.] The persistence of a straight-line fit out to
\(z=0.46\) indicates according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.10} that
\(q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}\) cannot be too different from unity.
Peach\hyperref[ch14-ref-54]{\textsuperscript{54}} gives a value, with
evolution neglected,
\begin{equation}
  q_0=1.5\pm0.4,
  \tag{14.6.18}\label{eq:14.6.18}
\end{equation}
while Sandage gives\hyperref[ch14-ref-55]{\textsuperscript{55}}
\begin{equation}
  q_0=1.2\pm0.4.
  \tag{14.6.19}\label{eq:14.6.19}
\end{equation}

What have we really learned from this forty-year program of astronomical
observations? There is little doubt that Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.15} is a good
fit to the data for small \(z\), so that \(H_0\) is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.16}. These results have hardly changed at all since
Hubble's work in 1936. What has changed dramatically is the distance scale,
which controls our estimates of \(M_B\) for first-ranked cluster galaxies,
and hence plays a crucial role in the determination of \(H_0\). A recent
survey by Sandage gives\hyperref[ch14-ref-44]{\textsuperscript{44}}
\[
  50\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
  \leq H_0\leq
  130\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}},
\]
or
\[
  20\times10^9\,\mathrm{yr}
  \geq H_0^{-1}\geq
  7.5\times10^9\,\mathrm{yr},
\]
and this probably represents a fair estimate of the range of possible error
in \(H_0\) due to uncertainties in the distance scale. Another change since
1936 is a tripling of the available range of red shifts. We can now be
reasonably confident that \(q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}\) is between \(1/2\) and
\(3/2\). However, the role of evolutionary and selection effects is still
very much in doubt. If
\(H_0=75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), and if galactic luminosities
increase at the rate \eqref{eq:14.6.13}, then the true deceleration
parameter \(q_0\) is related to the observed quantity
\(q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}\) by
\[
  q_0=q_0^{\mathrm{eff}}+0.5.
\]
This correction is highly uncertain; remember that it was believed to have
the opposite sign a few years ago! Thus we now know \(H_0\) to within a
factor of 2, and it seems likely that \(q_0>0\), indicating gravitational
braking, but about the precise value of \(q_0\) we know almost as little as
we did in 1931. (As this book goes to press, rumor has it that \(H_0\) is
going down again, perhaps even below
\(50\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\).)

In 1963 a discovery was made by Maarten
Schmidt,\hyperref[ch14-ref-56]{\textsuperscript{56}} which at first seemed
to offer hope of a tremendous improvement in our knowledge of the cosmic
scale factor. Since 1960, a number of radio sources had been identified with
\emph{quasi-stellar objects}, optical sources whose angular diameters are
too small to be resolved at Palomar. Schmidt discovered that one of them,
3C273, had a red shift \(z=0.158\), corresponding to a luminosity distance
(if \(H_0=75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\)) of
\(630\,\mathrm{Mpc}\). At this distance, its absolute luminosity would have
to be greater than that of a whole galaxy, even though its small angular
diameter (\(<0.5''\)) implied a size less than \(1500\,\mathrm{pc}\). From
1963 until the present, several hundred quasi-stellar objects have been
discovered,\hyperref[ch14-ref-57]{\textsuperscript{57}} of which a good
fraction have \(z>1\), and a few have \(z>2\). At the same time, the use of
lunar occultation and long-baseline radio interferometry, and the
observation of short-period time variations, made it clear that much of the
enormous energy output of these objects comes from regions much less than
\(1\,\mathrm{pc}\) in diameter. The discovery of the quasi-stellar objects
therefore revived interest in theories of gravitational collapse, already
discussed in Chapter 11. It also opened up the possibility of extending the
empirical relation between \(d_L\) and \(z\) out to really large distances
and red shifts, provided that some method could be found to determine the
absolute luminosities of the quasi-stellar objects.

Unfortunately, the plot of \(m_V\) versus \(\log z\) reveals no clear
correlation of apparent magnitude with red
shift.\hyperref[ch14-ref-57]{\textsuperscript{57},\,\textsuperscript{58}}
If quasi-stellar objects are indeed at cosmological distances (and about this
there remains some
doubt\hyperref[ch14-ref-59]{\textsuperscript{59},\,\textsuperscript{68}}),
then they must have an extremely wide spread in absolute luminosities. The
comparison of red shifts with apparent magnitudes will become
cosmologically interesting for quasi-stellar objects only when we learn how
to distinguish quasi-stellar objects of different absolute luminosity.

It is nevertheless an interesting question of principle to ask what could be
learned about \(k\) and \(a(t)\), if the luminosity distance could be exactly
determined as a function \(d_L(z)\) of red shift. It seems to be generally
believed that knowledge of \(d_L(z)\) would allow a unique determination of
\(k\) and \(a(t)\). However, this is not the
case.\hyperref[ch14-ref-60]{\textsuperscript{60}} The governing theoretical
equations here are \eqref{eq:14.3.1}, \eqref{eq:14.3.6}, and
\eqref{eq:14.4.14}. Equation~\eqref{eq:14.3.1} can be replaced with an
equivalent differential equation
\begin{equation}
  -\frac{1}{a(t_1)}\frac{\dd t_1}{\dd z}
  =
  (1-kr_1^2)^{-1/2}\frac{\dd r_1}{\dd z},
  \tag{14.6.20}\label{eq:14.6.20}
\end{equation}
with the initial condition that
\begin{equation}
  t_1=t_0
  \qquad\text{for \(r_1=0\).}
  \tag{14.6.21}\label{eq:14.6.21}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:14.3.6} and \eqref{eq:14.4.14} simply serve to
eliminate the unknowns \(a(t_1)\) and \(r_1\), so that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.20} becomes
\[
  (1+z)\frac{\dd t_1}{\dd z}
  =
  -\left[
    1-k\,a^{-2}(t_0)(1+z)^{-2}d_L^2(z)
  \right]^{-1/2}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd z}
  \left[
    (1+z)^{-1}d_L(z)
  \right].
\]
Thus \(t_1\) can be calculated as a function of \(z\) by a single
integration:
\[
  t_1(z)
  =
  t_0
  -
  \int_0^z
  \frac{1}{1+z'}
  \left[
    1-k\,a^{-2}(t_0)(1+z')^{-2}d_L^2(z')
  \right]^{-1/2}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd z'}
  \left[
    (1+z')^{-1}d_L(z')
  \right]
  \dd z',
\]
and the function \(a(t)\) can then be determined by solving the functional
equation
\begin{align}
  t
  =
  t_0
  -
  \int_0^{a(t_0)/a(t)-1}
  &\frac{1}{1+z}
  \left[
    1-k\,a^{-2}(t_0)(1+z)^{-2}d_L^2(z)
  \right]^{-1/2}
  \notag\\[-2pt]
  &{}\times
  \frac{\dd}{\dd z}
  \left[
    (1+z)^{-1}d_L(z)
  \right]
  \dd z.
  \tag{14.6.22}\label{eq:14.6.22}
\end{align}
Note that this procedure will give a solution for any assumed values of the
constants \(k\), \(a(t_0)\), or \(t_0\). Hence there is no way that
measurements of luminosity distances and red shifts can determine \(k\) or
\(a(t_0)\), unless we supplement the Robertson--Walker metric with
dynamical equations for \(a(t)\), as will be done in Chapter 15. This
curious ambiguity can also be observed in calculating the expansion of
\(d_L(z)\) in powers of \(z\): the first-order term depends on
\(\dot a(t_0)/a(t_0)\); the second-order term depends on
\(\dot a(t_0)/a(t_0)\) and \(\ddot a(t_0)/a(t_0)\); the third-order term
depends on these two quantities, \(a^{(3)}(t_0)/a(t_0)\), and
\(k/a^2(t_0)\); and terms of order \(z^N\), with \(N>3\), depend on
\(k/a^2(t_0)\) and the first \(N\) logarithmic derivatives of \(a(t)\) at
\(t_0\). Thus no measurement of any number of derivatives of \(d_L(z)\) can
ever allow us to determine \(k/a^2(t_0)\). However, once we assume values
for \(k\) and \(a(t_0)\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.22} will allow us to compute
\(a(t)\) as a function of \(t-t_0\) from the empirical relation between
\(d_L\) and \(z\).

In principle, we could also determine the form of the function \(a(t)\) by
observing a single spectral line for a long enough time. According to
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.6} and \eqref{eq:14.3.1}, the red shift of a comoving
source changes at a rate
\begin{align}
  \frac{\dd z}{\dd t_0}
  &=
  \frac{\dot a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}
  -
  \frac{a(t_0)\dot a(t_1)}{a^2(t_1)}
  \frac{\dd t_1}{\dd t_0}
  \notag\\
  &=
  \frac{\dot a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}
  -
  \frac{\dot a(t_1)}{a(t_1)}.
  \tag{14.6.23}\label{eq:14.6.23}
\end{align}
For \(z\ll1\), we can approximate \(t_0-t_1\) by the first term in the
series \eqref{eq:14.6.5}, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.23} reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{z}\frac{\dd z}{\dd t_0}
  =
  H_0+\frac{\ddot a(t_0)}{\dot a(t_0)}
  =
  -q_0H_0.
  \tag{14.6.24}\label{eq:14.6.24}
\end{equation}
It does not seem possible to measure this very slow change in red shift with
present techniques.\hyperref[ch14-ref-61]{\textsuperscript{61}}
