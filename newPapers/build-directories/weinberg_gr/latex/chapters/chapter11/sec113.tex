\subsection{Newtonian Stars: Polytropes and White Dwarfs}\label{sec:11.3}

Most of the stars in the sky are adequately described by Newtonian physics,
without taking account of general relativity. Such Newtonian stars deserve
some attention here, both because they serve as limiting cases for the more
exotic objects that interest the general relativist, and because they can
guide us in understanding the qualitative properties of these objects.

In Newtonian astrophysics the internal energy and pressure are very much less
than the rest-mass density,
\begin{equation}
  e\ll m_Nn,\qquad p\ll m_Nn,
  \tag{11.3.1}\label{eq:11.3.1}
\end{equation}
so that the total density is dominated by the density of rest mass,
\begin{equation}
  \rho\simeq m_Nn,
  \tag{11.3.2}\label{eq:11.3.2}
\end{equation}
and also
\[
  p\ll\rho,\qquad 4\pi r^3p\ll\mathcal{M}.
\]
In addition, the gravitational potential is everywhere small, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{2G\mathcal{M}}r\ll1.
  \tag{11.3.3}\label{eq:11.3.3}
\end{equation}
The fundamental equation \eqref{eq:11.1.13} thus simplifies to
\begin{equation}
  -r^2p'(r)=G\mathcal{M}(r)\rho(r),
  \tag{11.3.4}\label{eq:11.3.4}
\end{equation}
with \(\mathcal{M}(r)\) still defined by
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}(r)=\int_0^r4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,\dd r'.
  \tag{11.3.5}\label{eq:11.3.5}
\end{equation}
Dividing Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.4} by \(\rho(r)\) and differentiating allows us
to combine both Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.4} and \eqref{eq:11.3.5} in a single
second-order differential equation:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}
  \left[
    \frac{r^2}{\rho(r)}\frac{\dd p(r)}{\dd r}
  \right]
  =-4\pi Gr^2\rho(r).
  \tag{11.3.6}\label{eq:11.3.6}
\end{equation}
In order that \(\rho(0)\) be finite, it is necessary that \(p'(0)\) vanish.
Thus, given an equation of state \(p=p(\rho)\) (with \(\dd p/\dd\rho\ne0\)), we can
obtain \(\rho(r)\) by solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.6} with the initial
conditions that \(\rho(0)\) have some given value and that
\begin{equation}
  p'(0)=0.
  \tag{11.3.7}\label{eq:11.3.7}
\end{equation}
(Equation~\eqref{eq:11.3.7} also follows from the requirement that
\(\rho(r)\) be an analytic function of \(x,y,z\) at \(x=y=z=0\).)

We still need to prescribe an equation of state. It is often the case that the
internal energy density is proportional to the pressure, that is,
\begin{equation}
  e\equiv\rho-m_Nn=(\gamma-1)^{-1}p.
  \tag{11.3.8}\label{eq:11.3.8}
\end{equation}
(Here \((\gamma-1)^{-1}\) is just a constant proportionality coefficient;
\(\gamma\) will not be the ratio of specific heats unless \(e\) and \(p\) are
proportional to the temperature.) The condition of uniform entropy per nucleon
then reads
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  0
  &=
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac{\rho}{n}\right)
  +p\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac1n\right)\\
  &=
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac en\right)
  +p\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac1n\right)\\
  &=
  \frac1{\gamma-1}
  \left\{
    \gamma p\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac1n\right)
    +\frac1n\frac{\dd p}{\dd r}
  \right\},
  \end{aligned}
\]
and therefore \(p\propto n^\gamma\), or, since
\(\rho\simeq m_Nn\),
\begin{equation}
  p=K\rho^\gamma.
  \tag{11.3.9}\label{eq:11.3.9}
\end{equation}
The proportionality constant \(K\) depends on the entropy per nucleon and
chemical composition, but it does not depend on \(r\) or on \(\rho(0)\). Any
star for which the equation of state takes the form \eqref{eq:11.3.9} is
called a \emph{polytrope}.

The fundamental equation \eqref{eq:11.3.6} can, in the case of a polytrope,
be transformed into a convenient dimensionless form. Define a new independent
variable \(\xi\) by
\begin{equation}
  r
  =
  \left[\frac{K\gamma}{4\pi G(\gamma-1)}\right]^{1/2}
  \rho(0)^{(\gamma-2)/2}\xi,
  \tag{11.3.10}\label{eq:11.3.10}
\end{equation}
and a new dependent variable \(\theta\) by
\begin{equation}
  \rho=\rho(0)\theta^{1/(\gamma-1)},
  \qquad
  p=K\rho(0)^\gamma\theta^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}.
  \tag{11.3.11}\label{eq:11.3.11}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:11.3.6} then takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \frac1{\xi^2}\frac{\dd}{\dd\xi}
  \left(\xi^2\frac{\dd\theta}{\dd\xi}\right)
  +\theta^{1/(\gamma-1)}=0.
  \tag{11.3.12}\label{eq:11.3.12}
\end{equation}
The boundary conditions are
\begin{equation}
  \theta(0)=1,\qquad\theta'(0)=0.
  \tag{11.3.13}\label{eq:11.3.13}
\end{equation}
The function \(\theta(\xi)\) defined by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.12} and \eqref{eq:11.3.13} is known as the
Lane--Emden function\hyperref[ch11-ref-4]{\textsuperscript{4}} of index
\((\gamma-1)^{-1}\). For \(\xi\) near zero, Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.12} gives
\begin{equation}
  \theta(\xi)
  =
  1-\frac{\xi^2}{6}
  +\frac{\xi^4}{120(\gamma-1)}
  -\cdots.
  \tag{11.3.14}\label{eq:11.3.14}
\end{equation}
Also, it can be shown that for \(\gamma>6/5\), \(\theta(\xi)\) vanishes at
some finite \(\xi_1\):
\begin{equation}
  \theta(\xi_1)=0.
  \tag{11.3.15}\label{eq:11.3.15}
\end{equation}
The radius of the star is thus given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.10} as
\begin{equation}
  R
  =
  \left[\frac{K\gamma}{4\pi G(\gamma-1)}\right]^{1/2}
  \rho(0)^{(\gamma-2)/2}\xi_1.
  \tag{11.3.16}\label{eq:11.3.16}
\end{equation}
We can also use the Lane--Emden solutions to calculate the stellar mass:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  M
  &=
  \int_0^R4\pi r^2\rho(r)\,\dd r\\
  &=
  4\pi\rho(0)^{(3\gamma-4)/2}
  \left[\frac{K\gamma}{4\pi G(\gamma-1)}\right]^{3/2}
  \int_0^{\xi_1}\xi^2\theta^{1/(\gamma-1)}(\xi)\,\dd\xi\\
  &=
  4\pi\rho(0)^{(3\gamma-4)/2}
  \left[\frac{K\gamma}{4\pi G(\gamma-1)}\right]^{3/2}
  \xi_1^2\left|\theta'(\xi_1)\right|.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{11.3.17}\label{eq:11.3.17}
\end{equation}
By eliminating \(\rho(0)\) in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.16} and
\eqref{eq:11.3.17}, we obtain a relation between \(M\) and \(R\):
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  M={}&
  4\pi R^{(3\gamma-4)/(\gamma-2)}
  \left[\frac{K\gamma}{4\pi G(\gamma-1)}\right]^{-1/(\gamma-2)}
  \\
  &\times
  \xi_1^{-(3\gamma-4)/(\gamma-2)}
  \xi_1^2\left|\theta'(\xi_1)\right|.
  \end{split}
  \tag{11.3.18}\label{eq:11.3.18}
\end{equation}
Values\hyperref[ch11-ref-5]{\textsuperscript{5}} of the numerical constants
\(\xi_1\) and \(-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)\) are tabulated in
Table~\ref{tab:11.1}.

For Newtonian stars, \(M\) is dominated by the total rest mass \(Nm_N\), so
the nucleon number of the star is given to a good approximation by
\begin{equation}
  N\simeq\frac{M}{m_N}.
  \tag{11.3.19}\label{eq:11.3.19}
\end{equation}
We also want to know the internal energy \(E\equiv M-Nm_N\). For general
Newtonian stars this is given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.1.26},
\eqref{eq:11.1.29}, and \eqref{eq:11.1.30} as
\begin{equation}
  E=T+V,
  \tag{11.3.20}\label{eq:11.3.20}
\end{equation}
with the thermal energy \(T\) and the gravitational energy \(V\) given by
\begin{align}
  T&=\int_0^R4\pi r^2e(r)\,\dd r,
  \tag{11.3.21}\label{eq:11.3.21}\\
  V&=-\int_0^R4\pi rG\mathcal{M}(r)\rho(r)\,\dd r.
  \tag{11.3.22}\label{eq:11.3.22}
\end{align}
We now show that for polytropes, \(T\) and \(V\) are given by the remarkably
simple formulas\hyperref[ch11-ref-6]{\textsuperscript{6}}
\begin{align}
  T&=\frac1{5\gamma-6}\frac{GM^2}{R},
  \tag{11.3.23}\label{eq:11.3.23}\\
  V&=-\frac{3(\gamma-1)}{5\gamma-6}\frac{GM^2}{R},
  \tag{11.3.24}\label{eq:11.3.24}
\end{align}
so the total internal energy is
\begin{equation}
  E=-\frac{3\gamma-4}{5\gamma-6}\frac{GM^2}{R}.
  \tag{11.3.25}\label{eq:11.3.25}
\end{equation}
To prove the formula for \(V\), we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.4} to rewrite
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.22} as
\begin{equation}
  V
  =
  4\pi\int_0^Rr^3\frac{\dd p(r)}{\dd r}\,\dd r
  =
  -12\pi\int_0^Rr^2p(r)\,\dd r.
  \tag{11.3.26}\label{eq:11.3.26}
\end{equation}
Multiplying and dividing in the integrand by \(\rho(r)\), we have
\[
  V
  =
  -3\int_0^R\frac{p(r)}{\rho(r)}\,\dd\mathcal{M}(r)
  =
  3\int_0^R\mathcal{M}(r)\,
  \dd\left(\frac{p(r)}{\rho(r)}\right).
\]
(We assume here that \(\gamma>1\), so that \(p/\rho\) vanishes at \(R\).)
This can be evaluated by using the equation of state to calculate
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac{p(r)}{\rho(r)}\right)
  =
  \left(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\right)
  \frac{p'(r)}{\rho(r)}
  =
  -\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\right)
  \frac{G\mathcal{M}(r)}{r^2},
\]
so
\begin{equation}
  V
  =
  -3\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\right)
  \int_0^R\frac{G\mathcal{M}^2(r)}{r^2}\,\dd r.
  \tag{11.3.27}\label{eq:11.3.27}
\end{equation}
Since \(\dd r/r^2=-\dd(1/r)\), we can integrate by parts once again, and find
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  V
  &=
  3\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\right)
  \left\{
    \frac{GM^2}{R}
    -2\int_0^R4\pi rG\mathcal{M}(r)\rho(r)\,\dd r
  \right\}\\
  &=
  3\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\right)
  \left\{\frac{GM^2}{R}+2V\right\}.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Solving for \(V\) then gives the desired result \eqref{eq:11.3.24}. To
calculate \(T\) we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.8} in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.26}, which gives
\begin{equation}
  V=-3(\gamma-1)T.
  \tag{11.3.28}\label{eq:11.3.28}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:11.3.24} and \eqref{eq:11.3.28} then give the desired
result \eqref{eq:11.3.23}.

Inspection of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.17} and \eqref{eq:11.3.19} shows that the
nucleon number \(N\) behaves like \(\rho(0)^{(3\gamma-4)/2}\), whereas
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.25}, \eqref{eq:11.3.16}, and \eqref{eq:11.3.17} show
that the internal energy \(E\) behaves like \(\rho(0)^{(5\gamma-6)/2}\).
Thus \(\partial N/\partial\rho(0)\) and \(\partial E/\partial\rho(0)\) can
never vanish together. Theorem~1 of the last section tells us then that each
polytrope is either stable or unstable for all \(\rho(0)\), depending on the
value of \(\gamma\). But which?

In order to answer this question, we turn to Theorem~2 of the last section,
which tells us that the star will be stable if and only if \(E\) is a minimum
with respect to all variations in \(\rho(r)\) that leave \(N\) (and the
equation of state) unchanged. It is intuitively likely that the first
instability to occur will correspond to a uniform implosion of the whole star,
and since we are only trying to answer the yes-or-no question about stability
with respect to this mode, it will hopefully be sufficient for us to consider
only trial configurations with \(\rho(r)\) constant. In any such
configuration, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.19}, \eqref{eq:11.3.21},
\eqref{eq:11.3.22}, and \eqref{eq:11.3.8} give
\begin{align}
  N&=\frac{4\pi}{3m_N}\rho R^3,
  \tag{11.3.29}\label{eq:11.3.29}\\
  T&=\frac{4\pi}{3}(\gamma-1)^{-1}K\rho^\gamma R^3,
  \tag{11.3.30}\label{eq:11.3.30}\\
  V&=-\frac{16\pi^2}{15}G\rho^2R^5,
  \tag{11.3.31}\label{eq:11.3.31}
\end{align}
so, eliminating \(R\),
\begin{equation}
  E=T+V=a\rho^{\gamma-1}-b\rho^{1/3},
  \tag{11.3.32}\label{eq:11.3.32}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
  a&=\frac{KM}{\gamma-1},
  \tag{11.3.33}\label{eq:11.3.33}\\
  b&=\frac35\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)^{1/3}GM^{5/3}.
  \tag{11.3.34}\label{eq:11.3.34}
\end{align}
For \(\gamma>4/3\), \(E\) has a \emph{minimum} at
\begin{equation}
  \rho
  =
  \left[\frac{b}{3a(\gamma-1)}\right]^{1/(\gamma-4/3)}
  =
  \left[
    \frac{M^{2/3}G(4\pi/3)^{1/3}}{5K}
  \right]^{1/(\gamma-4/3)},
  \tag{11.3.35}\label{eq:11.3.35}
\end{equation}
corresponding to a configuration of \emph{stable} equilibrium. For
\(\gamma=4/3\), \(E\) is stationary with respect to \(\rho\) only if it
vanishes everywhere, which requires that \(a=b\), or
\begin{equation}
  M
  =
  \left(\frac{5K}{G}\right)^{3/2}
  \left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)^{-1/2}.
  \tag{11.3.36}\label{eq:11.3.36}
\end{equation}
For \(\gamma<4/3\), \(E\) has a \emph{maximum} at the point
\eqref{eq:11.3.35}, corresponding to a state of \emph{unstable} equilibrium.

Incidentally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.35} gives an estimate for the mass
\[
  M\simeq
  \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho^{(3\gamma-4)/2}
  \left(\frac{15K}{4\pi G}\right)^{3/2},
\]
which may be compared with the exact result \eqref{eq:11.3.17}. The ratio of
these two expressions is
\[
  \frac{M(\mathrm{variational})}{M(\mathrm{exact})}
  =
  \frac{[15(\gamma-1)/\gamma]^{3/2}}
  {3\xi_1^2|\theta'(\xi_1)|}.
\]
For \(\gamma=5/3\) this ratio is \(1.8\); for \(\gamma=4/3\) it is \(1.2\).
Not only does the variational method give the correct dependence of \(M\) on
\(\rho\) (including the fact that for \(\gamma=4/3\), \(M\) is independent
of \(\rho\), and \(E\) vanishes), but it even provides a fair approximation
to the exact numerical results. We can accept with confidence its prediction
that a polytrope is stable or unstable according to whether \(\gamma>4/3\) or
\(\gamma<4/3\).\hyperref[ch11-ref-7]{\textsuperscript{7}}

The variational approach also provides a simple method for estimating the
oscillation frequency for dilation and contraction of the star.
Equations~\eqref{eq:11.3.29}--\eqref{eq:11.3.31} show that for fixed \(N\),
\[
  T\propto R^{3(1-\gamma)},\qquad V\propto R^{-1}.
\]
We can use Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.23} and \eqref{eq:11.3.24} to fix the correct
values of \(T\) and \(V\) at the equilibrium radius (which we shall now write
as \(R_{\mathrm{eq}}\), to distinguish it from the instantaneous radius \(R\)
of an oscillating configuration). This gives then
\[
  E
  =
  \frac1{5\gamma-6}
  \frac{GM^2}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}^{4-3\gamma}}
  R^{3(1-\gamma)}
  -\frac{3(\gamma-1)}{5\gamma-6}GM^2R^{-1}.
\]
For \(\gamma>4/3\), this has a minimum at \(R=R_{\mathrm{eq}}\), as it
should. For \(R\) near \(R_{\mathrm{eq}}\), \(E\) behaves like
\[
  E\longrightarrow
  E_{\mathrm{eq}}
  +\frac{3(\gamma-1)(3\gamma-4)}{2(5\gamma-6)}
  \frac{GM^2}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}^3}
  (R-R_{\mathrm{eq}})^2.
\]
The uniform dilation of a sphere with uniform density will give it a kinetic
energy
\[
  U=\frac3{10}M\dot R^2,
\]
so the condition of energy conservation, that \(U+E\) be constant, leads to
modes with
\[
  R-R_{\mathrm{eq}}\propto\sin\omega_0t,
\]
where
\begin{equation}
  \omega_0
  \simeq
  \left[
    \frac{5(\gamma-1)(3\gamma-4)}{5\gamma-6}
    \frac{GM}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}^3}
  \right]^{1/2}.
  \tag{11.3.37}\label{eq:11.3.37}
\end{equation}
Finally, we note that a uniform sphere rotating with angular velocity
\(\Omega\) will have kinetic energy
\[
  U=\frac15MR_{\mathrm{eq}}^2\Omega^2.
\]
This must be less than the binding energy \(-E\), so the maximum angular
velocity with which a star can rotate is of order
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_{\max}
  \simeq
  \left[
    \frac{5(3\gamma-4)}{5\gamma-6}
    \frac{GM}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}^3}
  \right]^{1/2}
  \simeq\frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.
  \tag{11.3.38}\label{eq:11.3.38}
\end{equation}
Of course a star rotating this fast will no longer be a sphere, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.38} only gives an order-of-magnitude estimate of the actual
maximum rotation frequency.

Now let us apply what we have learned to the stars known as \emph{white
dwarfs}. Imagine an aged star that exhausts its nuclear fuel and begins to
cool and contract. When the temperature is sufficiently low (see below for
just how low), the electrons will be frozen into the lowest available energy
levels. The Pauli principle tells us that there will be two electrons in each
level (because of the two spin states available), and there are
\(4\pi k^2(2\pi\hbar)^{-3}\dd k\) levels per unit volume with momenta between
\(k\) and \(k+\dd k\), so the number of electrons per unit volume will be related
to the maximum momentum \(k_F\) by
\begin{equation}
  n
  =
  \frac{8\pi}{(2\pi\hbar)^3}\int_0^{k_F}k^2\,\dd k
  =
  \frac{k_F^3}{3\pi^2\hbar^3}.
  \tag{11.3.39}\label{eq:11.3.39}
\end{equation}
The mass density is
\begin{equation}
  \rho=nm_N\mu,
  \tag{11.3.40}\label{eq:11.3.40}
\end{equation}
where \(\mu\) is the number of nucleons per electron; \(\mu\simeq2\) for stars
that have used up their hydrogen. This gives
\begin{equation}
  k_F=\hbar\left(\frac{3\pi^2\rho}{m_N\mu}\right)^{1/3}.
  \tag{11.3.41}\label{eq:11.3.41}
\end{equation}
The condition that the temperature is negligible is
\[
  kT\ll\left(k_F^2+m_e^2\right)^{1/2}-m_e.
\]
The kinetic energy density and pressure of these electrons are
\begin{align}
  e
  &=
  \frac{8\pi}{(2\pi\hbar)^3}
  \int_0^{k_F}
  \left[\left(k^2+m_e^2\right)^{1/2}-m_e\right]k^2\,\dd k,
  \tag{11.3.42}\label{eq:11.3.42}\\
  p
  &=
  \frac{8\pi}{3(2\pi\hbar)^3}
  \int_0^{k_F}
  \frac{k^2}{(k^2+m_e^2)^{1/2}}\,k^2\,\dd k.
  \tag{11.3.43}\label{eq:11.3.43}
\end{align}
[See Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.8.4}.] The equation of state can be made explicit by
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.41} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.43}.

The equation of state here is not simple, but it reduces to a polytrope in two
extreme cases, distinguished by the criteria \(\rho\ll\rho_c\) or
\(\rho\gg\rho_c\), where \(\rho_c\) is the critical density at which \(k_F\)
becomes equal to \(m_e\) (in c.g.s.\ units):
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  =
  \frac{m_N\mu m_e^3c^3}{3\pi^2\hbar^3}
  =
  0.97\times10^6\mu\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
  \tag{11.3.44}\label{eq:11.3.44}
\end{equation}

\emph{(A) \(\rho\ll\rho_c\).} In this case \(k_F\ll m_e\), so
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.42} and \eqref{eq:11.3.43} give
\[
  e=\frac32p,
  \qquad
  p
  =
  \frac{8\pi k_F^5}{15m_e(2\pi\hbar)^3}
  =
  \frac{\hbar^2}{15m_e\pi^2}
  \left(\frac{3\pi^2\rho}{m_N\mu}\right)^{5/3}.
\]
This is a polytrope, with
\begin{equation}
  \gamma=\frac53,\qquad
  K
  =
  \frac{\hbar^2}{15m_e\pi^2}
  \left(\frac{3\pi^2}{m_N\mu}\right)^{5/3}.
  \tag{11.3.45}\label{eq:11.3.45}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:11.3.17} then gives a mass (in c.g.s.\ units)
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  M
  &=
  \frac12\left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\right)^{1/2}(2.71406)
  \left(
    \frac{\hbar^{3/2}c^{3/2}}
    {m_N^2\mu^2G^{3/2}}
  \right)
  \left(\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_c}\right)^{1/2}\\
  &=
  2.79\mu^{-2}
  \left(\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_c}\right)^{1/2}M_\odot.
  \end{split}
  \tag{11.3.46}\label{eq:11.3.46}
\end{equation}
whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.16} gives a radius (in c.g.s.\ units)
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  R
  &=
  \left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\right)^{1/2}(3.65375)
  \left(
    \frac{\hbar^{3/2}}
    {c^{1/2}G^{1/2}m_em_N\mu}
  \right)
  \left(\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_c}\right)^{-1/6}\\
  &=
  2.0\times10^4\mu^{-1}
  \left(\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_c}\right)^{-1/6}\mathrm{km}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{11.3.47}\label{eq:11.3.47}
\end{equation}

\emph{(B) \(\rho\gg\rho_c\).} In this case \(k_F\gg m_e\), so
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.42} and \eqref{eq:11.3.43} give
\[
  e=3p,
  \qquad
  p
  =
  \frac{8\pi k_F^4}{12(2\pi\hbar)^3}
  =
  \frac{\hbar}{12\pi^2}
  \left(\frac{3\pi^2\rho}{m_N\mu}\right)^{4/3}.
\]
This is a polytrope, with
\begin{equation}
  \gamma=\frac43,\qquad
  K
  =
  \frac{\hbar}{12\pi^2}
  \left(\frac{3\pi^2}{m_N\mu}\right)^{4/3}.
  \tag{11.3.48}\label{eq:11.3.48}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:11.3.17} then gives a unique mass (in c.g.s.\ units)
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  M
  &=
  \frac12(3\pi)^{1/2}(2.01824)
  \left(
    \frac{\hbar^{3/2}c^{3/2}}
    {G^{3/2}m_N^2\mu^2}
  \right)\\
  &=
  5.87\mu^{-2}M_\odot,
  \end{split}
  \tag{11.3.49}\label{eq:11.3.49}
\end{equation}
whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.16} gives the radius (in c.g.s.\ units)
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  R
  &=
  \frac12(3\pi)^{1/2}(6.89685)
  \left(
    \frac{\hbar^{3/2}}
    {c^{1/2}G^{1/2}m_em_N\mu}
  \right)
  \left(\frac{\rho_c}{\rho(0)}\right)^{1/3}\\
  &=
  5.3\times10^4\mu^{-1}
  \left(\frac{\rho_c}{\rho(0)}\right)^{1/3}\mathrm{km}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{11.3.50}\label{eq:11.3.50}
\end{equation}

We note that \(\gamma>4/3\) for \(\rho(0)\ll\rho_c\), so the least massive
white dwarfs are definitely stable. We also see that \(M\) appears to grow
monotonically with increasing central density, reaching a maximum
\eqref{eq:11.3.49} when \(\rho(0)\to\infty\), so there is no point where the
star can become unstable. Our tentative conclusion is that stable white dwarfs
can exist for any mass less than \eqref{eq:11.3.49}. This maximum mass is
known as the Chandrasekhar
limit.\hyperref[ch11-ref-8]{\textsuperscript{8}}

Actually, matters are not so simple. When \(k_F\simeq5m_e\), it becomes
energetically favorable for electrons to be captured by nuclei, turning
protons into neutrons, and producing neutrinos that escape forthwith. The
effect is to increase the number \(\mu\) of nucleons per electron, and
according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.46} this will reduce the mass \(M\) for a
given central density. We therefore expect \(M\) to increase toward the
Chandrasekhar limit until \(\rho(0)\simeq5^3\rho_c\) [see
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.41} and \eqref{eq:11.3.44}], where \(M\) reaches a
maximum and then begins to decrease. Detailed
calculations\hyperref[ch11-ref-9]{\textsuperscript{9}} show that the maximum
mass is \(1.2M_\odot\), nearly equal to the Chandrasekhar limit, which is
\(1.26M_\odot\) for \(\mu=56/26\). The radius of a star with this maximum mass
is about \(4\times10^3\ \mathrm{km}\). Theorem~2 of
Section~\ref{sec:11.2} suggests that this maximum is a point of transition
from stability to instability, so stable iron white dwarfs can exist only for
\(M<1.2M_\odot\).

To the student of general relativity, the most interesting parameter
characterizing a white dwarf is the absolute value \(GM/R\) of the
gravitational potential at its surface. For \(\rho(0)\ll\rho_c\), this is
given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.46} and \eqref{eq:11.3.47} as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{GM}{R}
  =
  \frac12\left(\frac{2.71406}{3.65375}\right)
  \mu^{-1}\left(\frac{m_e}{m_N}\right)
  \left(\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_c}\right)^{2/3},
  \tag{11.3.51}\label{eq:11.3.51}
\end{equation}
whereas for \(\rho(0)\gg\rho_c\), it is given by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.49} and \eqref{eq:11.3.50} as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{GM}{R}
  =
  \left(\frac{2.01824}{6.89685}\right)
  \mu^{-1}\left(\frac{m_e}{m_N}\right)
  \left(\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_c}\right)^{1/3}.
  \tag{11.3.52}\label{eq:11.3.52}
\end{equation}
We see that \(GM/R\) is always going to be quite small, because
\(m_e/m_N=5.4\times10^{-4}\). Thus general relativity plays no important role
in the structure of white dwarfs. The quantity \(GM/R\) increases with
increasing central density, so it is largest at the maximum mass
\(1.2M_\odot\), where it takes the value \(4\times10^{-4}\). Our old friend
40~Eridani~B had \(GM/R\simeq6\times10^{-5}\) (see Section~3.5), so it is
not going to be possible to improve astronomical redshift experiments very
dramatically by finding white dwarfs with much larger redshifts.
