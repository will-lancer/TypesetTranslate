\subsection{Generation of Gravitational Waves}\label{sec:10.4}

We wish to calculate the energy emitted in the form of gravitational
radiation by a system whose energy-momentum tensor can be expressed as a
Fourier integral,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =
  \int_0^\infty \dd\omega\,
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)e^{-\ii\omega t}
  +\text{c.c.},
  \tag{10.4.1}\label{eq:10.4.1}
\end{equation}
or as a sum of Fourier components,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =
  \sum_\omega e^{-\ii\omega t}T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  +\text{c.c.}
  \tag{10.4.2}\label{eq:10.4.2}
\end{equation}
(Here ``\( +\text{c.c.}\)'' means ``plus the complex conjugate of the
preceding term.'') We first do the calculation for a single Fourier
component,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)e^{-\ii\omega t}
  +\text{c.c.},
  \tag{10.4.3}\label{eq:10.4.3}
\end{equation}
and will then return to the more general systems described by
\eqref{eq:10.4.1} and \eqref{eq:10.4.2}.

From \eqref{eq:10.1.11} we find that the field emitted by the source
\eqref{eq:10.4.3} is
\begin{equation}
  h_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =
  4G\int\frac{\dd^3x'}{\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert}
  S_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x}',\omega)
  \exp\!\left\{-\ii\omega t
  +\ii\omega\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert\right\}
  +\text{c.c.},
  \tag{10.4.4}\label{eq:10.4.4}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  \equiv
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  -\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}T^\lambda{}_\lambda(\mathbf{x},\omega).
  \tag{10.4.5}\label{eq:10.4.5}
\end{equation}
Suppose that we observe this radiation in the \emph{wave zone}, that is, at
distances \(r\equiv\lvert\mathbf{x}\rvert\) much larger than the dimension
\(R=\lvert\mathbf{x}'\rvert_{\max}\) of the source, and also much larger
than \(\omega R^2\) and \(1/\omega\). Then the denominator
\(\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert\) can be replaced with \(r\), while
in the exponent we may approximate
\[
  \lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert
  \simeq r-\mathbf{x}'\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{x}},
  \qquad
  \widehat{\mathbf{x}}\equiv\frac{\mathbf{x}}{r},
\]
and the field becomes
\begin{equation}
  h_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =
  \frac{4G}{r}e^{\ii\omega r-\ii\omega t}
  \int \dd^3x'\,
  S_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x}',\omega)
  e^{-\ii\omega\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{x}'}
  +\text{c.c.}
  \tag{10.4.6}\label{eq:10.4.6}
\end{equation}
Since \(r\omega\) is assumed large, this looks just like a plane wave,
\begin{equation}
  h_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =
  e_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  \exp(\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda)
  +\text{c.c.},
  \tag{10.4.7}\label{eq:10.4.7}
\end{equation}
with ``wave vector'' and ``polarization tensor'' given by
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{k}\equiv\omega\widehat{\mathbf{x}},
  \qquad
  k^0\equiv\omega,
  \tag{10.4.8}\label{eq:10.4.8}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  e_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  \equiv
  \frac{4G}{r}\int \dd^3x'\,
  S_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x}',\omega)e^{-\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}'}.
  \tag{10.4.9}\label{eq:10.4.9}
\end{equation}
It will be convenient to write \(e_{\mu\nu}\) explicitly in terms of the
Fourier transform of \(T_{\mu\nu}\):
\begin{equation}
  e_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  =
  \frac{4G}{r}
  \left[
    T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
    -\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}T^\lambda{}_\lambda(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  \right],
  \tag{10.4.10}\label{eq:10.4.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  \equiv
  \int \dd^3x'\,
  T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x}',\omega)e^{-\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}'}.
  \tag{10.4.11}\label{eq:10.4.11}
\end{equation}

The conservation equation for \(T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)\) is
\[
  \partial_\mu T^\mu{}_\nu(\mathbf{x},t)=0.
\]
Applying this to \eqref{eq:10.4.3} gives
\[
  \partial_iT^i{}_\nu(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  -\ii\omega T^0{}_\nu(\mathbf{x},\omega)=0.
\]
Multiplying by \(e^{-\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\) and integrating over
\(\mathbf{x}\), we find that \(T_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\) is subject to
the algebraic relations
\begin{equation}
  k_\mu T^\mu{}_\nu(\mathbf{k},\omega)=0,
  \tag{10.4.12}\label{eq:10.4.12}
\end{equation}
where \(k^\mu\) is the vector given by \eqref{eq:10.4.8}. This, incidentally,
verifies that \eqref{eq:10.4.10} obeys the harmonic coordinate condition
\eqref{eq:10.2.3}.

Now let us calculate the power per unit solid angle emitted in a direction
\(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\). Since \(r\gg1/\omega\), we can use for the energy
flux vector the value \(\langle t^{i0}\rangle\) obtained by averaging over
spacetime dimensions large compared with \(1/\omega\), so that the power per
solid angle is
\[
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  r^2\widehat x_i\left\langle t^{i0}\right\rangle.
\]
We use for \(\langle t^{\mu\nu}\rangle\) the value \eqref{eq:10.3.5}, so
this gives
\[
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  \frac{r^2(\mathbf{k}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{x}})k^0}{16\pi G}
  \left[
    e^{\lambda\nu*}(\mathbf{x},\omega)e_{\lambda\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
    -\frac12\left|e^\lambda{}_\lambda(\mathbf{x},\omega)\right|^2
  \right].
\]
Inserting the values \eqref{eq:10.4.8} and \eqref{eq:10.4.10} for
\(k^\mu\) and \(e_{\lambda\nu}\), we find that the \(r^2\) factors cancel,
and
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  \frac{G\omega^2}{\pi}
  \left[
    T^{\lambda\nu*}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
    T_{\lambda\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
    -\frac12
    \left|T^\lambda{}_\lambda(\mathbf{k},\omega)\right|^2
  \right].
  \tag{10.4.13}\label{eq:10.4.13}
\end{equation}
The problem is thus solved once we have calculated the Fourier transform
\eqref{eq:10.4.11}.

It will be convenient to express this result in terms of the purely spacelike
components of \(T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\). From
\eqref{eq:10.4.12} we have
\[
  T_{0\ii}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  =-\widehat k^jT_{ji}(\mathbf{k},\omega),
  \qquad
  T_{00}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  =\widehat k^i\widehat k^jT_{ji}(\mathbf{k},\omega),
\]
where
\(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}\equiv\mathbf{k}/\omega
=\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\). Using this in \eqref{eq:10.4.13} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  \frac{G\omega^2}{\pi}
  \Lambda_{ij,lm}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}})
  T^{ij*}(\mathbf{k},\omega)T^{lm}(\mathbf{k},\omega),
  \tag{10.4.14}\label{eq:10.4.14}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
  \Lambda_{ij,lm}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}})
  \equiv{}&
  \delta_{il}\delta_{jm}
  -2\widehat k_j\widehat k_m\delta_{il}
  +\frac12\widehat k_i\widehat k_j\widehat k_l\widehat k_m
  -\frac12\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}
  \notag\\
  &+\frac12\delta_{ij}\widehat k_l\widehat k_m
  +\frac12\delta_{lm}\widehat k_i\widehat k_j.
  \tag{10.4.15}\label{eq:10.4.15}
\end{align}

If the energy-momentum tensor is a \emph{sum} of individual Fourier
components as in \eqref{eq:10.4.2}, then the field \(h_{\mu\nu}\) in the
wave zone will look like a sum of the plane waves \eqref{eq:10.4.7}. The
gravitational energy-momentum tensor will then be given by a double sum over
these Fourier components, but all cross-terms drop out when we average over a
time interval long compared with the longest ``beat period,'' that is, the
reciprocal of the shortest frequency difference. The power is thus given by
a sum of terms like \eqref{eq:10.4.14}, one for each frequency in the
source.

Suppose, on the other hand, that the energy-momentum tensor is a Fourier
\emph{integral}, as in \eqref{eq:10.4.1}. Then \(h_{\mu\nu}\) in the wave
zone will look like an integral over \(\omega\) of the individual plane
waves \eqref{eq:10.4.7}, and the gravitational energy-momentum tensor will
be given by a double integral \(\iint \dd\omega\,\dd\omega'\) of products of
these terms. The integrand again has time dependence
\(\exp[-\ii(\omega-\omega')t]\), but now there is no ``longest beat
period,'' so instead of computing the average power we calculate the total
energy emitted. This is given by integrating the power over all time, and
the effect is to replace the factors \(e^{-\ii\omega t}e^{\ii\omega't}\) in
the double integral for the power with
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
  \exp[-\ii(\omega-\omega')t]\,\dd t
  =
  2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega').
\]
The energy per solid angle emitted in a direction
\(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}\) is thus a single integral:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd E}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  2G\int_0^\infty \dd\omega\,\omega^2
  \left[
    T^{\lambda\nu*}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
    T_{\lambda\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
    -\frac12\left|T^\lambda{}_\lambda(\mathbf{k},\omega)\right|^2
  \right],
  \tag{10.4.16}\label{eq:10.4.16}
\end{equation}
or, in terms of the space-space components,
\[
  \frac{\dd E}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  2G\Lambda_{ij,lm}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}})
  \int_0^\infty \dd\omega\,\omega^2
  T^{ij*}(\mathbf{k},\omega)T^{lm}(\mathbf{k},\omega).
\]

As an example, consider a system of free particles \(n\) that initially
move at constant velocity \(\mathbf{v}_n\), collide at the origin at
\(t=0\), and then move off again at velocities
\(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_n\). The energy-momentum tensor is then
\begin{align}
  T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)
  ={}&
  \sum_n\frac{P_n^\mu P_n^\nu}{E_n}
  \delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}_nt)\theta(-t)
  \notag\\
  &+
  \sum_n\frac{\widetilde P_n^\mu\widetilde P_n^\nu}{\widetilde E_n}
  \delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_nt)\theta(t),
  \tag{10.4.17}\label{eq:10.4.17}
\end{align}
where \(P_n^0=E_n=m_n(1-\mathbf{v}_n^2)^{-1/2}\) and
\(\mathbf{P}_n=E_n\mathbf{v}_n\) are the energy and momentum of the \(n\)th
incoming particle, \(\widetilde P_n^0=\widetilde E_n\) and
\(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_n\) are the corresponding quantities for the
outgoing particles, and \(\theta\) is the step function
\begin{equation}
  \theta(s)=
  \begin{cases}
  +1,&s>0,\\
  0,&s<0.
  \end{cases}
  \tag{10.4.18}\label{eq:10.4.18}
\end{equation}
The functions \(\theta\) and \(\delta^3\) have the well-known integral
representations
\begin{equation}
  \theta(s)
  =
  \frac{1}{2\pi\ii}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
  \frac{e^{+\ii\omega s}}{\omega-\ii\epsilon}\,\dd\omega,
  \qquad
  \epsilon\to0+,
  \tag{10.4.19}\label{eq:10.4.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta^3(\mathbf{x})
  =
  \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\,e^{\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}.
  \tag{10.4.20}\label{eq:10.4.20}
\end{equation}
(To prove \eqref{eq:10.4.19}, note that the contour can be closed with a
large semicircle in the lower or upper half-plane, according to whether
\(s<0\) or \(s>0\). To prove \eqref{eq:10.4.20}, take the Fourier transform
of both sides.)

We see then that \(T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},t)\) is of the form
\eqref{eq:10.4.1}, with
\begin{align*}
  T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
  =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^4\ii}\Biggl[
  &\sum_n\frac{P_n^\mu P_n^\nu}{E_n}
  \int \dd^3k\,
  \frac{e^{\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}}
       {\omega-\mathbf{v}_n\cdot\mathbf{k}-\ii\epsilon}\\
  &-\sum_n\frac{\widetilde P_n^\mu\widetilde P_n^\nu}{\widetilde E_n}
  \int \dd^3k\,
  \frac{e^{\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}}
       {\omega-\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_n\cdot\mathbf{k}
        +\ii\epsilon}
  \Biggr],
\end{align*}
and the Fourier transform \eqref{eq:10.4.11} is
\[
  T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  =
  \frac{1}{2\pi\ii}
  \left[
    \sum_n\frac{P_n^\mu P_n^\nu}
    {E_n(\omega-\mathbf{v}_n\cdot\mathbf{k}-\ii\epsilon)}
    -
    \sum_n\frac{\widetilde P_n^\mu\widetilde P_n^\nu}
    {\widetilde E_n
      (\omega-\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_n\cdot\mathbf{k}+\ii\epsilon)}
  \right].
\]
We can now drop the \(\pm\ii\epsilon\) in the denominators, for
\(\omega-\mathbf{v}_n\cdot\mathbf{k}\) cannot vanish if
\(\omega=\lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert\) and
\(\lvert\mathbf{v}_n\rvert<1\). (For the case of particles traveling at
the speed of light, see below.) Also,
\(E_n(\mathbf{v}_n\cdot\mathbf{k}-\omega)
=P_n^\lambda k_\lambda\equiv(P_n\cdot k)\), so we can write
\begin{equation}
  T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  =
  \frac{1}{2\pi\ii}
  \sum_N
  \frac{P_N^\mu P_N^\nu\eta_N}{P_N\cdot k},
  \tag{10.4.21}\label{eq:10.4.21}
\end{equation}
where \(N\) runs over particles in both the initial and the final states,
the sign factor \(\eta_N\) being
\[
  \eta_N=
  \begin{cases}
  +1,&N\text{ in final state},\\
  -1,&N\text{ in initial state}.
  \end{cases}
\]
We note that \eqref{eq:10.4.12} is satisfied, because
\[
  k_\mu T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
  =
  \frac{1}{2\pi\ii}\sum_NP_N^\nu\eta_N,
\]
and this must vanish because \(\sum_NP_N^\nu\eta_N\) is simply the change
in the total \(P^\nu\), which is conserved.

The gravitational energy per solid angle and per unit frequency interval
emitted at frequency \(\omega\) and direction \(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}\) is
now given by \eqref{eq:10.4.16} as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd E}{\dd\Omega\,\dd\omega}
  =
  \frac{G\omega^2}{2\pi^2}
  \sum_{N,M}
  \frac{\eta_N\eta_M}
       {(P_N\cdot k)(P_M\cdot k)}
  \left[
    (P_N\cdot P_M)^2-\frac12m_N^2m_M^2
  \right].
  \tag{10.4.22}\label{eq:10.4.22}
\end{equation}
If we tried to compute the total emitted energy by integrating \(\omega\)
from zero to infinity, we would get a result that diverges like
\(\int_0^\infty \dd\omega\). This is just due to our approximation that the
collision occurs instantaneously; actually it must take up some finite time
\(\Delta t\), and the \(\omega\)-integral will be cut off at \(\omega\) of
order \(1/\Delta t\). Note that if none of the momenta \(P_N^\mu\) are
changed by the collision, then the contributions of the incoming and outgoing
particles in \eqref{eq:10.4.21} will cancel, and so
\(T^{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\) will vanish. Gravitational radiation is
only emitted when the particles actually undergo accelerations.

Note also that \eqref{eq:10.4.22} seems to become infinite if one of the
particles participating in the reaction (say, \(N=1\)) has zero mass, and
has momentum approaching a direction parallel to \(\mathbf{k}\), since then
\(P_1\cdot k=E_1\omega
(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_1\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{k}}-1)\to0\). However, this
singularity is spurious, for when \(P_1\) becomes parallel to \(k\) we can
treat \((P_1\cdot P_M)\) in \eqref{eq:10.4.22} as proportional for all
\(M\ne1\) to \((k\cdot P_M)\), so the singular part of
\eqref{eq:10.4.22} is
\[
  \frac{G\omega^2}{\pi^2}
  \frac{\eta_1}{P_1\cdot k}
  \sum_{M\ne1}\frac{\eta_M}{P_M\cdot k}(P_1\cdot P_M)^2
  \propto
  \sum_{M\ne1}\eta_M(P_1\cdot P_M).
\]
We have already remarked that \(\sum_M\eta_MP_M^\mu\) must vanish when the
sum is extended over all particles, so the right-hand side is simply
\(-\eta_1P_1^2\), and this vanishes because particle 1 is assumed to have
zero mass. Thus no difficulty is encountered in applying
\eqref{eq:10.4.22} to collisions involving photons, neutrinos, or even (to
run ahead of ourselves a bit) gravitons.

The total energy per unit frequency interval emitted as gravitational
radiation in a collision is obtained by integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.4.22}
over the directions of \(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}\). We then find
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd E}{\dd\omega}
  =
  \frac{G}{2\pi}
  \sum_{N,M}\eta_N\eta_Mm_Nm_M
  \frac{1+\beta_{NM}^2}
       {\beta_{NM}(1-\beta_{NM}^2)^{1/2}}
  \ln\!\left(\frac{1+\beta_{NM}}{1-\beta_{NM}}\right),
  \tag{10.4.23}\label{eq:10.4.23}
\end{equation}
with \(\beta_{NM}\) the relative speed of particles \(N\) and \(M\):
\[
  \beta_{NM}
  \equiv
  \left[
    1-\frac{m_N^2m_M^2}{(P_N\cdot P_M)^2}
  \right]^{1/2}.
\]
For nonrelativistic two-body elastic scattering this reduces to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd E}{\dd\omega}
  =
  \frac{8G}{5\pi}\mu^2v^4\sin^2\theta,
  \tag{10.4.24}\label{eq:10.4.24}
\end{equation}
where \(\mu\) is the reduced mass, \(v\) is the relative velocity, and
\(\theta\) is the scattering angle in the center-of-mass reference frame.

The gravitational radiation produced by the collisions occurring in a gas
can be determined by summing up the radiated energies per collision given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.4.23} or \eqref{eq:10.4.24}, provided that there is enough
time between collisions so that they do not interfere. This condition can be
expressed as
\begin{equation}
  \omega\gg\omega_c,
  \tag{10.4.25}\label{eq:10.4.25}
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_c\) is the collision frequency of a typical gas particle. (If
\(\omega\lesssim\omega_c\), then the gas behaves as a fluid rather than as a
collection of independent particles.) When \eqref{eq:10.4.25} is satisfied,
the power per unit volume and per unit frequency interval is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\omega}
  =
  \frac{8G}{5\pi}
  \sum_{(a,b)}\mu_{ab}^2n_an_b
  \left\langle
    v_{ab}^5
    \int\frac{\dd\sigma_{ab}}{\dd\Omega}\sin^2\theta\,\dd\Omega
  \right\rangle,
  \tag{10.4.26}\label{eq:10.4.26}
\end{equation}
where \(n_a\) is the number density of gas particles of type \(a\),
\(\dd\sigma_{ab}/\dd\Omega\) is the center-of-mass-system differential
scattering cross-section, the sum runs over all different pairs of particle
types, and the average \(\langle\cdots\rangle\) is taken over all collisions.

As an example, let us calculate the gravitational radiation emitted by
Coulomb collisions in a plasma. The Rutherford scattering cross-section is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\sigma_{ab}}{\dd\Omega}
  =
  \frac{e_a^2e_b^2}
       {4v_{ab}^4\mu_{ab}^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}.
  \tag{10.4.27}\label{eq:10.4.27}
\end{equation}
The integral over \(\theta\) must be cut off at a minimum angle
\(1/\Lambda\), with \(\Lambda\gg1\) determined by the Debye screening of
the Coulomb force at large impact parameters; we have then
\begin{equation}
  \int\frac{\dd\sigma_{ab}}{\dd\Omega}\sin^2\theta\,\dd\Omega
  \simeq
  \frac{4\pi e_a^2e_b^2\ln\Lambda}
       {\mu_{ab}^2v_{ab}^4}.
  \tag{10.4.28}\label{eq:10.4.28}
\end{equation}
We are left with an average of \(v_{ab}\), which for a Maxwell--Boltzmann
distribution is
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle v_{ab}\right\rangle
  =
  2\left(\frac{2kT}{\pi\mu_{ab}}\right)^{1/2}.
  \tag{10.4.29}\label{eq:10.4.29}
\end{equation}
Putting \eqref{eq:10.4.28} and \eqref{eq:10.4.29} into
\eqref{eq:10.4.26} gives the power per unit volume and per unit frequency
interval (in c.g.s. units) as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\omega}
  =
  \frac{64G}{5c^5}
  \left(\frac{2kT}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}
  \ln\Lambda
  \sum_{(a,b)}
  \frac{n_an_be_a^2e_b^2}{\sqrt{\mu_{ab}}}.
  \tag{10.4.30}\label{eq:10.4.30}
\end{equation}
Typically \(\ln\Lambda\) is of order 10. For a plasma of completely ionized
hydrogen we must take into account electron-electron and electron-proton
collisions, and \eqref{eq:10.4.30} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd\omega}
  =
  \frac{64Gn_e^2e^4}{5c^5}
  \left(\frac{2kT}{\pi m_e}\right)^{1/2}
  (1+\sqrt2)\ln\Lambda.
  \tag{10.4.31}\label{eq:10.4.31}
\end{equation}
The electron collision frequency may in this case be estimated as
\begin{equation}
  \omega_c
  \simeq
  \frac{e^4n_e\langle v\rangle}{(kT)^2}
  \simeq
  \frac{e^4n_e}{(kT)^{3/2}\sqrt{m_e}}.
  \tag{10.4.32}\label{eq:10.4.32}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:10.4.30} or \eqref{eq:10.4.31} holds for
\(\omega\gg\omega_c\) and \(\hbar\omega\ll kT\).

These results can be applied to the hydrogen plasma in the solar core. Within
a volume \(V\) of order \(2\times10^{31}\ \mathrm{cm}^3\) this plasma has
\(T\simeq10^7\ {\mathrm{K}}\), \(n_e\simeq3\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}\),
and \(\ln\Lambda\simeq4\). The collision frequency \eqref{eq:10.4.32} is
\(10^{15}\ \mathrm{sec}^{-1}\), three orders of magnitude less than the
thermal frequency \(kT/\hbar\simeq10^{18}\ \mathrm{sec}^{-1}\), so the total
power produced in gravitational radiation can be estimated by multiplying
\eqref{eq:10.4.31} by \(VkT/\hbar\). In this way we find that the thermal
collisions in the solar core produce about \(10^8\) watts of gravitational
radiation.
