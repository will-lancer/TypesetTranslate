\subsection{Energy, Momentum, and Angular Momentum of Gravitation}
\label{sec:7.6}

The physical significance of the Einstein equations can be clarified by
writing them in an entirely equivalent form that, because it is not
manifestly covariant, reveals their relation to the wave equations of
elementary-particle physics. Let us adopt a coordinate system that is
quasi-Minkowskian, in the sense that the metric \(g_{\mu\nu}\) approaches
the Minkowski metric \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\) at great distances from the finite
material system under study. (This is the case in harmonic coordinate
systems, and others as well.) We then write
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu},
  \tag{7.6.1}\label{eq:7.6.1}
\end{equation}
so that \(h_{\mu\nu}\) vanishes at infinity. (However, \(h_{\mu\nu}\) is
not assumed to be small everywhere.) The part of the Ricci tensor linear in
\(h_{\mu\nu}\) is then
\begin{equation}
  R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}
  =
  \frac12\left(
    \partial_\rho\partial_\mu h^\rho{}_\nu
    +\partial_\rho\partial_\nu h^\rho{}_\mu
    -\Box h_{\mu\nu}
    -\partial_\mu\partial_\nu h
  \right),
  \qquad
  h\equiv h^\rho{}_\rho,
  \quad
  \Box\equiv\eta^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho\partial_\sigma .
  \tag{7.6.2}\label{eq:7.6.2}
\end{equation}
[See Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.2}. We are adopting the convenient convention that
indices on \(h_{\mu\nu}\), \(R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}\), and \(\partial_\mu\) are
raised and lowered with \(\eta\)'s, for example,
\(h^\mu{}_\nu=\eta^{\mu\rho}h_{\rho\nu}\) and
\(\partial^\mu=\eta^{\mu\rho}\partial_\rho\), whereas indices on true
tensors such as \(R_{\mu\nu}\) are raised and lowered with \(g\)'s as
usual.] The exact Einstein equations can then be written as
\begin{equation}
  R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}
  -\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}R^{(1)\lambda}{}_\lambda
  =
  8\pi G\left(T_{\mu\nu}+t_{\mu\nu}\right),
  \tag{7.6.3}\label{eq:7.6.3}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mu\nu}
  \equiv
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}\left[
    R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}
    -\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}R^{(1)\lambda}{}_\lambda
    -R_{\mu\nu}
    +\frac12g_{\mu\nu}R
  \right].
  \tag{7.6.4}\label{eq:7.6.4}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:7.6.3} has just the form we should expect for the wave
equation of a field of spin 2 (see Section~10.2), but with the peculiarity
that its ``source'' depends explicitly on the field \(h_{\mu\nu}\). We
interpret this feature by saying that the field \(h_{\mu\nu}\) is generated
by the total densities and fluxes of energy and momentum, and
\(t_{\mu\nu}\) is simply the energy-momentum ``tensor'' of the
gravitational field itself. That is, we interpret the quantity
\begin{equation}
  \tau^{\nu\lambda}
  \equiv
  \eta^{\nu\mu}\eta^{\lambda\kappa}
  \left(T_{\mu\kappa}+t_{\mu\kappa}\right)
  \tag{7.6.5}\label{eq:7.6.5}
\end{equation}
as the total energy-momentum ``tensor'' of matter and gravitation. There
are several properties of \(\tau^{\nu\lambda}\) that support this
interpretation:

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(A)} The quantities \(R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}\) obey the
linearized Bianchi identities
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\nu\left(
    R^{(1)\nu\lambda}
    -\frac12\eta^{\nu\lambda}R^{(1)\mu}{}_\mu
  \right)\equiv0.
  \tag{7.6.6}\label{eq:7.6.6}
\end{equation}
It therefore follows from the field equations \eqref{eq:7.6.3} that
\(\tau^{\nu\lambda}\) is locally conserved:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\nu\tau^{\nu\lambda}=0.
  \tag{7.6.7}\label{eq:7.6.7}
\end{equation}
Note that although \(T^{\mu\nu}\) obeys the covariant conservation law
\(\nabla_\nu T^{\mu\nu}=0\), which really describes the exchange of energy
between matter and gravitation, the quantity \(\tau^{\nu\lambda}\) is
conserved in the ordinary sense. In particular, for any finite system of
volume \(V\) bounded by a surface \(S\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.7} tells us that
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int_V\tau^{0\lambda}\,\dd^3x
  =
  -\int_S\tau^{i\lambda}n_i\,\dd S,
  \tag{7.6.8}\label{eq:7.6.8}
\end{equation}
where \(n_i\) is the unit outward normal to the surface. Hence we may
interpret
\begin{equation}
  P^\lambda\equiv\int_V\tau^{0\lambda}\,\dd^3x
  \tag{7.6.9}\label{eq:7.6.9}
\end{equation}
as the total energy-momentum ``vector'' of the system, including matter,
electromagnetism, and gravitation; \(\tau^{i\lambda}\) is the corresponding
flux.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(B)} Besides being conserved,
\(\tau^{\nu\lambda}\) is also symmetric:
\begin{equation}
  \tau^{\nu\lambda}=\tau^{\lambda\nu},
  \tag{7.6.10}\label{eq:7.6.10}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\mu M^{\mu\nu\lambda}=0,
  \tag{7.6.11}\label{eq:7.6.11}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  M^{\mu\nu\lambda}
  \equiv
  \tau^{\mu\lambda}x^\nu-\tau^{\mu\nu}x^\lambda .
  \tag{7.6.12}\label{eq:7.6.12}
\end{equation}
We can thus interpret \(M^{0\nu\lambda}\) and \(M^{i\nu\lambda}\) as the
density and flux of a total angular momentum
\begin{equation}
  J^{\nu\lambda}
  \equiv
  \int\dd^3x\,M^{0\nu\lambda}
  =
  -J^{\lambda\nu},
  \tag{7.6.13}\label{eq:7.6.13}
\end{equation}
that is constant if \(M^{i\nu\lambda}\) vanishes on the surface of the
volume of integration.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(C)} We can compute \(t_{\mu\nu}\) as a power series in
\(h_{\mu\nu}\), and find that the first term is quadratic:
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mu\nu}
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}\left[
    \frac12h_{\mu\nu}R^{(1)\lambda}{}_\lambda
    -\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h^{\rho\sigma}R^{(1)}_{\rho\sigma}
    -R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}
    +\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta^{\rho\sigma}R^{(2)}_{\rho\sigma}
  \right]
  +\order(h^3),
  \tag{7.6.14}\label{eq:7.6.14}
\end{equation}
where \(R^{(2)}_{\mu\kappa}\) is the second-order part of the Ricci tensor,
given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.2} as
\begin{align}
  R^{(2)}_{\mu\kappa}
  ={}&
  \frac12h^{\lambda\nu}\left(
    \partial_\kappa\partial_\mu h_{\lambda\nu}
    -\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}
    -\partial_\nu\partial_\mu h_{\lambda\kappa}
    +\partial_\nu\partial_\lambda h_{\mu\kappa}
  \right)
  \notag\\
  &-\frac14\left(
    2\partial_\nu h^\nu{}_\sigma
    -\partial_\sigma h^\nu{}_\nu
  \right)
  \left(
    \partial_\kappa h^\sigma{}_\mu
    +\partial_\mu h^\sigma{}_\kappa
    -\partial^\sigma h_{\mu\kappa}
  \right)
  \notag\\
  &+\frac14\left(
    \partial^\lambda h_{\sigma\kappa}
    +\partial_\kappa h_\sigma{}^\lambda
    -\partial_\sigma h^\lambda{}_\kappa
  \right)
  \left(
    \partial_\lambda h^\sigma{}_\mu
    +\partial_\mu h^{\sigma\lambda}
    -\partial^\sigma h_{\mu\lambda}
  \right).
  \tag{7.6.15}\label{eq:7.6.15}
\end{align}
The example of electrodynamics would have led us to expect the
energy-momentum ``tensor'' of gravitation to start with a term quadratic in
\(h_{\mu\nu}\). [Compare Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.8.9}.] The presence in
\(t_{\mu\nu}\) of terms of third and higher order simply means that the
gravitational interaction of the gravitational field with itself also
contributes to the total energy and momentum. Of course, when the
gravitational field is weak, \(h_{\mu\nu}\) is small, so our inclusion of
\(t_{\mu\nu}\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.5} (and our use of \(\eta\) to raise
indices) does not seriously change our picture of the energy-momentum
content of physical systems.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(D)} Though not generally covariant, \(t_{\mu\nu}\),
\(\tau^{\nu\lambda}\), and \(M^{\mu\nu\lambda}\) are at least Lorentz
covariant. Thus, for a closed system, \(P^\mu\) and \(J^{\mu\nu}\) are not
only constant, but also Lorentz covariant. (See Section~2.6.)

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(E)} We chose at the beginning of this section to work in
a coordinate system in which \(h_{\mu\nu}\) vanishes at infinity. Far away
from the finite material system that produces the gravitational field,
\(T_{\mu\nu}\) is zero and \(t_{\mu\nu}\) is of order \(h^2\), so the source
term on the right-hand side of the field equations \eqref{eq:7.6.3} is
effectively confined to a finite region. This suggests that in a large
variety of physical problems \(h_{\mu\nu}\) will behave at great distances
as do the potentials in electrostatics or Newtonian gravitational theory;
that is, for \(r\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}
  h_{\mu\nu}=\order\left(\frac1r\right),
  \qquad
  \partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}
  =\order\left(\frac1{r^2}\right),
  \qquad
  \partial_\lambda\partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}
  =\order\left(\frac1{r^3}\right).
  \tag{7.6.16}\label{eq:7.6.16}
\end{equation}
In this case Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.14} shows that
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mu\nu}=\order\left(\frac1{r^4}\right),
  \tag{7.6.17}\label{eq:7.6.17}
\end{equation}
so the integral \(\int\tau^{0\lambda}\dd^3x\) that gives the total energy
and momentum converges. This is why it was so important to identify the
coordinate system as quasi-Minkowskian; if \(g_{\mu\nu}\) approached the
metric of spherical polar coordinates at infinity, then our definitions
\eqref{eq:7.6.1} and \eqref{eq:7.6.4} would have led to a gravitational
energy density concentrated at infinity! (Note, though, that
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.6.16} and \eqref{eq:7.6.17} are not always valid. If the
system is eternally radiating gravitational waves (see Chapter~10), then
\(h_{\mu\nu}\) oscillates so that \(\partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}\) and
\(\partial_\lambda\partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}\) are of the same order as
\(h_{\mu\nu}\), giving an infinite total energy---which is what we would
expect for gravitational radiation filling all space. In this case not even
\(h_{\mu\nu}\) behaves like \(1/r\).)\hyperref[ch7-ref-2a]{\textsuperscript{2a}}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(F)} By its construction, \(\tau^{\nu\lambda}\) is
clearly the energy-momentum ``tensor'' we determine when we measure the
gravitational field produced by any system. Indeed, there are many possible
definitions of the energy-momentum ``tensor'' of gravitation that share
most of the good properties of our \(t_{\mu\nu}\) (these definitions are
usually based on the action principle; see Chapter~12), but \(t_{\mu\nu}\)
is specially picked out by its role in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.3} as part of the
source of \(h_{\mu\nu}\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(G)} Although calculation of \(t_{\mu\nu}\) in specific
physical problems can be a nuisance, it is fortunately possible to avoid
this calculation if all we want is the total energy and momentum of the
system. The left-hand side of the field equations \eqref{eq:7.6.3} can be
written as
\begin{equation}
  R^{(1)\nu\lambda}
  -\frac12\eta^{\nu\lambda}R^{(1)\mu}{}_\mu
  =
  \partial_\rho Q^{\rho\nu\lambda},
  \tag{7.6.18}\label{eq:7.6.18}
\end{equation}
where
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: Q=-Q_Weinberg makes G^(1)=partial Q in the target
% curvature convention and leaves the physical ADM surface charges unchanged.
\begin{equation}
  Q^{\rho\nu\lambda}
  \equiv
  \frac12\left\{
    -\eta^{\rho\lambda}\partial^\nu h
    +\eta^{\nu\lambda}\partial^\rho h
    +\eta^{\rho\lambda}\partial_\mu h^{\mu\nu}
    -\eta^{\nu\lambda}\partial_\mu h^{\mu\rho}
    -\partial^\rho h^{\nu\lambda}
    +\partial^\nu h^{\rho\lambda}
  \right\}.
  \tag{7.6.19}\label{eq:7.6.19}
\end{equation}
Note that \(Q^{\rho\nu\lambda}\) is antisymmetric in its first two indices:
\begin{equation}
  Q^{\rho\nu\lambda}=-Q^{\nu\rho\lambda},
  \tag{7.6.20}\label{eq:7.6.20}
\end{equation}
from which follows the differential identity \eqref{eq:7.6.6}. By using
the field equations \eqref{eq:7.6.3} in conjunction with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.18}, we find for the total energy-momentum ``vector''
\eqref{eq:7.6.9} the value
\[
  P^\lambda
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}\int_V\partial_\rho Q^{\rho0\lambda}\,\dd^3x
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}\int_V\partial_i Q^{i0\lambda}\,\dd^3x,
\]
and using Gauss's theorem gives
\begin{equation}
  P^\lambda
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}
  \int Q^{i0\lambda}n_i r^2\,\dd\Omega .
  \tag{7.6.21}\label{eq:7.6.21}
\end{equation}
The integral is taken over a large sphere of radius \(r\), with \(n_i\) the
outward normal and \(\dd\Omega\) the differential solid angle; that is,
\[
  r=(x_ix_i)^{1/2},
  \qquad
  n_i=\frac{x_i}{r},
  \qquad
  \dd\Omega=\sin\theta\,\dd\theta\,\dd\varphi .
\]
(Repeated Latin indices are summed over 1, 2, 3.) In greater detail, the
total momentum and energy are given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.6.19} and
\eqref{eq:7.6.21} as
\begin{align}
  P^j
  ={}&
  -\frac{1}{16\pi G}
  \int\left\{
    -\partial_t h_{kk}\,\delta_{ij}
    +\partial_k h_{k0}\,\delta_{ij}
    -\partial_i h_{j0}
    +\partial_t h_{ij}
  \right\}n_i r^2\,\dd\Omega ,
  \tag{7.6.22}\label{eq:7.6.22}\\
  P^0
  ={}&
  -\frac{1}{16\pi G}
  \int\left(
    \partial_i h_{jj}
    -\partial_j h_{ij}
  \right)n_i r^2\,\dd\Omega .
  \tag{7.6.23}\label{eq:7.6.23}
\end{align}
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: For asymptotically Schwarzschild data in isotropic
% Cartesian coordinates, h_ij=(2GM/r)delta_ij, the integrand in (7.6.23) is
% -4GM/r^2 and therefore P^0=M.

By the same reasoning, the total angular-momentum ``tensor''
\eqref{eq:7.6.13} is
\[
  J^{\nu\lambda}
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}\int
  \left(
    x^\nu\partial_\rho Q^{\rho0\lambda}
    -x^\lambda\partial_\rho Q^{\rho0\nu}
  \right)\dd^3x .
\]
As remarked in Section~2.9, the physically interesting components of
\(J^{\mu\nu}\) are the three independent space-space components:
\[
  J_1\equiv J^{23},
  \qquad
  J_2\equiv J^{31},
  \qquad
  J_3\equiv J^{12}.
\]
Using Gauss's theorem again, these components are given by
\begin{align}
  J^{jk}
  ={}&
  -\frac{1}{16\pi G}
  \int\left\{
    -x_j\partial_i h_{0k}
    +x_k\partial_i h_{0j}
    +x_j\partial_t h_{ki}
    -x_k\partial_t h_{ji}
    +h_{0k}\delta_{ij}
    -h_{0j}\delta_{ik}
  \right\}n_i r^2\,\dd\Omega .
  \tag{7.6.24}\label{eq:7.6.24}
\end{align}
Thus, in order to calculate the total momentum, energy, and angular momentum
of an arbitrary finite system, it is only necessary to know the asymptotic
behavior of \(h_{\mu\nu}\) at great distances.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(H)} It has been shown that \(P^0\) is always positive,
and takes the value zero only for matter-free empty
space.\hyperref[ch7-ref-3]{\textsuperscript{3}}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(I)} Although \(t^{\mu\nu}\) is not a tensor and
\(P^\mu\) is not a vector, the total energy and momenta have the important
property of being invariant under any coordinate transformation that
reduces at infinity to the identity. Such a transformation will be of the
form
\[
  x'^\mu=x^\mu+\xi^\mu(x),
\]
where \(\xi^\mu(x)\) vanishes as \(r\to\infty\), although
\(\xi^\mu(x)\) need not be small at finite distances. The metric tensor
in the new coordinate system is
\[
  g'^{\mu\nu}
  =
  g^{\rho\sigma}
  \left(\delta^\mu{}_\rho+\partial_\rho\xi^\mu\right)
  \left(\delta^\nu{}_\sigma+\partial_\sigma\xi^\nu\right).
\]
For \(r\to\infty\), both \(\xi^\mu\) and \(h_{\mu\nu}\) are small, so
we can calculate \(g'^{\mu\nu}\) to first order in \(\xi^\mu\) and
\(h_{\mu\nu}\) by setting \(g^{\rho\sigma}\simeq
\eta^{\rho\sigma}-h^{\rho\sigma}\) and expanding; this gives
\[
  g'^{\mu\nu}\simeq\eta^{\mu\nu}-h'^{\mu\nu},
\]
where
\[
  h'^{\mu\nu}
  =
  h^{\mu\nu}
  -\partial^\nu\xi^\mu
  -\partial^\mu\xi^\nu .
\]
The change in the quantity \eqref{eq:7.6.19} produced by this coordinate
transformation is then given, for \(r\to\infty\), by
\begin{align*}
  \Delta Q^{\rho\nu\lambda}
  =\frac12\bigl\{&
    \eta^{\rho\lambda}\partial^\nu\partial_\mu\xi^\mu
    -\eta^{\nu\lambda}\partial^\rho\partial_\mu\xi^\mu
    -\eta^{\rho\lambda}\Box\xi^\nu
    +\eta^{\nu\lambda}\Box\xi^\rho\\
    &+\partial^\rho\partial^\lambda\xi^\nu
    -\partial^\nu\partial^\lambda\xi^\rho
  \bigr\}.
\end{align*}
This can be written
\[
  \Delta Q^{\rho\nu\lambda}
  =
  \partial_\sigma D^{\sigma\rho\nu\lambda},
\]
where
\begin{align*}
  D^{\sigma\rho\nu\lambda}
  =\frac12\bigl\{&
    \eta^{\rho\lambda}\partial^\nu\xi^\sigma
    -\eta^{\nu\lambda}\partial^\rho\xi^\sigma
    -\eta^{\rho\lambda}\partial^\sigma\xi^\nu
    +\eta^{\nu\lambda}\partial^\sigma\xi^\rho\\
    &+\eta^{\sigma\lambda}\partial^\rho\xi^\nu
    -\eta^{\sigma\lambda}\partial^\nu\xi^\rho
  \bigr\}.
\end{align*}
We note that \(D^{\sigma\rho\nu\lambda}\) is totally antisymmetric in its
first three indices:
\[
  D^{\sigma\rho\nu\lambda}
  =
  -D^{\rho\sigma\nu\lambda}
  =
  -D^{\sigma\nu\rho\lambda}.
\]
Therefore the change in the surface integral takes the form
\[
  \Delta P^\lambda
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}
  \lim_{r\to\infty}
  \int
  \partial_jD^{ji0\lambda}n_i r^2\,\dd\Omega,
\]
or, using Gauss's theorem again,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta P^\lambda
  =
  \frac{1}{8\pi G}
  \int
  \partial_i\partial_jD^{ji0\lambda}\,\dd^3x
  =0.
  \tag{7.6.25}\label{eq:7.6.25}
\end{equation}
As a corollary, a general coordinate transformation at infinity can be
written as a Lorentz transformation and translation
\(x'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu+a^\mu\), under which \(P^\mu\)
transforms as a four-vector [see (D) above], times a transformation that
approaches the identity at infinity and hence does not change \(P^\mu\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(J)} If the matter in our system is divided into distant
subsystems \(S_n\), the gravitational field can be approximated by writing
\(h_{\mu\nu}\) as the sum of the \(h_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}\) that would be
produced by each subsystem acting alone. (Interference terms between these
different \(h_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}\)'s may be neglected in \(\tau_{\mu\nu}\),
because any place where one \(h_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}\) is large, all others are
small.) It follows then from the calculation of \(P^\mu\) in (G) above
that the total energy and momentum are equal to the sum of the values
\(P_n^\mu\) for each subsystem alone.

The energy-momentum ``vector'' \(P^\mu\) defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.9}
is conserved, is a Lorentz four-vector, and is additive. What more could we
ask? Any four quantities with these properties are uniquely determined to
be the usual momentum and energy (as can be shown formally by applying the
conservation laws to a collision in which distant subsystems come together,
interact, and then go off to infinity
again\hyperref[ch7-ref-4]{\textsuperscript{4}}).

The arguments of this section can be turned around to provide yet another
derivation\hyperref[ch7-ref-5]{\textsuperscript{5}} of Einstein's field
equations. Suppose that we set out to construct equations for a long-range
field of spin 2. General group-theoretic considerations require them to take
the form\hyperref[ch7-ref-6]{\textsuperscript{6}}
\begin{equation}
  R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}
  -\frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}R^{(1)\lambda}{}_\lambda
  =
  \Theta_{\mu\nu},
  \tag{7.6.26}\label{eq:7.6.26}
\end{equation}
with \(\Theta_{\mu\nu}\) some source function, which because of the
identities \eqref{eq:7.6.6} must be conserved:
\begin{equation}
  \partial^\mu\Theta_{\mu\nu}=0.
  \tag{7.6.27}\label{eq:7.6.27}
\end{equation}
It will not do to set \(\Theta_{\mu\nu}\) proportional to the
energy-momentum tensor \(T_{\mu\nu}\) of matter alone, because matter can
interchange energy and momentum with gravitation, and therefore
\(T_{\mu\nu}\) does not satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.27}. We must include in
\(\Theta_{\mu\nu}\) terms involving \(h_{\mu\nu}\) itself, and when these
terms are calculated by imposing the condition \eqref{eq:7.6.27}, we find
that the field equation \eqref{eq:7.6.26} must be simply
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.6.3}, which is equivalent to Einstein's theory. We are thus
led back to the remark at the beginning of this chapter, that the major
difference between the electromagnetic and gravitational fields is that
the source of the electromagnetic potential \(A^\mu\) is a conserved
current \(J^\mu\) that does not involve \(A^\mu\), because the
electromagnetic field is not itself charged, whereas the source of the
gravitational field \(h_{\mu\nu}\) is a conserved ``tensor''
\(t^{\mu\nu}\) that must involve \(h_{\mu\nu}\), because the gravitational
field does carry energy and momentum.
