\subsection{Density and Pressure of the Present Universe}\label{sec:15.2}

At the present instant, the pressure and energy density of the universe are
given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.1.18} and \eqref{eq:15.1.19} as
\begin{align}
  \rho_0
  &=
  \frac{3}{8\pi G}
  \left(\frac{k}{a_0^2}+H_0^2\right),
  \tag{15.2.1}\label{eq:15.2.1}\\
  p_0
  &=
  -\frac{1}{8\pi G}
  \left[
    \frac{k}{a_0^2}+H_0^2(1-2q_0)
  \right].
  \tag{15.2.2}\label{eq:15.2.2}
\end{align}
Here \(a_0\) is the present value of the cosmic scale factor \(a(t)\), and
\(H_0\) and \(q_0\) are the Hubble constant and the deceleration parameter,
defined in Section~\ref{sec:14.3} as the present values of
\(H=\dot a/a\) and \(q=-a\ddot a/\dot a^2\). From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.2.1} it follows that the spatial curvature \(k/a_0^2\)
is positive or negative according to whether \(\rho_0\) is greater or less
than a \emph{critical density}
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  =
  \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}
  =
  1.1\times10^{-29}
  \left(
    \frac{H_0}{75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}
  \right)^2
  \mathrm{g/cm^3}.
  \tag{15.2.3}\label{eq:15.2.3}
\end{equation}

As we shall see, there are good grounds to believe that the energy density of
the present universe is dominated by nonrelativistic matter, with
\begin{equation}
  p_0\ll\rho_0.
  \tag{15.2.4}\label{eq:15.2.4}
\end{equation}
If this is the case, then Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.2.2} yields a formula for the
spatial curvature in terms of the observable parameters \(H_0\) and \(q_0\):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{k}{a_0^2}=(2q_0-1)H_0^2,
  \tag{15.2.5}\label{eq:15.2.5}
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.2.1} gives the ratio of the present density to the
critical density \eqref{eq:15.2.3} as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\rho_0}{\rho_c}=2q_0.
  \tag{15.2.6}\label{eq:15.2.6}
\end{equation}
For \(q_0>\tfrac12\) the universe is positively curved, with
\(\rho_0>\rho_c\), whereas for \(q_0<\tfrac12\) the universe is negatively
curved, with \(\rho_0<\rho_c\). If we give credence to the values
\(q_0\approx1\) and
\(H_0\approx75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\) deduced from the red shift
versus luminosity relation (see Section~\ref{sec:14.6}), then we must conclude
that the density of the universe is about \(2\rho_c\), or about
\(2\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\).

Unfortunately, this result does not agree with the observed density of galactic
mass.\hyperref[ch15-ref-3]{\textsuperscript{3}} The masses of spiral galaxies
within about \(15\,\mathrm{Mpc}\) can be determined by a dynamical analysis of
their rotational velocities as functions of distance from the galactic centers.
The masses of a half-dozen or so elliptical galaxies can be calculated from the
virial theorem,\hyperref[ch15-ref-4]{\textsuperscript{4}} which gives a mass
\begin{equation}
  M=\frac{2\langle v^2\rangle}{G\langle d^{-1}\rangle},
  \tag{15.2.7}\label{eq:15.2.7}
\end{equation}
where \(\langle v^2\rangle\) is the mean-square velocity relative to the
center of mass, and \(\langle d^{-1}\rangle\) is the mean reciprocal
separation between stars. The total masses of pairs of galaxies can be
determined statistically from their relative velocities and separations, under
the assumption that the pairs are oriented at random with respect to the line
of sight.

In all three of the above methods, the galactic mass is given by a formula of
form
\begin{equation}
  M=\frac{\mu V^2D}{G},
  \tag{15.2.8}\label{eq:15.2.8}
\end{equation}
where \(V\) is some characteristic internal velocity, \(D\) is some
characteristic dimension of the object under study, and \(\mu\) is a
dimensionless number of order unity, which depends on the details of the method
used and the object studied. The characteristic distance \(D\) is measured
from the corresponding angular dimension \(\delta\) and the cosmological red
shift \(z\), using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.4.15} and \eqref{eq:14.6.7}, which
for \(z\ll1\) give
\begin{equation}
  D=\frac{z\delta}{H_0}.
  \tag{15.2.9}\label{eq:15.2.9}
\end{equation}
(For nearby galaxies, the ``angular diameter distance'' \(D/\delta\) might
be determined from the apparent magnitude of brightest stars, brightest
globular clusters, and so on, rather than from the red shift. However, if such
distance determinations form part of the cosmic distance ladder used to measure
the Hubble constant, then any error in these distances would also show up in the
Hubble constant, so that \(D\) would still scale like \(1/H_0\).) The
internal velocities \(V\) are measured directly from the distribution in red
shift around the average value \(z\) for the galaxy. It is convenient to
describe the masses determined in this way in terms of a mass-to-luminosity
ratio \(M/L\), the absolute luminosity \(L\) being given in terms of the
apparent luminosity \(l\) by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.4.12} and
\eqref{eq:14.6.7}, which for a small red shift \(z\) yield
\begin{equation}
  L=4\pi l z^2H_0^{-2}.
  \tag{15.2.10}\label{eq:15.2.10}
\end{equation}
From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.2.8}--\eqref{eq:15.2.10}, it follows that the ratio
\(M/L\) determined by the three methods described above is
\emph{proportional} to the value assumed for the Hubble constant \(H_0\).

With \(H_0\) taken as \(75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), it
appears\hyperref[ch15-ref-3]{\textsuperscript{3}} that the galactic
mass-to-light ratio \(M/L\) for elliptical galaxies is about 50 times the
solar ratio \(M_\odot/L_\odot\), whereas for spiral galaxies estimates of
\(M/L\) range from 1 to 20 times \(M_\odot/L_\odot\). According to a
survey of these \(M/L\) values by
Oort,\hyperref[ch15-ref-5]{\textsuperscript{5}} the overall mass-to-light
ratio for all galaxies is about \(21M_\odot/L_\odot\). Since the Hubble
constant may well be different from \(75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\),
this result should be written
\begin{equation}
  \frac{M}{L}
  \approx
  21\frac{M_\odot}{L_\odot}
  \left(
    \frac{H_0}{75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}
  \right).
  \tag{15.2.11}\label{eq:15.2.11}
\end{equation}
(For instance, van den
Bergh\hyperref[ch15-ref-6]{\textsuperscript{6}} carried out an analysis of
galactic masses similar to Oort's, but assumed that
\(H_0=120\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), and therefore obtained for
\(M/L\) the result \(30M_\odot/L_\odot\).) Oort also used number counts
of galaxies to estimate the luminosity density of the universe to be
\(2.2\times10^{-10}L_\odot/\mathrm{pc^3}\); this value would scale with
\(H_0\) like \(L/D^3\), which according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.2.9} and
\eqref{eq:15.2.10} scales like \(H_0\), so for a general Hubble constant
Oort's estimate of the luminosity density would be
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L
  \approx
  2.2\times10^{-10}\frac{L_\odot}{\mathrm{pc^3}}
  \left(
    \frac{H_0}{75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}
  \right).
  \tag{15.2.12}\label{eq:15.2.12}
\end{equation}
The galactic mass density of the universe can now be obtained as
\begin{align}
  \rho_G
  &=
  \left(\frac{\mathcal L}{L_\odot}\right)
  \left(\frac{M/L}{M_\odot/L_\odot}\right)M_\odot
  \notag\\
  &=
  4.6\times10^{-9}\frac{M_\odot}{\mathrm{pc^3}}
  \left(
    \frac{H_0}{75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}
  \right)^2
  \notag\\
  &=
  3.1\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
  \left(
    \frac{H_0}{75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}
  \right)^2.
  \tag{15.2.13}\label{eq:15.2.13}
\end{align}
This is smaller than the critical density \eqref{eq:15.2.3} by a factor
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\rho_G}{\rho_c}\approx0.028.
  \tag{15.2.14}\label{eq:15.2.14}
\end{equation}
(More recently, Noonan\hyperref[ch15-ref-7]{\textsuperscript{7}} and
S.~L.~Shapiro\hyperref[ch15-ref-7a]{\textsuperscript{7a}} have given
estimates of 0.016 and 0.010 for this ratio.) Note that such results are
independent of the true value of Hubble's constant. Note also that although
\(\rho_G\) and \(\rho_c\) do not turn out to be equal, they are close
enough to reassure us that gravitation does have something to do with the
expansion of the universe.

If the mass of the universe were primarily concentrated in galaxies, then
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.2.14} and \eqref{eq:15.2.6} would yield a deceleration
parameter
\begin{equation}
  q_0\approx0.014
  \qquad\text{if }\rho_0\approx\rho_G,
  \tag{15.2.15}\label{eq:15.2.15}
\end{equation}
which would imply that the universe is negatively curved and open, with
\(a_0\approx H_0^{-1}\). This value of \(q_0\) is not in agreement with
the result found from red shifts and luminosities, which give \(q_0\approx1\),
apart from possible corrections for evolution or selection effects. Of course,
evolution or selection effects may have an appreciable effect on the
measurements of \(q_0\). However, if one tentatively accepts the result that
\(q_0\) is of order unity, then one is forced to the conclusion that the mass
density of about \(2\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\) must be found
somewhere outside the normal galaxies. But where?

One place to look for the missing mass is in the intergalactic space within
clusters of galaxies. In Coma there is a rich cluster of elliptical galaxies
that appears from its smooth shape to be gravitationally bound. If bound, its
mass is given by the virial formula \eqref{eq:15.2.7}. The values of \(M/L\)
obtained in this way
range\hyperref[ch15-ref-8]{\textsuperscript{8}} from 4 to 20 times the
\(M/L\) ratio for individual elliptical galaxies. (These values are for
\(H_0=75\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\).) If there actually is 20 times
more matter within clusters than within their individual galaxies, then the
density of the universe is raised to near the critical density
\eqref{eq:15.2.3}. In fact, an X-ray source has been
discovered\hyperref[ch15-ref-8a]{\textsuperscript{8a}} filling the Coma
cluster, which suggests the presence of an intergalactic gas of ionized
hydrogen, at a temperature of order \(7\times10^7\,\mathrm{K}\). However,
the strength of this source indicates that this gas has a mass only a percent or
so of the mass required by the virial theorem. It must be kept in mind that the
Coma cluster may not be bound at
all,\hyperref[ch15-ref-9]{\textsuperscript{9}} in which case the virial
theorem overestimates its mass. Many rich clusters, like those in Virgo or
Hercules, are highly irregular, and do not appear at all stable.

If the missing mass is not within clusters of galaxies, then we must look for it
in the space between the clusters. One reasonable requirement is that the total
density of intercluster space must be less than the density within clusters, so
that the clusters represent appreciable condensations. The total volume outside
clusters is roughly 500 times greater than the volume within clusters, so the
density within clusters is roughly 500 times \eqref{eq:15.2.13}, or about
\(10^{-28}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\). Hence, even if the density outside clusters
is an order of magnitude less than the density within clusters, there is still
plenty of room in intercluster space for all the missing mass we need.

It is possible that the missing mass might be contained in normal stars that
happen to lie in intergalactic space (inside or outside clusters) or in dwarf
galaxies which are too faint to have been observed. From limits on the
extragalactic contribution to the night-sky brightness, Peebles and
Partridge\hyperref[ch15-ref-9a]{\textsuperscript{9a}} estimate that the total
mass density in normal stars, wherever located, must be less than
\(0.13\rho_c\). This estimate does not rule out the possibility that the
missing mass is contained in dark stars, with very high values of \(M/L\),
either in dwarf galaxies or in intergalactic space. One immediately thinks of
the ``black holes'' discussed in Section~\ref{sec:11.9}. However, the estimates
of galactic mass discussed above show that typical galaxies do not contain
overwhelming numbers of dark stars, so why should dark stars predominate
anywhere else? Another possibility is that the missing mass is contained in
whole galaxies that have undergone gravitational collapse. It is difficult to
see how this hypothesis could ever be verified, except through observations of
galaxies in the throes of collapse, or through observation of the deflection of
light rays that happen to pass close to a collapsed galaxy.

The missing mass might be found in the form of highly relativistic particles,
such as cosmic rays, photons, neutrinos, or gravitons. It is easy to see that
photons and neutrinos produced in ordinary thermonuclear processes cannot have
an energy density comparable with that of ordinary nonrelativistic rest mass,
for even if the universe started out as pure hydrogen and has ``cooked'' all the
way to iron, the energy released would be at most about \(9\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
per nucleon, which is 1\% of the nucleon rest mass. If highly relativistic
particles dominate the mass density of the universe, then they must be produced
in exotic processes like matter-antimatter annihilation or gravitational
collapse, or be left over from the early universe. The observed total flux
density of faint discrete radio sources at frequency \(\nu\) is of the
order\hyperref[ch15-ref-10]{\textsuperscript{10}}
\[
  \mathscr S(\nu)
  \approx
  10^{-21}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,Hz^{-1}}
  \left(\frac{\nu}{408\,\mathrm{MHz}}\right)^{-0.7},
\]
so the total energy density of the radio emission at wavelengths longer than
\(75\,\mathrm{cm}\) from these sources is roughly
\[
  \rho_{\mathrm{radio}}
  =
  \int_0^{400\,\mathrm{MHz}}\mathscr S(\nu)\,\dd\nu
  \approx10^{-12}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}
  \approx10^{-40}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}.
\]
The isotropic background at these wavelengths is not more than an order of
magnitude greater.\hyperref[ch15-ref-11]{\textsuperscript{11}} For microwave
and far-infrared wavelengths between \(75\,\mathrm{cm}\) and
\(0.05\,\mathrm{cm}\), the radiation flux is dominated by the
\(2.7\,\mathrm{K}\) background (see Section~\ref{sec:15.5}), with an energy
density given by the Stefan--Boltzmann law as
\(4.4\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\). The total energy density of
starlight at optical frequencies is
estimated\hyperref[ch15-ref-12]{\textsuperscript{12}} to be no more than
about \(10^{-35}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\). The observed X-ray background has a
flux density at energy \(E\) of
order\hyperref[ch15-ref-13]{\textsuperscript{13}}
\[
  \Phi(E)
  \approx
  20\,\mathrm{photons\,cm^{-2}\,s^{-1}\,sr^{-1}\,keV^{-1}}\,
  [E(\mathrm{keV})]^{-2}.
\]
If this background is extragalactic, then it contains an energy density
between \(0.1\,\mathrm{keV}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{MeV}\) given by
\begin{align*}
  \rho_{\mathrm{X\text{-}ray}}
  &=
  \int_{0.1\,\mathrm{keV}}^{1\,\mathrm{MeV}}
  4\pi\Phi(E)E\,\dd E\\
  &\approx
  3\times10^3\,\mathrm{keV\,cm^{-2}\,s^{-1}}
  \approx10^{-37}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}.
\end{align*}
The energy density in \(\gamma\)-rays above \(100\,\mathrm{MeV}\) is
estimated\hyperref[ch15-ref-11]{\textsuperscript{11}} to be less than
\(3\times10^{-38}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\). The
observed\hyperref[ch15-ref-14]{\textsuperscript{14}} energy density of cosmic
ray particles is not more than about \(10^{-35}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\).

These estimates indicate that the largest contribution of relativistic
particles to the total cosmic energy density is provided by the
\(2.7\,\mathrm{K}\) microwave background, to be discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:15.5}. Its density is less than one-hundredth the density
\eqref{eq:15.2.13} of galactic rest mass, which justifies our tentative
neglect of pressure in the Einstein and conservation equations.

However, it is possible that the missing mass is made up by neutrinos or
gravitons,\hyperref[ch15-ref-14a]{\textsuperscript{14a}} which interact too
weakly with matter to have been detected. In particular, the neutrino energy
density is expected to be at least comparable with that of microwave
electromagnetic radiation, but may well be many orders of magnitude greater
(see Section~\ref{sec:15.6}). If the energy density of the universe is
dominated by highly relativistic particles, then the pressure is
\begin{equation}
  p_0=\frac{\rho_0}{3},
  \tag{15.2.16}\label{eq:15.2.16}
\end{equation}
and in place of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.2.5} and \eqref{eq:15.2.6}, the Einstein
equations now give
\begin{align}
  \frac{k}{a_0^2}
  &=H_0^2(q_0-1),
  \tag{15.2.17}\label{eq:15.2.17}\\
  \frac{\rho_0}{\rho_c}
  &=q_0.
  \tag{15.2.18}\label{eq:15.2.18}
\end{align}
Here \(\rho_c\) is the same critical density \eqref{eq:15.2.3} as before.
The critical deceleration parameter, for which \(k=0\) and
\(\rho_0=\rho_c\), is now \(q_0=1\) rather than \(q_0=\tfrac12\), and the
density required for a given \(q_0\) and \(H_0\) is half of that needed for
a dust-filled universe.

Although a photon-, neutrino-, or graviton-dominated universe cannot be ruled
out on observational grounds at present, it is more conservative to suppose
that the missing mass takes the form of a tenuous hydrogen gas, ionized or
neutral, filling all space. The various methods that have been proposed to
detect this gas depend on electromagnetic signals that reach us from
cosmological distances, so we must defer our discussion of this gas until
Section~\ref{sec:15.4}, and turn now to the solution of the equations of
dynamical cosmology.
