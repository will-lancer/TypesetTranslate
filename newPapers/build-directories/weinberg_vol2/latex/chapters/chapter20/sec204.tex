\subsection{Symmetry Properties of Coefficient Functions}

The usefulness of the operator product expansion is greatly enhanced by the fact that the coefficient functions exhibit the full symmetry of the underlying theory, even where part or all of that symmetry is spontaneously broken~\hyperref[ch20-ref-4]{[4]}. To prove this, we consider the operator product expansion for a product of renormalized operators $O_i(x)$ that transform linearly under some symmetry with conserved current $J^\mu(x)$, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
\bigl[J^0(\mathbf{x},t),O_i(\mathbf{y},t)\bigr]
=-\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\sum_j t_{ij}O_j(\mathbf{y},t),
\tag{20.4.1}\label{eq:20.4.1}
\end{equation}
where $t_{ij}$ is a constant matrix. We can write the operator product expansion as the statement that, as $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ approach $x$ together (with $x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x$ all having fixed ratios)
\begin{equation}
\bra{\beta}T\{O_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots O_{i_n}(x_n)\}\ket{\alpha}
\longrightarrow
\sum_i U_i^{i_1\cdots i_n}(x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x)
\bra{\beta}O_i(x)\ket{\alpha}.
\tag{20.4.2}\label{eq:20.4.2}
\end{equation}
Now suppose that the symmetry with current $J^\mu$ is spontaneously broken, with the corresponding Goldstone boson $\pi$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\bra{\Omega}J^\mu(0)\ket{\pi}
=\frac{Fp_\pi^\mu}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}}.
\tag{20.4.3}\label{eq:20.4.3}
\end{equation}
Then, as we saw in Section 19.2, the matrix element of the operator product between states with an additional low energy Goldstone boson is
\begin{align}
\bra{\beta}T\{O_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots O_{i_n}(x_n)\}\ket{\pi\alpha}
={}&
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}\,F}
\notag\\
&\cdot\int d^4x\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
\bra{\beta}T\{O_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots O_{i_n}(x_n)J^\mu(x)\}\ket{\alpha},
\tag{20.4.4}\label{eq:20.4.4}
\end{align}
because only the Goldstone pole term survives in the integral on the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:20.4.4}. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:20.4.1} and the conservation of the current, this can be put in the form
\begin{align}
\bra{\beta}T\{O_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots O_{i_n}(x_n)\}\ket{\pi\alpha}
={}&-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}\,F}
\notag\\
&\cdot
\sum_{r=1}^n\sum_{j_r}t_{i_rj_r}
\bra{\beta}T\{O_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots O_{j_r}(x_r)\cdots O_{i_n}(x_n)\}\ket{\alpha}.
\tag{20.4.5}\label{eq:20.4.5}
\end{align}
Now apply the operator product expansion to both sides of this formula. In the limit as $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ all approach $x$ together, we find
\begin{align}
&\sum_i U_i^{i_1\cdots i_n}(x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x)
\bra{\beta}O_i(x)\ket{\pi\alpha}
\notag\\
&\qquad=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}\,F}
\sum_{r=1}^n\sum_{j_r}\sum_i t_{i_rj_r}
U_i^{i_1\cdots j_r\cdots i_n}(x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x)
\bra{\beta}O_i(x)\ket{\alpha}.
\tag{20.4.6}\label{eq:20.4.6}
\end{align}
But as a special case of Eq.~\eqref{eq:20.4.5}, we have
\begin{equation}
\bra{\beta}O_i(x)\ket{\pi\alpha}
=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}\,F}
\sum_j t_{ij}\bra{\beta}O_j(x)\ket{\alpha}.
\tag{20.4.7}\label{eq:20.4.7}
\end{equation}
Since all this holds for arbitrary states $\bra{\beta}$ and $\ket{\alpha}$, the coefficients of $\bra{\beta}O_j(x)\ket{\alpha}$ on both sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:20.4.6} must be equal, so
\begin{align}
0={}&-\sum_i t_{ij}U_i^{i_1\cdots i_n}(x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x)
\notag\\
&+\sum_{r=1}^n\sum_{j_r}t_{i_rj_r}
U_j^{i_1\cdots j_r\cdots i_n}(x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x).
\tag{20.4.8}\label{eq:20.4.8}
\end{align}
This can be restated as the condition that $U_i^{i_1\cdots i_n}(x_1-x,\ldots,x_n-x)$ is invariant under the symmetry generated by $t$, with the action of this symmetry on the lower index contragredient to its action on the upper indices, in the sense that the matrix $t$ is replaced with $-t^T$. This is the same relation that would be expected if the symmetry generated by $J^\mu$ were not spontaneously broken.
