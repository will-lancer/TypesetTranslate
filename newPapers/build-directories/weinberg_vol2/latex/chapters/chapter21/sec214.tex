\subsection[Dynamically Broken Local Symmetries]{Dynamically Broken Local Symmetries\footnotemark}
\label{sec:21.4}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}

Our discussion of spontaneously broken local symmetries has so far been entirely within the context of perturbation theory. To some extent, this limitation is inevitable. Whereas for spontaneously broken global symmetries it is possible to prove exact theorems about the existence and interactions of massless Goldstone bosons, the spontaneous breakdown of a local symmetry does not lead to any such precise consequences. Even the existence of massive vector bosons is not really a general theorem; for sufficiently strong gauge coupling these particles decay so rapidly that they lose their identity as distinct resonances of definite spin $j=1$.

On the other hand, if the gauge couplings like $e$ or $g$ or $g'$ are sufficiently small then the theory with a spontaneously broken local symmetry must be very close to one with a spontaneously broken global symmetry, about which exact theorems can be proved. It is therefore possible to derive useful approximate results for such gauge theories, \emph{even if the other non-gauge couplings are very strong}. One example is provided by the standard $SU(2)\times U(1)$ electroweak theory with a large scalar self-coupling $\lambda$ (and hence a large scalar mass; see Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.3.27}). A more intriguing possibility is that the breakdown of electroweak symmetry is due to strong forces associated with some new gauge group acting on a set of new fermions. We will here consider the results that can be obtained for all such theories, without reference to the specific mechanism for spontaneous symmetry breaking~\hyperref[ch21-ref-28]{[28]}.

We assume that in the limit of zero gauge couplings, our theory is invariant under some group $G$ of \emph{global} symmetries, spontaneously broken to a subgroup $H$. As discussed in \hyperref[sec:19.5]{Section 19.5}, in this case the theory can be written in terms of a set of Goldstone boson fields $\xi_a$, plus other matter fields $\widetilde\psi$, whose $G$-transformation properties are such that the Lagrangian is $G$-invariant if it is $H$-invariant and constructed solely from $\widetilde\psi$ and covariant derivatives $\mathcal D_{a\mu}$, $\mathcal D_\mu\widetilde\psi$ etc., given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.6.14}, \eqref{eq:19.6.30}, etc.

We now `turn on' the gauge couplings. The gauge group $\mathcal G$ is of course required to be a subgroup $\mathcal G\subset G$ of the group $G$ of all symmetries of the theory, and when $G$ is spontaneously broken to $H$, $\mathcal G$ must be spontaneously broken to a subgroup $\mathcal H$, equal to the intersection of $\mathcal G$ with $H$. The generators $\mathcal T_\alpha$ of the gauge group $\mathcal G$ may be expressed as linear combinations of the generators $T_A$ of the full group $G$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal T_\alpha=\sum_A e_{\alpha A}T_A,
\tag{21.4.1}\label{eq:21.4.1}
\end{equation}
with coefficients $e_{\alpha A}$, the gauge couplings, that are taken very small. The index $A$ runs over the labels $i,a$ of the unbroken symmetry generators $t_i$ and the broken symmetry generators $x_a$. (We are here taking the generators $T_A$ to be conventionally normalized; that is, they are represented by matrices with elements of order unity. In particular, in contrast to the $\mathcal T_\alpha$, the structure constants of the $x_a$ and $t_i$ do not include factors of coupling constants.)

In the underlying theory in which $G$ invariance is linearly realized, we introduce the coupling of gauge fields $\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}$ to other fields $\psi$ by replacing ordinary derivatives with gauge-covariant derivatives
\begin{equation}
\left(\partial_\mu-i\sum_\alpha\mathcal T_\alpha\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}\right)\psi
=
\left(\partial_\mu-i\sum_A T_A A_{A\mu}\right)\psi,
\tag{21.4.2}\label{eq:21.4.2}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
A_{A\mu}\equiv\sum_\alpha e_{\alpha A}\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}.
\tag{21.4.3}\label{eq:21.4.3}
\end{equation}
The resulting theory is then invariant under \emph{formal} local transformations, under which the fields transform according to
\begin{equation}
\psi\longrightarrow g\psi,
\tag{21.4.4}\label{eq:21.4.4}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\sum_A T_A A_{A\mu}
\longrightarrow
g\left(\sum_A T_A A_{A\mu}\right)g^{-1}
-i(\partial_\mu g)g^{-1},
\tag{21.4.5}\label{eq:21.4.5}
\end{equation}
where $g(x)$ is an arbitrary spacetime-dependent element of the group $G$. This is a purely formal invariance, because the gauge couplings in general actually break $G$, as shown by the fact that the transformation \eqref{eq:21.4.5} does not in general preserve the form of the linear combination \eqref{eq:21.4.3}. Nevertheless, we can temporarily forget about Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.3}, treating $A_A^\mu$ as an unconstrained classical external field, and analyze the structure of the Lagrangian for the matter fields and their interaction with the gauge fields by requiring that it be invariant under local transformations \eqref{eq:21.4.4}, \eqref{eq:21.4.5}. In this way we will insure not only that the Lagrangian is invariant under the true local symmetry subgroup $\mathcal G$ (and for $e_{\alpha A}\to0$ under the larger global symmetry group $G$), but also that the currents, the variational derivatives of the matter action with respect to $\mathcal A_\alpha^\mu$, will have the correct transformation under the broken global symmetry group $G$. Later we will restrict $A_A^\mu$ to the form \eqref{eq:21.4.3}, and treat the field $\mathcal A_\alpha^\mu$ as a quantum field, supplying a suitable kinematic term in the Lagrangian for this field.

In order to explore the implications of the spontaneous breakdown of the invariance group $G$ to its subgroup $H$, we will proceed as we did in \hyperref[sec:19.6]{Section 19.6}. First, replace $\psi$ and $A$ with new fields $\widetilde\psi$, $\widetilde A$:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\psi=\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\psi,
\tag{21.4.6}\label{eq:21.4.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\widetilde A_A^\mu
=\sum_B D_{AB}\bigl(\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\bigr)A_B^\mu,
\tag{21.4.7}\label{eq:21.4.7}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma(\xi)$ is the standard $G$ transformation that eliminates the Goldstone boson degrees of freedom in $\psi$, and $D(g)$ is the representation of $G$ furnished by the gauge fields:
\begin{equation}
gT_Ag^{-1}=\sum_B D_{BA}(g)T_B.
\tag{21.4.8}\label{eq:21.4.8}
\end{equation}
These Goldstone degrees of freedom reappear in the spacetime-dependent parameters $\xi_a$ on which $\gamma(\xi)$ depends. By the same calculations as in \hyperref[sec:19.6]{Section 19.6}, for local as well as global transformations the transformation rule \eqref{eq:21.4.4} translates into the transformations
\begin{equation}
\xi^a\longrightarrow\xi^{a\prime}=f^a(\xi,g),
\tag{21.4.9}\label{eq:21.4.9}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\psi\longrightarrow\widetilde\psi'
=h(\xi,g)\widetilde\psi,
\tag{21.4.10}\label{eq:21.4.10}
\end{equation}
where $h$ and $f$ are defined by
\begin{equation}
g\gamma(\xi)=\gamma\bigl(f(\xi,g)\bigr)h(\xi,g),
\tag{21.4.11}\label{eq:21.4.11}
\end{equation}
with $h$ in the unbroken subgroup $H$. We also need to work out the transformation rule for $\widetilde A_A^\mu$. Recall that, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.5}, under these local transformations the linear combinations of gauge fields in Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.2} transform as
\[
\sum_A T_A A_{A\mu}
\longrightarrow
\sum_A T_A A'_{A\mu}
=g\left[\sum_A T_A A_{A\mu}-ig^{-1}\partial_\mu g\right]g^{-1}.
\]
Multiplying on the left and right by $\gamma^{-1}(\xi')$ and $\gamma(\xi')$ respectively, and using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.4.7}, \eqref{eq:21.4.8}, and \eqref{eq:21.4.11}, we can write this as
\begin{align}
i\sum_A T_A\widetilde A'_{A\mu}
={}&h(\xi,g)
\left[
i\sum_A T_A\widetilde A_{A\mu}
+\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\bigl[g^{-1}\partial_\mu g\bigr]\gamma(\xi)
\right]
h^{-1}(\xi,g).
\tag{21.4.12}\label{eq:21.4.12}
\end{align}
To see how to cancel the inhomogeneous $g^{-1}\partial_\mu g$ term, we note that by differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.11} and multiplying on the left with its inverse, we have
\begin{align}
\gamma^{-1}(\xi')\partial_\mu\gamma(\xi')
={}&h(\xi,g)\gamma^{-1}(\xi)
\bigl[g^{-1}\partial_\mu g\bigr]\gamma(\xi)h^{-1}(\xi,g)
\notag\\
&+h(\xi,g)
\bigl[\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\partial_\mu\gamma(\xi)\bigr]
h^{-1}(\xi,g)
+h(\xi,g)\partial_\mu h^{-1}(\xi,g).
\tag{21.4.13}\label{eq:21.4.13}
\end{align}
So to cancel the inhomogeneous term, we must subtract Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.12} from Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.13}:
\begin{align}
\gamma^{-1}(\xi')\partial_\mu\gamma(\xi')
-i\sum_A T_A\widetilde A'_{A\mu}
={}&h(\xi,g)
\left[
\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\partial_\mu\gamma(\xi)
-i\sum_A T_A\widetilde A_{A\mu}
\right]
h^{-1}(\xi,g)
\notag\\
&-\bigl[\partial_\mu h(\xi,g)\bigr]h^{-1}(\xi,g).
\tag{21.4.14}\label{eq:21.4.14}
\end{align}
We therefore define new gauge-covariant quantities $\mathcal D$ and $\mathcal E$ by
\begin{equation}
\sum_a i\mathcal D_{a\mu}x_a+\sum_i i\mathcal E_{i\mu}t_i
\equiv
\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\partial_\mu\gamma(\xi)
-i\sum_A T_A\widetilde A_{A\mu}
\tag{21.4.15}\label{eq:21.4.15}
\end{equation}
with transformation properties
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_{a\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal D'_{a\mu},
\qquad
\mathcal E_{i\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal E'_{i\mu},
\tag{21.4.16}\label{eq:21.4.16}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\sum_a\mathcal D'_{a\mu}x_a
=h(\xi,g)
\left(\sum_a\mathcal D_{a\mu}x_a\right)
h^{-1}(\xi,g),
\tag{21.4.17}\label{eq:21.4.17}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
\sum_i\mathcal E'_{i\mu}t_i
={}&h(\xi,g)
\left(\sum_i\mathcal E_{i\mu}t_i\right)
h^{-1}(\xi,g)
\notag\\
&+i\bigl(\partial_\mu h(\xi,g)\bigr)h^{-1}(\xi,g),
\tag{21.4.18}\label{eq:21.4.18}
\end{align}
just as in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.6.26} and \eqref{eq:19.6.27}. We can use $\mathcal E$ to construct fully covariant derivatives of matter fields
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_\mu\widetilde\psi
=\partial_\mu\widetilde\psi
+i\sum_i t_i\mathcal E_{i\mu}\widetilde\psi
\tag{21.4.19}\label{eq:21.4.19}
\end{equation}
as well as higher derivatives like $\mathcal D_\nu\mathcal D_\mu\widetilde\psi$, etc. Because of the inhomogeneous term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.12}, we cannot freely introduce $\widetilde A_{A\mu}$ or covariant derivatives like \eqref{eq:21.4.19} of $\widetilde A_{A\mu}$ into the Lagrangian. However, it is easy to construct a `curl' that transforms covariantly under both local $\mathcal G$ transformations and global $G$ transformations. It is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde F_{A\mu\nu}
\equiv
\sum_B D_{AB}\bigl(\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\bigr)
\left(
\partial_\mu A_{B\nu}-\partial_\nu A_{B\mu}
-\sum_{CD}C_{BCD}A_{C\mu}A_{D\nu}
\right).
\tag{21.4.20}\label{eq:21.4.20}
\end{equation}
This transforms under formal local $G$ transformations as
\begin{equation}
\widetilde F_{A\mu\nu}
\longrightarrow
\widetilde F'_{A\mu\nu}
=\sum_BD_{AB}\bigl(h(\xi,g)\bigr)\widetilde F_{B\mu\nu}.
\tag{21.4.21}\label{eq:21.4.21}
\end{equation}
\emph{The Lagrangian is therefore invariant under formal local $G$ transformations if it is constructed as an arbitrary function of $\widetilde\psi$, $\mathcal D_{a\mu}$, $\mathcal D_\mu\widetilde\psi$, $\widetilde F_{A\mu\nu}$, and higher covariant derivatives, that satisfies global $H$ invariance.}

Now let us return to reality, and treat $A_A^\mu$ as a quantum field of the restricted form \eqref{eq:21.4.3}. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.4.15} and \eqref{eq:21.4.20} now become
\begin{align}
\sum_a i\mathcal D_{a\mu}x_a+\sum_i i\mathcal E_{i\mu}t_i
={}&\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\partial_\mu\gamma(\xi)
\notag\\
&-i\sum_{AB\alpha}T_A
D_{AB}\bigl(\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\bigr)e_{\alpha B}\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}
\tag{21.4.22}\label{eq:21.4.22}
\end{align}
and
\begin{equation}
\widetilde F_{A\mu\nu}
=\sum_{B\alpha}
D_{AB}\bigl(\gamma^{-1}(\xi)\bigr)e_{\alpha B}
\mathcal F_{\alpha\mu\nu},
\tag{21.4.23}\label{eq:21.4.23}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathcal F_{\beta\mu\nu}
=\partial_\mu\mathcal A_{\beta\nu}
-\partial_\nu\mathcal A_{\beta\mu}
-\sum_{\gamma\delta}
\mathcal C_{\beta\gamma\delta}
\mathcal A_{\gamma\mu}\mathcal A_{\delta\nu},
\tag{21.4.24}\label{eq:21.4.24}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal C_{\beta\gamma\delta}$ the structure constant of the gauge group, related to the structure constant of $G$ by
\begin{equation}
\sum_{CD}C_{BCD}e_{\gamma C}e_{\delta D}
=\sum_\beta\mathcal C_{\beta\gamma\delta}e_{\beta B}.
\tag{21.4.25}\label{eq:21.4.25}
\end{equation}
We include in the Lagrangian as the kinematic term for this field the usual Yang--Mills term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathcal A}
=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_\alpha
\mathcal F_{\alpha\mu\nu}\mathcal F_\alpha{}^{\mu\nu},
\tag{21.4.26}\label{eq:21.4.26}
\end{equation}
in which, by linear transformations of the $\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}$, and correspondingly of the $e_{\alpha A}$, we have adjusted the coefficient of $\mathcal F_{\alpha\mu\nu}\mathcal F_\beta{}^{\mu\nu}$ to be just $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. The linear term in $\mathcal F_{\alpha\mu\nu}$ is just $\partial_\mu\mathcal A_{\alpha\nu}-\partial_\nu\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.26} has the effect of making $\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}$ a canonically normalized vector field. The effective Lagrangian density is therefore to be taken as a function of $\widetilde\psi$, $\mathcal D_\mu\widetilde\psi$, and $\mathcal D_{a\mu}$ that is invariant under global $H$ transformations, plus possible terms that conserve $\mathcal G$ but not $G$, with coefficients proportional to two or more factors of the $e_{\alpha A}$.

Now let us see what sort of perturbation theory we can construct out of these ingredients. We know that the gauge bosons become massless in the limit $e_{\alpha A}\to0$, where they decouple from the matter fields that experience the spontaneous symmetry breakdown. Let us therefore tentatively anticipate that their mass for small $e_{\alpha A}$ is of order $eM$, where $e$ is a typical value of $e_{\alpha A}$ (the generators $T_A$ being normalized to have structure constants of order unity) and $M$ is an energy scale typical of the dynamics that leads to spontaneous symmetry breaking. We shall consider here a general Feynman diagram involving gauge and Goldstone bosons of energy or momentum $Q\lesssim eM$, and with all particles of higher energy or momentum and all heavier matter particles buried in corrections to the coupling constants in an effective field theory. Our perturbation theory will be an expansion in powers of $e$ and $Q/M$. Following the same analysis as in \hyperref[sec:19.4]{Sections 19.4--6}, the total number of powers of $e$ and/or $Q/M$ in any such diagram is
\begin{equation}
\nu=\sum_iV_i(d_i+e_i-2)+2L+2,
\tag{21.4.27}\label{eq:21.4.27}
\end{equation}
where $V_i$ is the number of vertices of type $i$; $d_i$ and $e_i$ are the number of derivatives and factors of $e_{\alpha A}$, respectively, in an interaction of type $i$; and $L$ is the number of loops. With the constraint \eqref{eq:21.4.3}, a field $A_{A\mu}$ or $\widetilde A_{A\mu}$ contributes one factor of $e$. Inspection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.15} shows that each Goldstone boson covariant derivative $\mathcal D_{a\mu}$ contributes $+1$ to $d_i+e_i$, and inspection of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.4.19} and \eqref{eq:21.4.15} shows that each additional covariant derivative of $\mathcal D_{a\mu}$ contributes another $+1$ to $d_i+e_i$. All allowed terms in the Lagrangian have $d_i+e_i\geq2$, so the dominant contributions are those from tree graphs ($L=0$) constructed entirely from interactions with $d_i+e_i=2$. The only such interactions are the Goldstone boson kinematic term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L
=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}F_{ab}^2
\mathcal D_{a\mu}\mathcal D_b{}^\mu,
\tag{21.4.28}\label{eq:21.4.28}
\end{equation}
the Yang--Mills term \eqref{eq:21.4.26}, and possible symmetry-breaking non-derivative terms of second order in the $e_{\alpha A}$.

To see the physical significance of the field $\xi_a$, note that the linear term in $\mathcal D_{a\mu}$ is
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal D_{a\mu})_{\mathrm{LIN}}
=\partial_\mu\xi_a-\sum_\alpha e_{\alpha a}\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}.
\tag{21.4.29}\label{eq:21.4.29}
\end{equation}
As shown in the \hyperref[app:21.A]{appendix to this chapter}, we may always choose a `unitarity gauge' in which, for all $\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{ab}F_{ab}^2\xi_a e_{\alpha b}=0,
\tag{21.4.30}\label{eq:21.4.30}
\end{equation}
which makes the cross term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.28} vanish. To clarify the significance of this condition, note that in the special case where all broken symmetries are gauge symmetries, any $x_a$ can be written as a linear combination of gauge generators and unbroken generators
\begin{align*}
x_a
&=\sum_\alpha c_{a\alpha}\mathcal T_\alpha+\sum_i c_{ai}t_i
\\
&=\sum_\alpha c_{a\alpha}
\left(\sum_b e_{\alpha b}x_b+\sum_i e_{\alpha i}t_i\right)
+\sum_i c_{ai}t_i,
\end{align*}
and therefore
\[
\sum_\alpha c_{a\alpha}e_{\alpha b}=\delta_{ab}.
\]
Contracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.30} with $c_{b\alpha}$, we see then that $\xi_a=0$; there are no Goldstone bosons at all in this gauge. More generally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.30} leaves us with just those Goldstone bosons that do \emph{not} correspond to gauge symmetries. Some of these are associated with elements of $G$ that are broken by the gauge interactions, and therefore have masses of second order in gauge couplings; these are called pseudo-Goldstone bosons.

With $\xi$ chosen to satisfy the unitarity gauge condition \eqref{eq:21.4.30}, the quadratic part of the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:21.4.28} is simply
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal L_\xi)_{\mathrm{QUAD}}
=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}F_{ab}^2
\partial_\mu\xi_a\partial^\mu\xi_b
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}
\mu_{\alpha\beta}^2\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}\mathcal A_\beta{}^\mu,
\tag{21.4.31}\label{eq:21.4.31}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\alpha\beta}^2
=\sum_{ab}F_{ab}^2 e_{\alpha a}e_{\beta b}.
\tag{21.4.32}\label{eq:21.4.32}
\end{equation}
This has two important implications. First, we note that $\xi_a$ may be expressed in terms of a canonically orthonormalized field $\pi_a$, as
\begin{equation}
\xi_a=\sum_bF_{ab}^{-1}\pi_b,
\tag{21.4.33}\label{eq:21.4.33}
\end{equation}
where $F_{ab}$ is the positive square root of the positive matrix $F_{ab}^2$. This shows that $F_{ab}^{-1}$ are the factors analogous to $F_\pi^{-1}$ that accompany the emission and absorption of low energy Goldstone bosons. Second, since $\mathcal A_{\alpha\mu}$ has been defined to be a canonically normalized vector field, Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.31} shows that $\mu_{\alpha\beta}^2$ is the square of the vector boson mass matrix. Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.32} is a universal formula for the vector boson mass matrix, valid to second order in the gauge couplings but to all orders in all other interactions. By using Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.1} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.1.7}, it is easy to see that our previous result Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.1.7} is a special case of Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.32}, with
\[
F_{ab}^2=-\sum_{nm\ell}(x_a)_{nm}(x_b)_{n\ell}v_mv_\ell.
\]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.32} may also be understood on the basis of the continuity arguments outlined (in a somewhat different notation) at the end of \hyperref[sec:21.1]{Section 21.1}. It guarantees that the effects of gauge boson exchange that survive in the limit of zero gauge coupling are the same as the effects that would have been produced by Goldstone boson exchange if there were no gauge coupling.

In general, we cannot calculate the $F_{ab}^2$ matrix, but we do know that it must be invariant under the unbroken subgroup $H$, in the sense that
\[
\sum_d\left[C_{ibd}F_{dc}^2+C_{icd}F_{bd}^2\right]=0.
\]
This condition allows us to put useful constraints on the gauge boson masses \eqref{eq:21.4.32}.

As an example, consider the case of the electroweak gauge group $SU(2)\times U(1)$, spontaneously broken to the $U(1)$ of electromagnetism. The three broken symmetry generators $x_a$ can be taken as the three generators of $SU(2)$ (called $t_1$, $t_2$, $t_3$ in \hyperref[sec:21.3]{Section 21.3}), without the coupling constant factor $g$, and the one unbroken symmetry generator $t$ can be taken as the charge $q$, without the factor $e$. That is, the $x_a$ and $t$ are represented on the lepton doublets by the matrices
\[
\vec x=\frac{1}{4}(1+\gamma^5)
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
\right\},
\qquad
t=-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The gauge generators are then given by
\begin{equation}
\vec{\mathcal T}=g\vec x,
\qquad
\mathcal T_y=g'(x_3-t).
\tag{21.4.34}\label{eq:21.4.34}
\end{equation}
That is, the non-zero coefficients $e_{\alpha a}$ of $x_a$ in the gauge generator $\mathcal T_\alpha$ are
\[
e_{11}=e_{22}=e_{33}=g,
\qquad
e_{y3}=g'.
\]
Also, since $t$ subjects the three-vector $\vec x$ to a rotation around the three-axis, this unbroken symmetry requires the matrix $F_{ab}^2$ to have the non-zero components
\[
F_{11}^2=F_{22}^2=F_C^2,
\qquad
F_{33}^2=F_N^2.
\]
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.32}, the mass squared matrix of the gauge bosons then has the non-vanishing elements
\[
\mu_{11}^2=\mu_{22}^2=g^2F_C^2,
\qquad
\mu_{33}^2=g^2F_N^2,
\qquad
\mu_{3y}^2=gg'F_N^2,
\qquad
\mu_{yy}^2=g'^2F_N^2.
\]
Its eigenvalues are
\begin{equation}
m_W^2=g^2F_C^2,
\qquad
m_Z^2=(g^2+g'^2)F_N^2,
\qquad
m_A^2=0.
\tag{21.4.35}\label{eq:21.4.35}
\end{equation}

To go further, we need a relation between $F_C$ and $F_N$. This is provided if the theory is invariant in the limit $g=g'=0$ under a global symmetry group $G$ larger than $SU(2)\times U(1)$, which breaks spontaneously to a subgroup $H$, that includes three-dimensional rotations under which $\vec x$ rotates as a three-vector. Such an unbroken symmetry would require $F_{ab}^2$ to be proportional to $\delta_{ab}$, and hence
\[
F_C=F_N.
\]
Any such symmetry is known as a `custodial' symmetry. It has the consequence that $m_Z/m_W$ is given in terms of the gauge coupling constants by the successful formula discussed in \hyperref[sec:21.3]{Section 21.3}
\begin{equation}
\frac{m_Z}{m_W}
=\sqrt{1+\frac{g'^2}{g^2}}
=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}.
\tag{21.4.36}\label{eq:21.4.36}
\end{equation}

For instance, in the absence of the gauge couplings, the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:21.3.28} for the scalar doublet $\phi$ in the simplest version of the $SU(2)\times U(1)$ electroweak theory may be written
\[
\left(\mathcal L_\phi\right)_{g=g'=0}
=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_n
-\frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi_n\phi_n
-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\phi_n\phi_n)^2,
\]
where
\[
\phi_1\equiv\mathop{\rm Im}\phi^+,
\qquad
\phi_2\equiv\mathop{\rm Re}\phi^+,
\qquad
\phi_3\equiv\mathop{\rm Im}\phi^0,
\qquad
\phi_4\equiv\mathop{\rm Re}\phi^0.
\]
This is automatically invariant under an `accidental' $SO(4)=SU(2)\times SU(2)$ global symmetry group, which is spontaneously broken by the vacuum expectation value of $\mathop{\rm Re}\phi^0$ down to an approximate unbroken $SO(3)$ custodial subgroup~\hyperref[ch21-ref-29]{[29]}. The result \eqref{eq:21.4.36} applies even if there is more than one scalar doublet, because even though in this case the mass and interaction terms in the scalar Lagrangian will not in general respect the custodial symmetry, it is only the kinematic term that enters in the derivation, and this always has the full $SO(4)$ symmetry.

Custodial symmetries can be found in other theories. Consider for instance a theory with no scalar fields, but with new extra-strong vector gauge interactions~\hyperref[ch21-ref-30]{[30]}, called technicolor interactions, that act on a new $SU(2)\times U(1)$ doublet $(U_r,D_r)$ of `techniquarks' $U_r$ and $D_r$, with $r$ a technicolor index. As long as the left- and right-handed parts of both $U_r$ and $D_r$ all transform in the same way under the technicolor gauge group, the Lagrangian will be invariant in the limit of vanishing electroweak couplings under the group $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ of independent $SU(2)$ transformations on the left- and right-handed techniquark doublets. According to the arguments described in \hyperref[sec:19.9]{Section 19.9}, the subgroup $SU(2)_V$ consisting of simultaneous $SU(2)$ transformations on both the left- and right-handed techniquark doublets will not be spontaneously broken. It is reasonable to suppose that the technicolor interactions will produce a spontaneous breakdown of $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ to $SU(2)_V$, just as color interactions led to a spontaneous breakdown of the chiral $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry of quantum chromodynamics (for vanishing $u$ and $d$ quark masses) to its isospin subgroup. Under the unbroken $SU(2)_V$ symmetry the electroweak generator $\vec x$ or $\vec{\mathcal T}$ rotates as a three-vector, leading again to the relation $F_C=F_N$, and the consequent successful prediction of the relation between the $W$ and $Z$ masses.

The technicolor idea is attractive, because it provides a natural mechanism for breaking the electroweak symmetry at a characteristic scale which is very much smaller than what is often supposed to be the fundamental scale of physics, generally assumed (as in string theories) to be of the order of the Planck mass, or about $10^{18}$ GeV. It is only necessary to suppose that just below the fundamental scale there is an unbroken gauge group consisting of the $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ of the strong and electroweak interactions, plus a technicolor gauge group, all with comparable small coupling constants. If the technicolor gauge interactions are asymptotically free the technicolor coupling like the QCD color coupling will increase slowly with decreasing energy, becoming strong at an energy much less than the fundamental scale. This energy where the technicolor coupling becomes strong would set the scale for the parameters $F_{ab}$ that appear in our formula, Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.4.32}, for the gauge boson masses, and would therefore presumably be of the order of 300 GeV. Since the increase of coupling with decreasing energy is logarithmic, a moderate difference in the beta-functions for technicolor and color can easily give rise to the three orders of magnitude difference between the energy scales where color and technicolor forces become strong.

Unfortunately, although technicolor provides a very attractive picture of the spontaneous breaking of $SU(2)\times U(1)$, it does not by itself offer a mechanism for giving masses to the quarks and leptons. For this reason it has been suggested to add additional `extended technicolor' gauge interactions with transformations that link quarks and techniquarks~\hyperref[ch21-ref-31]{[31]}. Such theories have potential problems with flavor-changing neutral current weak interactions, and though these problems may be surmounted, the added complications reduce their attractiveness. The question of elementary weakly coupled scalars versus dynamical symmetry breaking remains open.
