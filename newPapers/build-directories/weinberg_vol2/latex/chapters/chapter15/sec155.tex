\subsection{The De Witt--Faddeev--Popov Method}

Our formula \eqref{eq:15.4.16} for the path integral was derived in a gauge
that is convenient for canonical quantization, but the Feynman rules that
would be derived from this formula would hide the underlying rotational and
Lorentz invariance of the theory. In order to derive manifestly
Lorentz-invariant Feynman rules, we need to change the gauge.

We first note that Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.4.16} is (up to an unimportant constant
factor) a special case of a general class of functional integrals, of the form:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal I=\int\left[\prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x)\right]
  \mathcal G[\phi]B\bigl[f[\phi]\bigr]\operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[\phi],
  \tag{15.5.1}\label{eq:15.5.1}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_n(x)$ are a set of gauge and matter fields;
$\prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x)$ is a volume element; and $\mathcal G[\phi]$ is a
functional of the $\phi_n(x)$, satisfying the gauge-invariance condition:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal G[\phi_\lambda]\prod_{n,x}d\phi_{\lambda n}(x)
  =\mathcal G[\phi]\prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x),
  \tag{15.5.2}\label{eq:15.5.2}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{\lambda n}(x)$ is the result of operating on $\phi$ with a gauge
transformation having parameters $\lambda_a(x)$. (Usually when this is
satisfied both the functional $\mathcal G$ and the volume element are
separately invariant, but Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.2} is all we need here.) Also,
$f_a[\phi;x]$ is a non-gauge-invariant `gauge-fixing functional' of these
fields that also depends on $x$ and $a$; $B[f]$ is some numerical
functional defined for general functions $f_a(x)$ of $x$ and $a$;
and $\mathcal F$ is the `matrix':
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal F_{a x,b y}[\phi]
  \equiv\left.\frac{\delta f_a[\phi_\lambda;x]}
  {\delta\lambda^b(y)}\right|_{\lambda=0}.
  \tag{15.5.3}\label{eq:15.5.3}
\end{equation}
(In accordance with our usual notation for functionals of functions or of
functionals, $B\bigl[f[\phi]\bigr]$ is understood to depend on the values taken
by $f_a[\phi;x]$ for all values of the undisplayed variables $a$ and
$x$, with the displayed variable, the function $\phi_n(x)$, held fixed.)
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.1} does not represent the widest possible generalization of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.4.16}; we will see in Section 15.7 that there is a further
generalization that is needed for some purposes. We start here with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.1} because it will help to motivate the formalism of
Section 15.7, and it is adequate for dealing with non-Abelian gauge theories
in the most convenient gauges.

We now must check that the path integral \eqref{eq:15.4.16} is in fact a
special case of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.1}. In Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.4.16} the fields
$\phi_n(x)$ consist of both $A_{a\mu}(x)$ and matter fields $\psi_\ell(x)$,
and
\begin{equation}
  f_a[A,\psi;x]=A_{a3}(x),
  \tag{15.5.4}\label{eq:15.5.4}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  B[f]=\prod_{x,a}\delta\bigl(f_a(x)\bigr),
  \tag{15.5.5}\label{eq:15.5.5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal G[A,\psi]=\exp\{iI+\epsilon\text{ terms}\}
  \mathcal O_A\mathcal O_B\cdots,
  \tag{15.5.6}\label{eq:15.5.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x)
  =\left[\prod_{\ell,x}d\psi_\ell(x)\right]
  \left[\prod_{a,\mu,x}dA_a^\mu(x)\right].
  \tag{15.5.7}\label{eq:15.5.7}
\end{equation}
(We are now dropping the distinction between upper and lower indices
$a$, $b$, $\ldots$.) Comparison of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.4.16} with
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.5.1}--\eqref{eq:15.5.3} shows that these path integrals are
indeed the same, aside from the factor $\operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[\phi]$.
For the particular gauge-fixing functional \eqref{eq:15.5.4}, this factor is
field-independent: if $A_a^3(x)=0$, then the change in
$A_a^3(x)$ under a gauge transformation with parameters
$\lambda_a(x)$ is
\begin{equation*}
  A_{\lambda a}^3(x)=\partial_3\lambda_a(x)
  =\int d^4y\,\lambda_a(y)\partial_3\delta^4(x-y),
\end{equation*}
so that here Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.3} is the field-independent `matrix'
\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal F_{a x,b y}[\phi]
  =\delta_{ab}\partial_3\delta^4(x-y).
\end{equation*}
The determinant in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.1} is therefore also field-independent
in this gauge. As discussed in Chapter 9, field-independent factors in the
functional integral affect only the vacuum-fluctuation part of expectation
values and $S$-matrix elements, and so are irrelevant to the calculation of
the connected parts of the $S$-matrix.

The point of recognizing the functional integral \eqref{eq:15.4.16} for
non-Abelian gauge theories as a special case of the general path integral
\eqref{eq:15.5.1} is that in this form we may freely change the gauge.
Specifically, we have a theorem, that \emph{the integral \eqref{eq:15.5.1} is
actually independent (within broad limits) of the gauge-fixing functional
$f_a[\phi;x]$, and depends on the choice of the functional $B[f]$ only
through an irrelevant constant factor.}

\emph{Proof}: Replace the integration variable $\phi$ everywhere in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.1} with a new integration variable $\phi_\Lambda$, with
$\Lambda^a(x)$ any arbitrary (but fixed) set of gauge transformation
parameters:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal I=\int\left[\prod_{n,x}d\phi_{\Lambda n}(x)\right]
  \mathcal G[\phi_\Lambda]B\bigl[f[\phi_\Lambda]\bigr]
  \operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[\phi_\Lambda].
  \tag{15.5.8}\label{eq:15.5.8}
\end{equation}
(This step is a mathematical triviality, like changing an integral
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)dx$ to read
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)dy$, and does not yet make use of our assumptions
regarding gauge invariance.) Now use the assumed gauge invariance
\eqref{eq:15.5.2} of the measure $\prod d\phi$ times the functional
$\mathcal G[\phi]$ to rewrite this as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal I=\int\left[\prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x)\right]
  \mathcal G[\phi]B\bigl[f[\phi_\Lambda]\bigr]
  \operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[\phi_\Lambda].
  \tag{15.5.9}\label{eq:15.5.9}
\end{equation}
Since $\Lambda^a(x)$ was arbitrary, the left-hand side here cannot depend
on it. Integrating over $\Lambda^a(x)$ with some suitable
weight-functional $\rho[\Lambda]$ (to be chosen below) thus gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal I\int\left[\prod_{a,x}d\Lambda^a(x)\right]\rho[\Lambda]
  =\int\left[\prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x)\right]\mathcal G[\phi]C[\phi],
  \tag{15.5.10}\label{eq:15.5.10}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  C[\phi]\equiv\int\left[\prod_{a,x}d\Lambda^a(x)\right]
  \rho[\Lambda]B\bigl[f[\phi_\Lambda]\bigr]
  \operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[\phi_\Lambda].
  \tag{15.5.11}\label{eq:15.5.11}
\end{equation}
Now, Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.3} gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal F_{a x,b y}[\phi_\Lambda]
  \equiv\left.\frac{\delta f_a[(\phi_\Lambda)_\lambda;x]}
  {\delta\lambda^b(y)}\right|_{\lambda=0}.
  \tag{15.5.12}\label{eq:15.5.12}
\end{equation}
We are assuming that these transformations form a group; that is, we may
write the result of performing the gauge transformation with parameters
$\Lambda^a(x)$ followed by the gauge transformation with parameters
$\lambda^a(x)$ as the action of a single `product' gauge transformation
with parameters $\widetilde\Lambda^a(x;\Lambda,\lambda)$,
\begin{equation}
  (\phi_\Lambda)_\lambda=\phi_{\widetilde\Lambda(\Lambda,\lambda)}.
  \tag{15.5.13}\label{eq:15.5.13}
\end{equation}
Using the chain rule of partial (functional) differentiation, we have then
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal F_{a x,b y}[\phi_\Lambda]
  =\int\mathcal J_{a x,c z}[\phi,\Lambda]
  \mathcal R^{c z}{}_{b y}[\Lambda]d^4z,
  \tag{15.5.14}\label{eq:15.5.14}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{a x,c z}[\phi,\Lambda]
  \equiv\left.\frac{\delta f_a[\phi_{\widetilde\Lambda};x]}
  {\delta\widetilde\Lambda^c(z)}\right|_{\widetilde\Lambda=\Lambda}
  =\frac{\delta f_a[\phi_\Lambda;x]}{\delta\Lambda^c(z)}
  \tag{15.5.15}\label{eq:15.5.15}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal R^{c z}{}_{b y}[\Lambda]
  \equiv\left.\frac{\delta\widetilde\Lambda^c(z;\Lambda,\lambda)}
  {\delta\lambda^b(y)}\right|_{\lambda=0}.
  \tag{15.5.16}\label{eq:15.5.16}
\end{equation}
It follows that
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[\phi_\Lambda]
  =\operatorname{Det}\mathcal J[\phi,\Lambda]
  \operatorname{Det}\mathcal R[\Lambda].
  \tag{15.5.17}\label{eq:15.5.17}
\end{equation}
We note that $\operatorname{Det}\mathcal J[\phi,\Lambda]$ is nothing but the
Jacobian of the transformation of integration variables from the
$\Lambda^a(x)$ to (for a fixed $\phi$) the $f_a[\phi_\Lambda;x]$.
Hence, if we choose the weight-function $\rho(\Lambda)$ as
\begin{equation}
  \rho(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Det}\mathcal R[\Lambda]}
  \tag{15.5.18}\label{eq:15.5.18}
\end{equation}
then
\begin{align}
  C[\phi]
  &=\int\left[\prod_{a,x}d\Lambda^a(x)\right]
  \operatorname{Det}\mathcal J[\phi,\Lambda]
  B\bigl[f[\phi_\Lambda]\bigr]
  \notag\\
  &=\int\left[\prod_{a,x}df_a(x)\right]B[f]=C,
  \tag{15.5.19}\label{eq:15.5.19}
\end{align}
which is clearly independent of $\phi$. (Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.18} may be
recognized by the reader as giving the invariant (Haar) measure on the space
of group parameters.) We have then at last
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal I=
  \frac{C\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{n,x}d\phi_n(x)\right]
  \mathcal G[\phi]}
  {\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{a,x}d\Lambda^a(x)\right]
  \rho[\Lambda]}.
  \tag{15.5.20}\label{eq:15.5.20}
\end{equation}
This is clearly independent of our choice of $f_a[\phi;x]$, which has
been reduced to a mere variable of integration, and it depends on $B[f]$ only
through the constant $C$, as was to be proved.

Before proceeding with the applications of this theorem, we should pause to
note a tricky point in the derivation. The integrals in the numerator and
denominator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.20} are both ill-defined for the same reason.
Since $\mathcal G[\phi]$ is assumed to be gauge-invariant, its integral over
$\phi$ cannot possibly converge; the integrand is constant along all `orbits,'
obtained by sending $\phi$ into $\phi_\lambda$ with all possible
$\lambda^a(x)$. Likewise, the integrand in the denominator is divergent,
because $\rho(\Lambda)\prod d\Lambda$ is nothing but the usual invariant
volume element for integrating over the group, and this is also constant along
`orbits' $\Lambda\longrightarrow\widetilde\Lambda(\Lambda,\lambda)$. This
divergence can be eliminated in both the numerator and denominator of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.20} by formulating the theory on a finite spacetime lattice,
in which case the volume of the gauge group is just the volume of the global
Lie group itself times the number of lattice sites. Because the gauge-fixing
factor $B[f]$ eliminates this divergence in the original definition
\eqref{eq:15.5.1} of the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.20}, we may
presume that, as the number of lattice sites goes to infinity, it cancels
between the numerator and denominator of the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.20}.

Now to the point. We have seen that the vacuum expectation value
\eqref{eq:15.4.16} in axial gauge is given by a functional integral of the
general form \eqref{eq:15.5.1}. Armed with the above theorem, we conclude then
that
\begin{align}
  \bigl\langle T\{\mathcal O_A\mathcal O_B\cdots\}\bigr\rangle_V
  \propto{}&\int\left[\prod_{\ell,x}d\psi_\ell(x)\right]
  \left[\prod_{a,\mu,x}dA_a^\mu(x)\right]
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\mathcal O_A\mathcal O_B\cdots\exp\{iI+\epsilon\text{ terms}\}
  B\bigl[f[A,\psi]\bigr]\operatorname{Det}\mathcal F[A,\psi]
  \tag{15.5.21}\label{eq:15.5.21}
\end{align}
for (almost) any choice of $f_a[A,\psi;x]$ and $B[f]$. We are now
therefore free to use Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.21} to derive the Feynman rules in a
more convenient gauge.

The path integrals that we understand how to calculate are of Gaussians times
polynomials, so we will generally take
\begin{equation}
  B[f]=\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2\xi}\int d^4x\,f_a(x)f_a(x)\right)
  \tag{15.5.22}\label{eq:15.5.22}
\end{equation}
with arbitrary real parameter $\xi$. With this choice, the effect of the factor
$B$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.21} is just to add a term to the effective Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_{\mathrm{EFF}}=\mathcal L-\frac{1}{2\xi}f_a f_a.
  \tag{15.5.23}\label{eq:15.5.23}
\end{equation}
The simplest Lorentz-invariant choice of the gauge-fixing function $f_a$
is the same as in electrodynamics:
\begin{equation}
  f_a=\partial_\mu A_a^\mu.
  \tag{15.5.24}\label{eq:15.5.24}
\end{equation}
The bare gauge-field propagator can then be calculated just as in quantum
electrodynamics. The free-vector-boson part of the effective action can be
written
\begin{align*}
  I_{0A}={}&-\int d^4x\left[\frac14
  (\partial_\mu A_{a\nu}-\partial_\nu A_{a\mu})
  (\partial^\mu A_a^\nu-\partial^\nu A_a^\mu)
  +\frac{1}{2\xi}(\partial_\mu A_a^\mu)
  (\partial_\nu A_a^\nu)+\epsilon\text{ terms}\right]
  \\
  ={}&-\frac12\int d^4x\,
  \mathcal D_{a\mu x,b\nu y}A_a^\mu(x)A_b^\nu(y),
\end{align*}
where
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal D_{a\mu x,b\nu y}
  ={}&\eta_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^\lambda\partial y_\lambda}
  \delta^4(x-y)
  -\left(1-\frac1\xi\right)
  \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^\mu\partial y^\nu}\delta^4(x-y)
  +\epsilon\text{ terms}
  \\
  ={}&(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4p\left[\eta_{\mu\nu}(p^2-i\epsilon)
  -\left(1-\frac1\xi\right)p_\mu p_\nu\right]e^{ip\cdot(x-y)}.
\end{align*}
Taking the reciprocal of the matrix in square brackets, we find the propagator:
\begin{align}
  \Delta_{a\mu,b\nu}(x,y)
  &=(\mathcal D^{-1})_{a\mu x,b\nu y}
  \notag\\
  &=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4p\left[\eta_{\mu\nu}
  +(\xi-1)\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{p^2}\right]
 \frac{e^{ip\cdot(x-y)}}{p^2-i\epsilon}.
  \tag{15.5.25}\label{eq:15.5.25}
\end{align}
This is a generalization of both Landau and Feynman gauges, which are
recovered by taking $\xi=0$ and $\xi=1$, respectively. For $\xi\to0$, the
functional \eqref{eq:15.5.22} oscillates very rapidly except near $f_a=0$,
so this functional acts like a delta-function imposing the Landau gauge
condition $\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$, leading naturally to a propagator satisfying
the corresponding condition $\partial^\mu\Delta_{a\mu,b\nu}=0$. For
non-zero values of $\xi$ the functional $B[f]$ does not pick out gauge fields
satisfying any specific gauge condition on the field $A_{a\mu}$, but it is
common to refer to the propagator \eqref{eq:15.5.25} as being in a `generalized
Feynman gauge' or `generalized $\xi$-gauge.' It is often a good strategy to
calculate physical amplitudes with $\xi$ left arbitrary, and then at the end of
the calculation check that the results are $\xi$-independent.

With one qualification, the Feynman rules are now obvious: the contributions
of vertices are to be read off from the interaction terms in the original
Lagrangian $\mathcal L$, with gauge-field propagators given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.25}, and matter-field propagators calculated as before. To
be specific, the trilinear interaction term in $\mathcal L$
\begin{equation*}
  -\frac12C_{abc}
  (\partial_\mu A_{a\nu}-\partial_\nu A_{a\mu})
  A_b^\mu A_c^\nu
\end{equation*}
corresponds to a vertex to which are attached three vector boson lines. If
these lines carry (incoming) momenta $p$, $q$, $k$ and Lorentz and gauge-field
indices $\mu\alpha$, $\nu\beta$, $\lambda\gamma$, then according to the
momentum-space Feynman rules, the contribution of such a vertex to the
integrand is
\begin{multline}
  i(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p+q+k)[-iC_{abc}]
  \bigl[p_\nu\eta_{\mu\lambda}-p_\lambda\eta_{\mu\nu}
  +q_\lambda\eta_{\nu\mu}-q_\mu\eta_{\nu\lambda}\\
  {}+k_\mu\eta_{\lambda\nu}-k_\nu\eta_{\lambda\mu}\bigr].
  \tag{15.5.26}\label{eq:15.5.26}
\end{multline}
Also, the $A^4$ interaction term in $\mathcal L$,
\begin{equation*}
  -\frac14C_{eab}C_{ecd}
  A_{a\mu}A_{b\nu}A_c^\mu A_d^\nu,
\end{equation*}
corresponds to a vertex to which are attached four vector boson lines. If these
lines carry (incoming) momenta $p$, $q$, $k$, $\ell$, and Lorentz and gauge
indices $\mu\alpha$, $\nu\beta$, $\rho\gamma$, and $\sigma\delta$, then the
contribution of such a vertex to the integrand is
\begin{align}
  i(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p+q+k+\ell)\mathbin{\cdot}
  \bigl[{}&-C_{eab}C_{ecd}
  (\eta_{\mu\rho}\eta_{\nu\sigma}-\eta_{\mu\sigma}\eta_{\nu\rho})
  \notag\\
  &-C_{eac}C_{edb}
  (\eta_{\mu\sigma}\eta_{\rho\nu}-\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta_{\sigma\rho})
  \notag\\
  &-C_{ead}C_{ebc}
  (\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta_{\rho\sigma}-\eta_{\mu\rho}\eta_{\sigma\nu})\bigr].
  \tag{15.5.27}\label{eq:15.5.27}
\end{align}
(Recall that the structure constants $C_{abc}$ contain coupling
constant factors, so the factors \eqref{eq:15.5.26} and \eqref{eq:15.5.27} are
respectively of first and second order in coupling constants.)

The one complication in the Feynman rules with which we have not yet dealt is
the presence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.21} of the factor
$\operatorname{Det}\mathcal F$, which for general gauges is \emph{not} a
constant. We now turn to a consideration of this factor.
