\subsection{Pions as Goldstone Bosons}
\label{sec:19.4}

The classic example of a broken symmetry in elementary particle physics is the approximate symmetry of strong interactions known as chiral $SU(2)\times SU(2)$. According to our present understanding, this symmetry arises because there are two quark fields, $u$ and $d$, that happen to have relatively small masses. (An estimate is given in Section 19.7.) In the approximation that the $u$ and $d$ are massless, the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:18.7.5} of quantum chromodynamics is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\bar u\gamma^\mu D_\mu u-\bar d\gamma^\mu D_\mu d-\cdots,
\tag{19.4.1}\label{eq:19.4.1}
\end{equation}
where $D_\mu$ is a color-gauge-covariant derivative (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.1.10}) and `$\cdots$' refers to terms involving only gluon fields and/or other quark flavors, but not $u$ or $d$. This Lagrangian is invariant under the transformations
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\d\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\exp\bigl(i\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\,V}\cdot\mathbf{t}
+i\gamma_5\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\,A}\cdot\mathbf{t}\bigr)
\begin{pmatrix}u\\d\end{pmatrix},
\tag{19.4.2}\label{eq:19.4.2}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{t}$ is the three-vector\footnote{This edition uses boldface for
all three-vectors; isotopic-spin and ordinary spatial vectors are
distinguished by context.} of isospin matrices
\[
t_1=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
t_2=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
t_3=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},
\]
and $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\,V}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\,A}$ are independent real three-vectors.\footnote{The Lagrangian \eqref{eq:19.4.1} has this symmetry because $\overline{\gamma_5q}\gamma^\mu=-\bar q\gamma_5\gamma^\mu=+\bar q\gamma^\mu\gamma_5$. The Lagrangian also has two other continuous internal symmetries. One is baryon conservation, the invariance under a common phase transformation of the $u$ and $d$ quark fields. This is unbroken and commutes with the other symmetries, so it does not affect our discussion in this section. The other symmetry is invariance under multiplication of the quark doublet with $\exp(i\alpha\gamma_5)$. As discussed in Section 23.5, this $U(1)$ symmetry is strongly intrinsically broken by non-perturbative effects associated with instantons.} This Lie algebra may be written in terms of two commuting $SU(2)$ subalgebras that act respectively only on the left- and right-handed parts of the quark fields, with generators
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{t}_L=\frac12(\mathbf{1}+\gamma_5)\mathbf{t},
\qquad
\mathbf{t}_R=\frac12(\mathbf{1}-\gamma_5)\mathbf{t}.
\tag{19.4.3}\label{eq:19.4.3}
\end{equation}
satisfying the commutation relations
\begin{align}
[t_{Li},t_{Lj}]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}t_{Lk},
\tag{19.4.4}\label{eq:19.4.4}\\
[t_{Ri},t_{Rj}]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}t_{Rk},
\tag{19.4.5}\label{eq:19.4.5}\\
[t_{Li},t_{Rj}]&=0.
\tag{19.4.6}\label{eq:19.4.6}
\end{align}
The underlying symmetry group is therefore identified as $SU(2)\times SU(2)$. It has another obvious $SU(2)$ subgroup, consisting of ordinary isospin transformations with $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\,A}=0$, and generators
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}_L+\mathbf{t}_R.
\tag{19.4.7}\label{eq:19.4.7}
\end{equation}
The algebra of $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ may be written in terms of $\mathbf{t}$ and another triplet of generators:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{t}_L-\mathbf{t}_R=\gamma_5\mathbf{t}
\tag{19.4.8}\label{eq:19.4.8}
\end{equation}
with commutation relations
\begin{align}
[t_i,t_j]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}t_k,
\tag{19.4.9}\label{eq:19.4.9}\\
[t_i,x_j]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}x_k,
\tag{19.4.10}\label{eq:19.4.10}\\
[x_i,x_j]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}t_k.
\tag{19.4.11}\label{eq:19.4.11}
\end{align}
We will see that the $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry is spontaneously broken, while its isotopic spin subgroup generated by the $\mathbf{t}$ is an ordinary unbroken (though approximate) symmetry.

By Noether's method (see, e.g., Section 7.3) we may derive from the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:19.4.1} the conserved vector and axial-vector currents
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V}^\mu=i\bar q\gamma^\mu\mathbf{t}\,q,
\qquad
\mathbf{A}^\mu=i\bar q\gamma^\mu\gamma_5\mathbf{t}\,q,
\tag{19.4.12}\label{eq:19.4.12}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\partial_\mu\mathbf{V}^\mu=\partial_\mu\mathbf{A}^\mu=0,
\tag{19.4.13}\label{eq:19.4.13}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the quark doublet,
\begin{equation}
q\equiv\begin{pmatrix}u\\d\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{19.4.14}\label{eq:19.4.14}
\end{equation}
Their associated charges are the generators respectively of isospin and of the remaining symmetries
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{T}=\int d^3x\,\mathbf{V}^0,
\tag{19.4.15}\label{eq:19.4.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}=\int d^3x\,\mathbf{A}^0.
\tag{19.4.16}\label{eq:19.4.16}
\end{equation}
The currents \eqref{eq:19.4.12} are normalized so that the quantum operators $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{X}$ satisfy the same commutation relations as the matrices $\mathbf{t}$ and $\mathbf{x}$:
\begin{align}
[T_i,T_j]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}T_k,
\tag{19.4.17}\label{eq:19.4.17}\\
[T_i,X_j]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}X_k,
\tag{19.4.18}\label{eq:19.4.18}\\
[X_i,X_j]&=i\epsilon_{ijk}T_k.
\tag{19.4.19}\label{eq:19.4.19}
\end{align}
Acting on the quark fields, these operators induce the transformation \eqref{eq:19.4.2}, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{T},q]=-\mathbf{t}\,q,
\tag{19.4.20}\label{eq:19.4.20}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{X},q]=-\mathbf{x}\,q.
\tag{19.4.21}\label{eq:19.4.21}
\end{equation}

This symmetry, if exact and unbroken, would require any one-hadron state $\ket{h}$ to be degenerate with another state $\mathbf{X}\ket{h}$ of opposite parity and equal spin, baryon number, and strangeness.\footnote{One way to satisfy this condition is for the hadron to have zero mass, with two states $\ket{\pm}$ of helicity $\pm\frac12$ and equal spin, baryon number, and strangeness; the states $\ket{+}+\ket{-}$ and $\ket{+}-\ket{-}$ would then have opposite parity. It is not true that an unbroken chiral symmetry necessarily implies a zero nucleon mass, unless we make further assumptions about the matrix elements of the axial-vector current. But as we shall see in Section 22.5, an exact unbroken chiral symmetry would in fact require that some baryons be massless.} No such parity doubling is seen in the hadron spectrum, so we are forced to conclude that if the chiral symmetry $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is a good approximation at all, then it must be spontaneously broken to its isotopic spin $SU(2)$ subgroup. In this case the operator $\mathbf{X}$ takes a one-hadron state $\ket{h}$ into a hadron $h$ plus a massless pseudoscalar Goldstone boson, so there is no need for parity doubling of the hadron spectrum.

The question of whether quantum chromodynamics actually exhibits such a pattern of symmetry breaking involves all the complications of strong interaction dynamics. As we shall see in Section 19.9, there are general grounds for believing that the isospin $SU(2)$ is not spontaneously broken in quantum chromodynamics, but it is much more difficult to show that the chiral part of $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is spontaneously broken. (But according to an argument given in Section 22.5, the $SU(3)\times SU(3)$ symmetry of quantum chromodynamics with three massless quark flavors must be spontaneously broken.) It was something of a breakthrough in the 1960s to realize that one does not have to have a detailed understanding of the mechanism of the breaking of chiral symmetry; we derive the most interesting consequences of this symmetry breaking by simply assuming that $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is spontaneously broken to $SU(2)$.

The $u$ and $d$ quarks have small but nonzero masses, so the $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry is not exact. A broken \emph{approximate} chiral symmetry entails the existence of an approximately massless Goldstone boson with the same quantum numbers as the broken symmetry generator $\mathbf{X}$: it must be a state of negative parity, zero spin, unit isospin, and zero baryon number and strangeness. In fact the lightest of all hadrons is the pion, which has precisely these quantum numbers, so we are led to identify the pion as the Goldstone boson associated with the spontaneous breaking of approximate chiral symmetry. As we shall see below, it is $m_\pi^2$ rather than $m_\pi$ that is proportional to a linear combination of $m_u$ and $m_d$, and $m_\pi^2/m_N^2\simeq0.022$ is very small, so the consequences we derive from spontaneously broken $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ should be reasonably accurate.

In exploring the consequences of chiral symmetry for pion interactions, it is very useful to note that although the chiral symmetry of the strong interactions does not depend in any way on the existence of weak interactions, the symmetry currents $\mathbf{V}^\mu$ and $\mathbf{A}^\mu$ happen to be the currents entering into strangeness-conserving semileptonic weak interactions like nuclear beta decay. As we shall see in Section 21.3, the standard model of electroweak interactions requires that the effective Lagrangian for these interactions at low energy must take the form:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{wk}}
=-\frac{iG_{\mathrm{wk}}}{\sqrt2}
\bigl(V_+^\lambda+A_+^\lambda\bigr)
\sum_\ell\bar\ell\gamma_\lambda(\mathbf{1}+\gamma_5)\nu_\ell
+\mathrm{H.c.}
\tag{19.4.22}\label{eq:19.4.22}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ runs over the renormalized fields of the three charged leptons $e$, $\mu$ and $\tau$; $\nu_\ell$ runs over the renormalized fields of the associated neutrinos; and $V_\pm^\lambda$ and $A_\pm^\lambda$ are the charge changing currents
\begin{equation}
V_\pm^\lambda=V_1^\lambda\pm iV_2^\lambda,
\qquad
A_\pm^\lambda=A_1^\lambda\pm iA_2^\lambda.
\tag{19.4.23}\label{eq:19.4.23}
\end{equation}
The constant\footnote{$G_{\mathrm{wk}}$ is related to the conventional Fermi coupling constant $G_F$ and the Cabibbo angle $\theta_C$ by $G_{\mathrm{wk}}=G_F\cos\theta_C$; see Section 21.3.} $G_{\mathrm{wk}}$ may be measured from the rates of beta transitions between states of zero spin within the same isotopic multiplet, such as the decays $\pi^+\to\pi^0+e^++\nu_e$ and ${}^{14}O\to{}^{14}N^*+e^++\nu_e$. The momentum transfer in these transitions is very small, so parity conservation (in strong interactions) and rotational invariance tell us that only the matrix elements of $\int d^3x\,V_-^0=T_1-iT_2$ enter in the $S$-matrix elements for these decays. This operator has matrix elements between states in a given isospin multiplet that are just known Clebsch--Gordan coefficients, so from the rates for these `$0\to0$' processes we may calculate a value~\hyperref[ch19-ref-9]{[9]} for the coupling in Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.22}: $G_{\mathrm{wk}}\simeq1.14959(38)\cdot10^{-5}\ \mathrm{GeV}^{-2}$. On the other hand, in the process of pion decay, $\pi^+\to\mu^++\nu_\mu$, the only current matrix element we need is the matrix element of $A_-^\mu$ between a one-pion state and the vacuum:
\begin{equation}
\bra{\Omega}A_i^\mu(x)\ket{\pi_j}
=\frac{iF_\pi\delta_{ij}p_\pi^\mu
e^{ip_\pi\cdot x}}
{2(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}}.
\tag{19.4.24}\label{eq:19.4.24}
\end{equation}
which is completely known except for the factor $F_\pi$. The rate for pion decay turns out to be
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(\pi\to\mu+\nu)
=\frac{G_{\mathrm{wk}}^2F_\pi^2m_\mu^2
(m_\pi^2-m_\mu^2)^2}
{16\pi m_\pi^3}.
\tag{19.4.25}\label{eq:19.4.25}
\end{equation}
From the known rate for $\pi^+\to\mu^++\nu_\mu$, $\Gamma=(2.6033(24)\cdot10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s})^{-1}$, and the above value of $G_{\mathrm{wk}}$, one finds that\footnote{It is common to encounter a pion decay constant $f_\pi$, which in terms of the $F_\pi$ used here is variously defined as $F_\pi$, $F_\pi/\sqrt2$, or $F_\pi/2$.}
\begin{equation}
F_\pi\simeq184\ \mathrm{MeV}.
\tag{19.4.26}\label{eq:19.4.26}
\end{equation}

Now let us consider the matrix element of $\mathbf{A}^\mu$ between one-nucleon states. This is of interest in its own right, and as discussed in Section 19.2, it provides information we need to calculate the emission of low energy pions in collisions of nucleons. Following the same reasoning as used for the electromagnetic current in Section 10.6, one finds that Lorentz invariance and parity conservation require this matrix element to take the form\footnote{The currents $A_\pm^\mu$ in the standard model have charge conjugation properties that make the coefficient $h(q^2)$ vanish. It is possible that there are `second-class' terms~\hyperref[ch19-ref-10]{[10]} in the weak currents with opposite charge conjugation properties that give a non-vanishing $h(q^2)$, but there is no evidence for such terms. As we shall see, keeping the $h(q^2)$ term here has no effect on the inferences drawn from chiral symmetry.}
\begin{align}
\bra{p}A_\pm^\mu(x)\ket{n}
={}&(2\pi)^{-3}e^{iq\cdot x}\,
\bar u_p\Bigl[
-i\gamma^\mu\gamma_5 f(q^2)
+q^\mu\gamma_5 g(q^2)
\notag\\
&\hspace{44mm}
+i q_\nu[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]\gamma_5h(q^2)
\Bigr]u_n,
\tag{19.4.27}\label{eq:19.4.27}
\end{align}
where $q=p_n-p_p$. In the approximation in which the $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry is exact, the conservation of current requires that
\begin{equation}
q_\mu\bra{p}A_+^\mu(x)\ket{n}=0.
\tag{19.4.28}\label{eq:19.4.28}
\end{equation}
Using the defining equations for the Dirac spinors $u_p$ and $u_n$,
\[
\bar u_p(i\sl{p}_p+m_N)=(i\sl{p}_n+m_N)u_n=0,
\]
we see that
\[
q_\mu\bar u_p[-i\gamma^\mu\gamma_5]u_n
=-2m_N\bar u_p\gamma_5u_n
\]
and so Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.28} requires that
\begin{equation}
2m_Nf(q^2)=q^2g(q^2).
\tag{19.4.29}\label{eq:19.4.29}
\end{equation}
If $g(q^2)$ had no singularity at $q^2=0$, then \eqref{eq:19.4.29} would require that either $m_N=0$, which is certainly not true, or that $f(0)=0$, which is not true either. In fact, the quantity $f(0)$ is measured in low energy nuclear beta decays, like neutron decay, where it is usually called $g_A$; it is found to have the value
\begin{equation}
f(0)\equiv g_A=1.2573(28).
\tag{19.4.30}\label{eq:19.4.30}
\end{equation}
The fact that neither $m_N$ nor $f(0)=g_A$ is small requires that in the limit of exact $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry, $g(q^2)$ must have a pole as $q^2\to0$:
\begin{equation}
g(q^2)\longrightarrow\frac{2m_Ng_A}{q^2}.
\tag{19.4.31}\label{eq:19.4.31}
\end{equation}
Such a pole is naturally provided by the massless pion that would be required by the spontaneous breakdown of an exact $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry. Suppose that the pion couples to the one-nucleon state as if the interaction Lagrangian were\footnote{This is the conventional definition of the pseudoscalar pion-nucleon coupling $G_{\pi N}$. The factor 2 is introduced here to cancel the $1/2$ in the isospin matrices.} $-2iG_{\pi N}\boldsymbol{\pi}\cdot\bar N\gamma_5\mathbf{t}\,N$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.24} tells us that the matrix element of the current between a one-pion state and the vacuum is the same as if there were a term in $\mathbf{A}^\mu(x)$ of the form $F_\pi\partial^\mu\boldsymbol{\pi}/2$. Therefore in the limit $q^2\to0$ the matrix element \eqref{eq:19.4.27} has the pole
\[
\bra{p}A_+^\mu(x)\ket{n}
\longrightarrow
\left[\frac{e^{iq\cdot x}}{(2\pi)^3}\right]
\left[i(2\pi)^4\,2G_{\pi N}\bar u_p(i\gamma_5)u_n\right]
\left[\frac{-i}{(2\pi)^4q^2}\right]
\left[iq^\mu F_\pi/2\right].
\]
Comparing with Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.27}, we see that one-pion exchange gives the function $g(q^2)$ a pole
\begin{equation}
g(q^2)\longrightarrow\frac{G_{\pi N}F_\pi}{q^2}
\tag{19.4.32}\label{eq:19.4.32}
\end{equation}
for $q^2\to0$. Putting together Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.4.31} and \eqref{eq:19.4.32}, we find
\begin{equation}
G_{\pi N}=\frac{2m_Ng_A}{F_\pi}.
\tag{19.4.33}\label{eq:19.4.33}
\end{equation}
This is the famous Goldberger--Treiman relation~\hyperref[ch19-ref-11]{[11]}. It works reasonably well; taking $m_N=(m_p+m_n)/2=938.9\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $g_A=1.257$, and $F_\pi=184\ \mathrm{MeV}$ gives $G_{\pi N}\simeq12.7$, in fair agreement with the value\footnote{The textbook~\hyperref[ch19-ref-12]{[12]} value is $G_{\pi N}^2/4\pi=14.3$, or $G_{\pi N}=13.4$. More recently, a high precision study~\hyperref[ch19-ref-13]{[13]} of neutron-proton charge-exchange scattering at $162\ \mathrm{MeV}$ has given a value $G_{\pi N}^2/4\pi=14.6\pm0.3$, or $G_{\pi N}=13.5$.} $G_{\pi N}=13.5$ measured in various ways (including the effects of the one-pion pole in nucleon-nuclear scattering and the one-nucleon pole in pion-nucleon scattering.)

In the real world the pion is not massless and the $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry is not exact (even before being spontaneously broken.) This circumstance may be analyzed using the general formalism presented in the previous section. The Lagrangian \eqref{eq:19.4.1} yields a symmetry-breaking term in the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
H_1=m_u\bar u u+m_d\bar d d
=(m_u+m_d)\Phi_4^+
+(m_u-m_d)\Phi_3^-,
\tag{19.4.34}\label{eq:19.4.34}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\Phi_4^+=\frac12(\bar u u+\bar d d),
\qquad
\Phi_3^-=\frac12(\bar u u-\bar d d).
\tag{19.4.35}\label{eq:19.4.35}
\end{equation}
The operators $\Phi_4^+$ and $\Phi_3^-$ are spatial scalars and, as this notation is meant to suggest, they transform under $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ as components of independent chiral four-vectors $\Phi_n^\pm$:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\, +}=i\bar q\gamma_5\mathbf{t}\,q,
\qquad
\Phi_4^+=\frac12\bar q q,
\tag{19.4.36}\label{eq:19.4.36}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\, -}=\bar q\mathbf{t}\,q,
\qquad
\Phi_4^-=-\frac12i\bar q\gamma_5q.
\tag{19.4.37}\label{eq:19.4.37}
\end{equation}
These are chiral four-vectors, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{T},\Phi_n^\pm]
=-\sum_m(\mathbf{\mathcal T})_{nm}\Phi_m^\pm,
\tag{19.4.38}\label{eq:19.4.38}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{X},\Phi_n^\pm]
=-\sum_m(\mathbf{\mathcal X})_{nm}\Phi_m^\pm,
\tag{19.4.39}\label{eq:19.4.39}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{\mathcal T}$ and $\mathbf{\mathcal X}$ are Hermitian $4\times4$ matrices that furnish the four-vector representation of the algebra of $SU(2)\times SU(2)\equiv SO(4)$:
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal T_a)_{bc}=-i\epsilon_{abc},
\qquad
(\mathcal T_a)_{b4}=(\mathcal T_a)_{4b}
=(\mathcal T_a)_{44}=0,
\tag{19.4.40}\label{eq:19.4.40}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal X_a)_{b4}=-(\mathcal X_a)_{4b}=-i\delta_{ab},
\qquad
(\mathcal X_c)_{ab}=(\mathcal X_c)_{44}=0.
\tag{19.4.41}\label{eq:19.4.41}
\end{equation}
This notation makes it easy to see that the vacuum alignment conditions for generators $T_1$, $T_2$, $X_1$, $X_2$, and $X_3$ respectively take the form
\begin{align}
0
&=\langle\Phi_2^-\rangle_0
=\langle\Phi_1^-\rangle_0
=\langle\Phi_1^+\rangle_0
=\langle\Phi_2^+\rangle_0
\notag\\
&=(m_u+m_d)\langle\Phi_3^+\rangle_0
+(m_d-m_u)\langle\Phi_4^-\rangle_0.
\tag{19.4.42}\label{eq:19.4.42}
\end{align}
We have been assuming that in the absence of $u$ and $d$ quark masses, the symmetry $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is spontaneously broken in such a way as to preserve unbroken the $SU(2)$ symmetry generated by $\mathbf{T}$ as well as parity, in which case $\langle\Phi_n^+\rangle_0$ points in the four-direction and $\langle\Phi_n^-\rangle_0=0$ vanishes, so that the conditions \eqref{eq:19.4.42} are satisfied. But with $m_u=m_d=0$, we can find other symmetry-breaking solutions with other definitions of parity by subjecting this one to an arbitrary $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ transformation. Thus in the absence of $u$ and $d$ quark masses, there would be no way to tell in what direction $\langle\Phi_n^+\rangle_0$ should point, or which $SU(2)$ subgroup of $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is left unbroken, though in all cases $\langle\Phi_n^-\rangle_0=0$. The vacuum alignment condition \eqref{eq:19.4.42} tells us that, with the symmetry broken intrinsically by the perturbation \eqref{eq:19.4.34} and spontaneously in such a way that $\langle\Phi_n^-\rangle_0=0$, the vacuum must `line up' in such a way that $\langle\Phi_n^+\rangle_0\ne0$ points in the four-direction, so that the unbroken $SU(2)$ symmetry is ordinary isospin.

This formalism may be used to calculate the pion mass. From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.4.39}--\eqref{eq:19.4.41} we find
\begin{equation}
[X_a,[X_b,\Phi_4^+]]=\delta_{ab}\Phi_4^+,
\qquad
[X_a,[X_b,\Phi_3^-]]=\Phi_a^-\delta_{b3}.
\tag{19.4.43}\label{eq:19.4.43}
\end{equation}
Also, isospin invariance tells us that the symmetry-breaking parameter $F_{ab}$ introduced in Section 19.2 is proportional to $\delta_{ab}$, with a proportionality factor that according to \eqref{eq:19.4.24} is just $F_\pi/2$, so
\begin{equation}
F_{ab}=\delta_{ab}F_\pi/2.
\tag{19.4.44}\label{eq:19.4.44}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.3.20} thus gives the pion mass matrix as
\begin{equation}
M_{ab}^2=\delta_{ab}m_\pi^2,
\tag{19.4.45}\label{eq:19.4.45}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
m_\pi^2=4(m_u+m_d)\langle\Phi_4^+\rangle_0/F_\pi^2.
\tag{19.4.46}\label{eq:19.4.46}
\end{equation}
It is striking that the masses of the charged and neutral pions turn out to be equal, even though we have made no assumption about the ratio of $m_u$ and $m_d$. We shall see below that this ratio is not near unity; isospin is a good quantum number not because the $u$ and $d$ quark masses are nearly equal, but because they are \emph{small}. Also, as promised, we see that it is the \emph{square} of the pion mass that is proportional to the quark masses, so the quark masses should be quite small. (See Section 19.7 for an estimate.) The observed pion mass difference arises not from the $u$--$d$ quark mass difference, but from electromagnetism. Indeed, the pion isospin multiplet is the only one whose mass difference has been successfully calculated on the basis of one-photon exchange alone~\hyperref[ch19-ref-14]{[14]}.

Of course, even with quark masses taken into account, Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.24} can still be used to define $F_\pi$; its divergence gives
\begin{equation}
\bra{\Omega}\partial_\mu A_i^\mu(x)\ket{\pi_j}
=\frac{F_\pi\delta_{ij}m_\pi^2e^{ip_\pi\cdot x}}
{2(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_\pi^0}}.
\tag{19.4.47}\label{eq:19.4.47}
\end{equation}
Instead of assuming that $\partial_\mu\mathbf{A}^\mu$ vanishes, we can now assume that it is \emph{small}, of order $m_\pi^2$, except where a pion pole compensates for the smallness of $m_\pi^2$. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.47}, the behavior of matrix elements of $\partial_\mu\mathbf{A}^\mu$ near a one-pion pole is the same as if $\partial_\mu\mathbf{A}^\mu$ were $F_\pi m_\pi^2$ times a properly renormalized pion field. For instance, the one-nucleon matrix element of $\partial_\mu A_+^\mu$ should be
\begin{align}
\bra{p}\partial_\mu A_+^\mu(0)\ket{n}
\simeq{}&
\frac{F_\pi m_\pi^2}{2}
\left[\frac{-i}{(2\pi)^4(q^2+m_\pi^2)}\right]
\notag\\
&\cdot
\left[i(2\pi)^4\,2G_{\pi N}
\bar u_p(-i\gamma_5)u_n\right].
\tag{19.4.48}\label{eq:19.4.48}
\end{align}
so in terms of the form-factors in \eqref{eq:19.4.27}
\begin{equation}
q^2g(q^2)-2m_Nf(q^2)
\simeq-\frac{G_{\pi N}F_\pi m_\pi^2}
{q^2+m_\pi^2}.
\tag{19.4.49}\label{eq:19.4.49}
\end{equation}
This is expected to be valid when $q^2$ is of order $m_\pi^2$, and not only in the limit $q^2\to-m_\pi^2$, because the pion pole dominates matrix elements of $\partial_\mu A^\mu$ for all such small $q^2$. Also, for such $q^2$, in place of Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.32} we have
\begin{equation}
g(q^2)\simeq\frac{G_{\pi N}F_\pi}{q^2+m_\pi^2}.
\tag{19.4.50}\label{eq:19.4.50}
\end{equation}
From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.4.49} and \eqref{eq:19.4.50} we find that for $q^2$ of order $m_\pi^2$,
\begin{equation}
f(q^2)\simeq G_{\pi N}F_\pi/2m_N.
\tag{19.4.51}\label{eq:19.4.51}
\end{equation}
It should be no surprise that this function is roughly constant over the range of $q^2$ from zero to of order $m_\pi^2$, because it has no one-pion pole, and there is nothing else that could give it a substantial variation in such a small range of $q^2$. This constant value is approximately $f(0)\equiv g_A$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.51} thus again yields the Goldberger--Treiman relation.

We can now use the results of Section 19.2 to calculate the amplitude for emission of a single low energy pion in an arbitrary process $\alpha\to\beta$. We found that the amplitude is to be calculated from Feynman diagrams in which the pion is emitted only from the external lines of the process, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.49} shows that these contributions are to be calculated as if the pion field interaction were $\partial_\mu\boldsymbol{\pi}\cdot\mathbf{A}_N^\mu/F_\pi$, in which the subscript $N$ indicates that we are to drop the one-pion pole term in matrix elements of the axial-vector current. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.4.27} (and using isospin invariance) we conclude that for soft pions emitted from a nucleon line this interaction is effectively
\[
-\frac{ig_A}{F_\pi}\,
\partial_\mu\boldsymbol{\pi}\cdot\bar N\gamma^\mu\gamma_5\mathbf{t}\,N.
\]
Using the free-particle Dirac equation, we can see that for nucleons on the mass shell (that is, at the one-nucleon poles in Figure~\ref{fig:19.2}) this is equivalent to the pseudoscalar interaction $-2im_Ng_A\boldsymbol{\pi}\cdot\bar N\gamma_5\mathbf{t}\,N/F_\pi$. This provides yet another demonstration of the Goldberger--Treiman relation \eqref{eq:19.4.33}.

Our discussion here has not paralleled the historical development of these ideas. In fact, the course of historical development was chronologically almost exactly opposite to the line of argument presented here. Broken symmetries in particle physics started with the Goldberger--Treiman relation \eqref{eq:19.4.33}, which was derived~\hyperref[ch19-ref-11]{[11]} in 1957 on the basis of a dynamical calculation of pion decay. In order to explain the surprising success of this very approximate calculation, several theorists~\hyperref[ch19-ref-15]{[15]} introduced the idea of a `partial conservation of the axial-vector current' (PCAC), the idea that although the axial-vector current is not conserved (as shown by the fact that pions do decay) its divergence $\partial_\mu A_\pm^\mu$ is proportional to the pion field. In itself, this assumption is meaningless---we saw in Chapter 10 that \emph{any} field with a non-vanishing matrix element between the vacuum and one-pion states may be regarded as a pion field. Although it was not clear at the time, what was really being assumed was that the divergence of the axial-vector current is \emph{small}, of order $m_\pi^2$, except where a pion pole gives it a large matrix element. The problem was greatly clarified by a 1960 paper of Nambu~\hyperref[ch19-ref-16]{[16]}, who pointed out that the axial-vector current could be regarded as exactly conserved in the limit of zero pion mass, in which case the Goldberger--Treiman relation could be derived as we have done here. In this and a subsequent paper with Jona-Lasinio~\hyperref[ch19-ref-17]{[17]}, Nambu recognized that the appearance of this massless or nearly massless pion was a symptom of a broken exact or approximate symmetry. With other collaborators~\hyperref[ch19-ref-18]{[18]}, Nambu also showed how to calculate the rates of emission of a single soft pion in various processes. Subsequently Goldstone~\hyperref[ch19-ref-3]{[3]} remarked that broken symmetries always entail massless bosons, and this was proved in 1962 by Goldstone, Salam, and myself~\hyperref[ch19-ref-5]{[5]}, using the arguments presented here in Section 19.2.

None of this early work on pions as Goldstone bosons depended on any specific assumptions about the nature of the broken symmetry group; for instance it might have been a direct product of three commuting $U(1)$ groups, whose generators form an isotopic spin vector, or it might have been the non-compact group $SO(3,1)$, for which a minus sign appears on the right-hand side of the commutation relation \eqref{eq:19.4.19}. The nature of the broken symmetry group became important only with the consideration of processes involving more than one pion, starting with the Adler--Weisberger sum rule in 1965~\hyperref[ch19-ref-19]{[19]}, whose success showed that the broken symmetry is indeed $SU(2)\times SU(2)$. (Such processes are discussed in the following section.) The identification of the symmetry group $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ led to a shift of emphasis~\hyperref[ch19-ref-20]{[20]}, toward the implications of this symmetry within strong interaction physics, and away from an earlier concentration~\hyperref[ch19-ref-21]{[21]} on the currents themselves.

All of this work was done without a specific theory of the strong interactions. One of the reasons for the rapid acceptance of quantum chromodynamics in 1973 as the correct theory of strong interactions was that it explained the $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ symmetry as a simple consequence of the smallness of the $u$ and $d$ quark masses.
