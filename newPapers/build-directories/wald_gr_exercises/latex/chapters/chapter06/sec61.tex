\subsection{Derivation of the Schwarzschild Solution}\label{sec:6.1}

We seek all solutions of Einstein's equation which describe the exterior
gravitational field of a static, spherically symmetric body. Thus, we wish to
find all four-dimensional Lorentz signature metrics whose Ricci tensor vanishes
and which are static and possess spherical symmetry. Our first task is to define
more precisely the meaning of the terms \emph{static} and \emph{spherically
symmetric} and to choose a convenient coordinate system for analyzing this class
of spacetimes. It will be assumed in the discussion below that the reader has
read (or will refer to) appendix C.

A spacetime is said to be \emph{stationary} if there exists a one-parameter group
of isometries, \(\phi_t\), whose orbits are timelike curves. This group of
isometries expresses the time translation symmetry of the spacetime. Equivalently,
a stationary spacetime is one which possesses a timelike Killing vector field,
\(\xi^a\). A spacetime is said to be \emph{static} if it is stationary and if, in
addition, there exists a (spacelike) hypersurface \(\Sigma\) which is orthogonal to
the orbits of the isometry. By Frobenius's theorem (see appendix B) this is
equivalent to the requirement that the timelike Killing vector field \(\xi^a\)
satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \xi_{[a}\nabla_b\xi_{c]}=0 .
  \tag{6.1.1}\label{eq:6.1.1}
\end{equation}
The meaning of this extra condition of hypersurface orthogonality perhaps can be
seen best by introducing convenient coordinates for static spacetimes as follows.
If \(\xi^a\ne0\) everywhere on \(\Sigma\), then in a neighborhood of \(\Sigma\),
every point will lie on a unique orbit of \(\xi^a\) which passes through
\(\Sigma\). Assuming \(\xi^a\ne0\), we choose arbitrary coordinates
\(\{x^\mu\}\) on \(\Sigma\) and label each point \(p\) in this neighborhood of
\(\Sigma\) by the parameter, \(t\), of the orbit which starts from \(\Sigma\) and
ends at \(p\), and the coordinates, \(x^1,x^2,x^3\), of the orbit at \(\Sigma\).
Since this coordinate system employs the Killing parameter, \(t\), as one of the
coordinates, the metric components in this coordinate basis will be independent
of \(t\). Furthermore, since the surface \(\Sigma_t\), defined as the set of points
whose time coordinate has the value \(t\), is the image of \(\Sigma\) under the
isometry \(\phi_t\), it follows that each \(\Sigma_t\) is also orthogonal to
\(\xi^a\). Thus, in these coordinates, the metric components take the form
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-V^2(x^1,x^2,x^3)\,\dd t^2+
  \sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{3}h_{\mu\nu}(x^1,x^2,x^3)\,\dd x^\mu \dd x^\nu ,
  \tag{6.1.2}\label{eq:6.1.2}
\end{equation}
where \(V^2=-\xi_a\xi^a\), and the absence of \(\dd t\,\dd x^\mu\) cross terms
expresses the orthogonality of \(\xi^a\) with \(\Sigma\). A stationary but
nonstatic metric unavoidably must have \(\dd t\,\dd x^\mu\) cross terms in any
coordinate system which uses the Killing parameter as one of the coordinates.

From the explicit form of a static metric, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.2}, it can be seen
that the diffeomorphism defined by \(t\mapsto-t\), i.e., the map which takes each
point on each \(\Sigma_t\) to the point with the same spatial coordinates on
\(\Sigma_{-t}\), is an isometry. Thus, in addition to the time translation
symmetry \(t\mapsto t+\text{constant}\), possessed by all stationary spacetimes,
the static spacetimes also possess a time reflection symmetry. Physically, fields
which are time translation invariant can fail to be time reflection invariant when
rotational motion is involved, since the time reflection will change the direction
of rotation and thus will not restore one to the original configuration. Thus, for
example, a rotating fluid ball may have a time-independent matter and velocity
distribution, but does not possess a time reflection symmetry. In the case
considered here, the failure of Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.1} to hold implies that
neighboring orbits of \(\xi^a\) twist around each other. This helps explain why
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.1} is the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of
a time reflection symmetry.

A spacetime is said to be \emph{spherically symmetric} if its isometry group
contains a subgroup isomorphic to the group \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\), and the orbits of
this subgroup, i.e., the collection of points resulting from the action of the
subgroup on a given point, are two-dimensional spheres. The \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\)
isometries may then be interpreted physically as rotations, and thus a
spherically symmetric spacetime is one whose metric remains invariant under
rotations. The spacetime metric induces a metric on each orbit 2-sphere, which,
because of the rotational symmetry, must be a multiple of the metric of a unit
2-sphere, and thus can be completely characterized by the total area, \(A\), of
the 2-sphere. In spherically symmetric spacetimes it is convenient to introduce
the function \(r\), defined by
\begin{equation}
  r=(A/4\pi)^{1/2}.
  \tag{6.1.3}\label{eq:6.1.3}
\end{equation}
Thus, in spherical coordinates \((\theta,\phi)\), the metric on each orbit
2-sphere takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=r^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2).
  \tag{6.1.4}\label{eq:6.1.4}
\end{equation}
In flat, three-dimensional Euclidean space, \(r\) would also be the value of the
radius of the sphere, i.e., the distance from the surface of the sphere to its
center. However, in a curved space, a sphere need not have a center, e.g., the
manifold structure could be \(\mathbb R\times S^2\), rather than \(\mathbb R^3\);
and even if it does, \(r\) need not bear any relation to the distance to the
center. Nevertheless, we shall refer to \(r\) as the radial coordinate of the
sphere.

If a spacetime is both static and spherically symmetric, and if the static
Killing field \(\xi^a\) is unique (as we shall assume), then \(\xi^a\) must be
orthogonal to the orbit 2-spheres. Namely, if \(\xi^a\) is unique, it must be
invariant under all the rotational isometries. However, this requires its
projection onto any orbit sphere to vanish, since a nonvanishing vector field on a
sphere cannot be invariant under all rotations. Thus the orbit spheres lie wholly
within the hypersurfaces, \(\Sigma_t\), orthogonal to \(\xi^a\). Convenient
coordinates on the spacetime may be chosen as follows: We select a sphere on
\(\Sigma=\Sigma_0\) and choose spherical coordinates \((\theta,\phi)\) on it. We
carry these spherical coordinates to the other spheres of \(\Sigma\) by means of
geodesics orthogonal to the two-sphere (similar to the manner in which we used the
isotropic observers to carry coordinate labels to other hypersurfaces in
Section~5.1). Provided that \(\nabla_a r\ne0\), we choose \((r,\theta,\phi)\) as
coordinates in \(\Sigma_t\), and, finally, we choose \((t,r,\theta,\phi)\) as
coordinates for the spacetime according to the prescription described above
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.2}. In these coordinates, the metric of an arbitrary static,
spherically symmetric spacetime\footnote{It is worth pointing out that every
stationary, spherically symmetric spacetime must be static. This is because, as
in the static case, the stationary Killing field \(\xi^a\) must be orthogonal to
the orbit spheres and, in addition, \(\xi^a\nabla_a r=0\) since \(r\) is a
geometrical quantity. Hence, \(\xi^a\) must be orthogonal to the hypersurface,
\(\Sigma\), generated by the integral curves of \(\nabla_a r\) starting from an
orbit sphere. (If \(\nabla_a r=0\), this argument breaks down, but the conclusion
of hypersurface orthogonality of \(\xi^a\) remains valid.)} takes the simple form
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-f(r)\,\dd t^2+h(r)\,\dd r^2+r^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2).
  \tag{6.1.5}\label{eq:6.1.5}
\end{equation}
It should be kept in mind, however, that, in addition to the well understood
breakdown of the spherical coordinates at the north and south poles of the
spheres, this coordinate system breaks down at points where \(\xi^a=0\) or
\(\nabla_a r=0\) (or, more generally, where \(\xi^a\) and \(\nabla_a r\) become
collinear). As we shall see in Section~\ref{sec:6.4}, this occurs in the strong
field region of the Schwarzschild solution.

Most of the work required to obtain the static, spherically symmetric vacuum
solutions of Einstein's equation has now been completed, as we have reduced the
general problem of determining 10 unknown functions (the metric components,
\(g_{\mu\nu}\)) of four variables (the four coordinates) to determining two
functions (\(f\) and \(h\)) of one variable (\(r\)). The remaining tasks are to
compute the Ricci tensor, \(R_{ab}\), of the metric, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.5}, and
solve the equation \(R_{ab}=0\) for \(f\) and \(h\). We shall calculate \(R_{ab}\)
using the second tetrad approach of Section~3.4b.

A convenient orthonormal basis for the metric of Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.5} is
\begin{align}
  (e_0)_a&=f^{1/2}(\dd t)_a, &&\tag{6.1.6a}\label{eq:6.1.6a}\\
  (e_1)_a&=h^{1/2}(\dd r)_a, &&\tag{6.1.6b}\label{eq:6.1.6b}\\
  (e_2)_a&=r(\dd\theta)_a, &&\tag{6.1.6c}\label{eq:6.1.6c}\\
  (e_3)_a&=r\sin\theta(\dd\phi)_a. &&\tag{6.1.6d}\label{eq:6.1.6d}
\end{align}
Using the ordinary derivative, \(\partial_a\), of our coordinate system, we find
\begin{align}
  \partial_{[a}(e_0)_{b]}&=\frac12f^{-1/2}f'(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd t)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.7}\label{eq:6.1.7}\\
  \partial_{[a}(e_1)_{b]}&=0,
  &&\tag{6.1.8}\label{eq:6.1.8}\\
  \partial_{[a}(e_2)_{b]}&=(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd\theta)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.9}\label{eq:6.1.9}\\
  \partial_{[a}(e_3)_{b]}&=\sin\theta(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd\phi)_{b]}
     +r\cos\theta(\dd\theta)_{[a}(\dd\phi)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.10}\label{eq:6.1.10}
\end{align}
where \(f'=\dd f/\dd r\). Thus, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.25} (or
equivalently, Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.27}), we must solve the following equations for
the connection one-forms \(\omega_{a\mu\nu}=-\omega_{a\nu\mu}\):
\begin{align}
  \frac12f^{-1/2}f'(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd t)_{b]}
    &=h^{1/2}(\dd r)_{[a}\omega_{b]01}
      +r(\dd\theta)_{[a}\omega_{b]02}
      +r\sin\theta(\dd\phi)_{[a}\omega_{b]03},
  &&\tag{6.1.11}\label{eq:6.1.11}\\
  0&=f^{1/2}(\dd t)_{[a}\omega_{b]01}
      +r(\dd\theta)_{[a}\omega_{b]12}
      +r\sin\theta(\dd\phi)_{[a}\omega_{b]13},
  &&\tag{6.1.12}\label{eq:6.1.12}\\
  (\dd r)_{[a}(\dd\theta)_{b]}
    &=-f^{1/2}(\dd t)_{[a}\omega_{b]20}
      +h^{1/2}(\dd r)_{[a}\omega_{b]21}
      +r\sin\theta(\dd\phi)_{[a}\omega_{b]23},
  &&\tag{6.1.13}\label{eq:6.1.13}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \sin\theta(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd\phi)_{b]}
    +r\cos\theta(\dd\theta)_{[a}(\dd\phi)_{b]}
    &=-f^{1/2}(\dd t)_{[a}\omega_{b]30}\\
    &\quad{}+h^{1/2}(\dd r)_{[a}\omega_{b]31}
      +r(\dd\theta)_{[a}\omega_{b]32}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{6.1.14}\label{eq:6.1.14}
\end{equation}
A plausible guess at solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.11} is
\begin{equation}
  \omega_{b02}=\omega_{b03}=0,
  \tag{6.1.15}\label{eq:6.1.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \omega_{b01}=\frac12\frac{f'}{(fh)^{1/2}}(\dd t)_b+\alpha_1(\dd r)_b,
  \tag{6.1.16}\label{eq:6.1.16}
\end{equation}
where the function \(\alpha_1\) is undetermined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.11}.
Substitution of this trial solution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.12} then requires
\(\alpha_1=0\). From Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.12}, we might also guess
\(\omega_{b12}=\omega_{b13}=0\), but this leads to inconsistency later, so we
merely conclude that
\begin{align}
  \omega_{b12}&=\alpha_2(\dd\theta)_b+\alpha_3(\dd\phi)_b,
  &&\tag{6.1.17}\label{eq:6.1.17}\\
  \omega_{b13}&=\alpha_4(\dd\phi)_b+\frac{\alpha_3}{\sin\theta}(\dd\theta)_b.
  &&\tag{6.1.18}\label{eq:6.1.18}
\end{align}
Substituting our trial solution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.13}, we find
\begin{align}
  \alpha_2&=-h^{-1/2},
  &&\tag{6.1.19}\label{eq:6.1.19}\\
  \omega_{b23}&=-\frac{h^{1/2}}{r\sin\theta}\alpha_3(\dd r)_b+\alpha_5(\dd\phi)_b.
  &&\tag{6.1.20}\label{eq:6.1.20}
\end{align}
Finally, substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.14} yields
\begin{align}
  \alpha_3&=0,
  &&\tag{6.1.21}\label{eq:6.1.21}\\
  \alpha_4&=-h^{-1/2}\sin\theta,
  &&\tag{6.1.22}\label{eq:6.1.22}\\
  \alpha_5&=-\cos\theta.
  &&\tag{6.1.23}\label{eq:6.1.23}
\end{align}
Since we have found no inconsistency, this means that our trial solution
(generated by the initial guess \(\omega_{b02}=\omega_{b03}=0\)) is, in fact,
\emph{the} solution. Thus, we have found
\begin{align}
  \omega_{b02}&=\omega_{b03}=0,
  &&\tag{6.1.24}\label{eq:6.1.24}\\
  \omega_{b01}&=\frac{f'}{2(fh)^{1/2}}(\dd t)_b,
  &&\tag{6.1.25}\label{eq:6.1.25}\\
  \omega_{b12}&=-h^{-1/2}(\dd\theta)_b,
  &&\tag{6.1.26}\label{eq:6.1.26}\\
  \omega_{b13}&=-h^{-1/2}\sin\theta(\dd\phi)_b,
  &&\tag{6.1.27}\label{eq:6.1.27}\\
  \omega_{b23}&=-\cos\theta(\dd\phi)_b.
  &&\tag{6.1.28}\label{eq:6.1.28}
\end{align}

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.20} (or, equivalently, Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.28}) we obtain
the Riemann tensor with remarkably little total computation compared with other
approaches (see problem~2),
\begin{align}
  R_{ab01}&=-R_{ab10}=\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[(fh)^{-1/2}f'\right]
     (\dd r)_{[a}(\dd t)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.29}\label{eq:6.1.29}\\
  R_{ab02}&=-R_{ab20}=f^{-1/2}h^{-1}f'(\dd\theta)_{[a}(\dd t)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.30}\label{eq:6.1.30}\\
  R_{ab03}&=-R_{ab30}=f^{-1/2}h^{-1}f'\sin\theta(\dd\phi)_{[a}(\dd t)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.31}\label{eq:6.1.31}\\
  R_{ab12}&=-R_{ab21}=h^{-3/2}h'(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd\theta)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.32}\label{eq:6.1.32}\\
  R_{ab13}&=-R_{ab31}=\sin\theta\,h^{-3/2}h'(\dd r)_{[a}(\dd\phi)_{b]},
  &&\tag{6.1.33}\label{eq:6.1.33}\\
  R_{ab23}&=-R_{ab32}=2(1-h^{-1})\sin\theta(\dd\theta)_{[a}(\dd\phi)_{b]}.
  &&\tag{6.1.34}\label{eq:6.1.34}
\end{align}
The Ricci tensor is easily computed from the Riemann tensor via
Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.22}. Equating it to zero, we obtain the vacuum Einstein
equation for a static, spherically symmetric spacetime,
\begin{align}
  0=R_{00}
    &=R_{010}{}^1+R_{020}{}^2+R_{030}{}^3 \nonumber\\
    &=\frac12(fh)^{-1/2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[(fh)^{-1/2}f'\right]
      +(rfh)^{-1}f',
  &&\tag{6.1.35}\label{eq:6.1.35}\\
  0=R_{11}
    &=-\frac12(fh)^{-1/2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[(fh)^{-1/2}f'\right]
      +(rh^2)^{-1}h',
  &&\tag{6.1.36}\label{eq:6.1.36}\\
  0=R_{22}
    &=-\frac12(rfh)^{-1}f'+\frac12(rh^2)^{-1}h'
      +r^{-2}(1-h^{-1}).
  &&\tag{6.1.37}\label{eq:6.1.37}
\end{align}
Here \(R_{\mu\nu}=R_{ab}(e_\mu)^a(e_\nu)^b\). We also find that
\(R_{33}=R_{22}\) and that the off-diagonal components of \(R_{\mu\nu}\) vanish
identically, as could be predicted from symmetry arguments similar to those used
in Section~5.2.

Adding Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.1.35} and \eqref{eq:6.1.36}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  f'/f+h'/h=0,
  \tag{6.1.38}\label{eq:6.1.38}
\end{equation}
which implies
\begin{equation}
  f=Kh^{-1},
  \tag{6.1.39}\label{eq:6.1.39}
\end{equation}
where \(K\) is a constant. By re-scaling the time coordinate,
\(t\mapsto K^{1/2}t\), we may set \(K=1\). Equation~\eqref{eq:6.1.37} now yields
\begin{equation}
  -f'+\frac{1-f}{r}=0,
  \tag{6.1.40}\label{eq:6.1.40}
\end{equation}
i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}(rf)=1,
  \tag{6.1.41}\label{eq:6.1.41}
\end{equation}
which implies
\begin{equation}
  f=1+C/r,
  \tag{6.1.42}\label{eq:6.1.42}
\end{equation}
where \(C\) is a constant. Equation~\eqref{eq:6.1.42} together with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.39} (with \(K=1\)) solves Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.1.35}--\eqref{eq:6.1.37},
and thus we have found the general solution, first discovered by Schwarzschild,
of the vacuum Einstein equation for static, spherically symmetric spacetimes,
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-\left(1+\frac{C}{r}\right)\dd t^2+
  \left(1+\frac{C}{r}\right)^{-1}\dd r^2+r^2\dd\Omega^2,
  \tag{6.1.43}\label{eq:6.1.43}
\end{equation}
where \(\dd\Omega^2\) is shorthand for \(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2\).

Perhaps the first point to notice about the Schwarzschild solution is that it is
asymptotically flat,\footnote{A precise, general notion of asymptotic flatness
will be given in Chapter~11.} i.e., as \(r\to\infty\), the metric components
approach those of Minkowski spacetime in spherical coordinates. This allows us to
interpret the Schwarzschild metric as the exterior gravitational field of an
isolated body. We may interpret the constant \(C\) by comparing the behavior of a
test body in the weak field regime (\(r\to\infty\)) with the behavior of a test
body in the Newtonian theory of gravity. Examination of the geodesics of the
Schwarzschild metric (see Section~\ref{sec:6.3})---or, equivalently, direct
comparison of the Schwarzschild metric with the Newtonian metric discussed in
Section~4.4a (which, however, is not expressed in a convenient gauge to
facilitate the comparison)---shows that, for large \(r\), the behavior of a test
body in the Schwarzschild solution with parameter \(C\) agrees with the behavior
of a test body in a Newtonian gravitational field of mass \(M=-C/2\), i.e.,
restoring \(G\) and \(c\), \(GM/c^2=-C/2\). Thus, we interpret \(-C/2\) as the
total mass\footnote{A general definition of total mass for asymptotically flat
solutions (based on this idea of examining the distant gravitational field) will
be given in Chapter~11.} of the Schwarzschild field, and we may write the
Schwarzschild metric in its final form,
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\dd t^2+
  \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\dd r^2+r^2\dd\Omega^2.
  \tag{6.1.44}\label{eq:6.1.44}
\end{equation}

A striking feature of the Schwarzschild solution is that the metric components
become singular in the strong field regime at both \(r=2M\) and \(r=0\). This
singular behavior of the components could be due to either (i) a breakdown of the
coordinates used to obtain the general form of the metric, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.5},
because \(\xi^a=0\) or \(\nabla_a r=0\) (or \(\xi^a\) and \(\nabla_a r\) become
collinear) or (ii) a true singularity of the spacetime structure. We shall see in
Section~\ref{sec:6.4} that the singularity at \(r=2M\) is caused merely by a
breakdown of the coordinates, while the singularity at \(r=0\) is a true,
physical singularity. However, we note here that the singularity at \(r=2M\)
occurs at a numerical value of the radial coordinate given by
\begin{equation}
  r_s=\frac{2GM}{c^2}\approx3\left(\frac{M}{M_\odot}\right)\,\mathrm{km},
  \tag{6.1.45}\label{eq:6.1.45}
\end{equation}
where \(M_\odot\approx2\times10^{33}\,\mathrm{g}\) is the mass of the Sun. Thus,
for an ordinary body, such as the Sun or the Earth, the Schwarzschild radius,
\(r_s\), is well inside the radius of the body where, of course, the vacuum
Schwarzschild solution is no longer valid. Indeed, the coordinate and physical
singularities respectively at \(r=r_s\) and \(r=0\) are relevant only for bodies
which have undergone complete gravitational collapse.

Finally, we mention that the vacuum Einstein equation also can be solved for a
general spherically symmetric spacetime, without the assumption of staticity. As
was first shown by Birkhoff (1923), the Schwarzschild solution remains the only
solution of this more general system of equations (see, e.g., Hawking and Ellis
1973 for a proof). Thus, all spherically symmetric spacetimes with \(R_{ab}=0\)
are static. This result, known as \emph{Birkhoff's theorem}, is closely analogous
to the fact that the Coulomb solution is the only spherically symmetric solution
of Maxwell's equations in vacuum. It can be interpreted as saying that in gravity,
as in electromagnetism, there exists no monopole, i.e., spherically symmetric,
radiation.
