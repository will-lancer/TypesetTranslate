\subsection{Conformal Infinity}\label{sec:11.1}

As already indicated by the above discussion, we must overcome two problems in
order to have a useful formalism for analyzing gravitational radiation and other
aspects of the distant gravitational field of an isolated system. (i) We need a precise
definition of the notion of asymptotic flatness. (ii) We need a meaningful notion of
how to take \lq\lq limits as one goes to infinity\rq\rq\ and a precise framework for describing
the mathematical entities these limits represent. We shall proceed by proposing a
solution to problem (ii) for nongravitational fields in Minkowski spacetime. This
solution then will be used to motivate solutions to problems (i) and (ii) for curved
spacetimes.

In spherical coordinates, the metric of Minkowski spacetime takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-\dd t^2+\dd r^2+r^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2).
  \tag{11.1.1}\label{eq:11.1.1}
\end{equation}
Suppose we are interested in describing properties of radiation carried to infinity by
a massless field such as a Klein-Gordon scalar field \(\phi\). Since this entails taking
limits as one goes to infinity along null directions, it is convenient to introduce
advanced and retarded null coordinates defined by
\begin{align}
  v&=t+r, \tag{11.1.2}\label{eq:11.1.2}\\
  u&=t-r. \tag{11.1.3}\label{eq:11.1.3}
\end{align}
In the coordinates \(u,v,\theta,\phi\) the Minkowski metric components are
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-\dd u\,\dd v+\frac14(v-u)^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2).
  \tag{11.1.4}\label{eq:11.1.4}
\end{equation}
Suppose we are concerned with analyzing, say, outgoing radiation. With \(u\) fixed,
we wish to take limits as \(v\to\infty\) of our physical field \(\phi\) and extract
information about the radiation from the way this field approaches zero. In
particular, the energy carried to infinity by the field is determined by the
\lq\lq \(1/v\) piece\rq\rq\ of \(\phi\) in this limit. However, the taking of these limits is a
rather awkward procedure which does not generalize easily to curved spacetime. It
would make the analysis much easier if infinity were a \lq\lq definite place\rq\rq\ and one
simply had to evaluate the fields and/or their derivatives at this \lq\lq place.\rq\rq

A naive approach toward achieving this goal would be to introduce a new coordinate
\(V=1/v\), so that \lq\lq infinity\rq\rq\ along outgoing null geodesics would correspond to
the finite value, \(V=0\), of the new spacetime coordinate. However, the spacetime
metric components in the new coordinates \(u,V,\theta,\phi\) are
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=\frac{1}{V^2}\dd u\,\dd V
      +\frac14\left(\frac1V-u\right)^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2).
  \tag{11.1.5}\label{eq:11.1.5}
\end{equation}
These components are singular at \(V=0\), so we cannot extend the spacetime metric
there. Thus, we cannot do tensor analysis at \(V=0\) as though it were an ordinary
\lq\lq place.\rq\rq\ Of course, all we have done is introduce a bad coordinate whose behavior
is much like the first example considered above in Section~6.4.

However, suppose we consider a new, unphysical metric \(\tilde g_{ab}\) obtained by
multiplying the Minkowski metric, \(\eta_{ab}\), by \(V^2=1/v^2\), i.e.,
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) is related to \(\eta_{ab}\) by a conformal transformation with conformal
factor \(\Omega=V\) (see Appendix~D). Then, in the coordinates
\(u,V,\theta,\phi\), the components of \(\tilde g_{ab}\) are
\begin{equation}
  \dd\tilde{s}^2=\dd u\,\dd V+\frac14(1-uV)^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2),
  \tag{11.1.6}\label{eq:11.1.6}
\end{equation}
and these components are well behaved at \(V=0\). Thus, let us extend the
Minkowski manifold by \lq\lq adding in\rq\rq\ the points represented by \(V=0\). As seen
above, the original flat metric, \(\eta_{ab}\), cannot be smoothly extended to
\(V=0\)---Minkowski spacetime \((\mathbb R^4,\eta_{ab})\), of course, is
inextendible as a spacetime---but the new, unphysical metric \(\tilde g_{ab}\) can be
smoothly extended to \(V=0\). Hence, we may do ordinary tensor analysis at
\lq\lq infinity\rq\rq\ (or, more precisely, at that portion of \lq\lq infinity\rq\rq\ represented by
\(v\to\infty\) at constant \(u\)) as a \lq\lq place.\rq\rq\ In effect, we have brought in
infinity to a finite distance by a conformal transformation. We now simply may
evaluate fields and their covariant derivatives with respect to \(\tilde g_{ab}\) at
infinity and thus avoid dealing with limits in the original physical spacetime.

In fact, our particular choice of \(\tilde g_{ab}\) is not ideal since our conformal
factor \(V=1/v\) needlessly blows up at the events \(v=0\) of the original spacetime.
Furthermore, although we have extended \(\tilde g_{ab}\) to \lq\lq future null
infinity\rq\rq\ (i.e., the limit \(v\to\infty\) at fixed \(u\)), we cannot similarly extend
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) to \lq\lq past null infinity\rq\rq\ (\(u\to-\infty\) at fixed \(v\)) or
\lq\lq spatial infinity\rq\rq\ (\(r\to\infty\) at fixed \(t\)). However, all of these
drawbacks can be remedied by a more judicious choice of conformal factor. Let
\begin{equation}
  \tilde g_{ab}=\Omega^2\eta_{ab},
  \tag{11.1.7}\label{eq:11.1.7}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  \Omega^2=4(1+v^2)^{-1}(1+u^2)^{-1}.
  \tag{11.1.8}\label{eq:11.1.8}
\end{equation}
Then \(\tilde g_{ab}\) is a smooth metric on the original Minkowski manifold and
\((\mathbb R^4,\tilde g_{ab})\) can be smoothly extended to a \lq\lq larger\rq\rq\ spacetime
such that the boundary of the Minkowski region in this larger spacetime gives us a
precise representation of \lq\lq infinity.\rq\rq\ To see this, we define new coordinates
\(T,R\) for Minkowski spacetime by
\begin{align}
  T&=\tan^{-1}v+\tan^{-1}u, \tag{11.1.9}\label{eq:11.1.9}\\
  R&=\tan^{-1}v-\tan^{-1}u. \tag{11.1.10}\label{eq:11.1.10}
\end{align}
Then \(T\) and \(R\) have ranges restricted by the inequalities
\begin{align}
  -\pi&<T+R<\pi, \tag{11.1.11}\label{eq:11.1.11}\\
  -\pi&<T-R<\pi, \tag{11.1.12}\label{eq:11.1.12}\\
  0&\leq R. \tag{11.1.13}\label{eq:11.1.13}
\end{align}

The components of \(\tilde g_{ab}\) in the coordinates \(T,R,\theta,\phi\) are given by
\begin{equation}
  \dd\tilde{s}^2=-\dd T^2+\dd R^2+\sin^2R(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2).
  \tag{11.1.14}\label{eq:11.1.14}
\end{equation}
Remarkably, this is precisely the natural Lorentz metric on \(S^3\times\mathbb R\),
known as the Einstein static universe (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.11} and Problem~3 of
Chapter~5) except that the coordinate ranges are restricted by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.1.11} and \eqref{eq:11.1.12}. Thus, we have obtained the following
result: There exists a conformal isometry\footnote{As defined in Appendix~C, a
conformal isometry of \((M,g_{ab})\) into \((M',g'_{ab})\) is a diffeomorphism
\(\psi:M\to M'\) such that \((\psi^*g')_{ab}=\Omega^2g_{ab}\).} of Minkowski
spacetime \((\mathbb R^4,\eta_{ab})\) into the open region \(\mathcal O\) of the
Einstein static universe \((S^3\times\mathbb R,\tilde g_{ab})\) given by the
coordinate restrictions \eqref{eq:11.1.11} and \eqref{eq:11.1.12}.

This result allows us to give a precise definition of infinity for Minkowski
spacetime: We define the \emph{conformal infinity} of Minkowski spacetime to be the
boundary, \(\partial\mathcal O\), of \(\mathcal O\) in the Einstein static universe. As
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:11.1}, this boundary can be naturally divided into five
parts: (1) The \lq\lq bottom vertex point\rq\rq\ \(i^-\), called \emph{past timelike
infinity}, given by coordinates \(R=0,T=-\pi\). (2) The three-dimensional null
surface \(\mathcal I^-\), called \emph{past null infinity}, given by \(T=-\pi+R\) for
\(0<R<\pi\). (3) The point \(\ii^0\) at \(R=\pi,T=0\), called \emph{spatial infinity}.
(4) The three-dimensional null surface \(\mathcal I^+\), called \emph{future null
infinity}, given by \(T=\pi-R\) for \(0<R<\pi\). (5) The \lq\lq top vertex point\rq\rq\
\(i^+\) at \(R=0,T=\pi\), called \emph{future timelike infinity}. Note that all
timelike geodesics of Minkowski spacetime begin at \(i^-\) and end at \(i^+\), all
spacelike geodesics begin and end at \(\ii^0\), while all null geodesics begin at
\(\mathcal I^-\) and end at \(\mathcal I^+\). Since it is difficult to draw complicated
spacetime diagrams on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) in Figure~\ref{fig:11.1} and

\input{figures/chapter11/fig11_2.tex}

since two
spatial dimensions are suppressed in this diagram (i.e., \(S^3\) is represented as
\(S^1\)), one often represents \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) as two null cones joined at
their base, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:11.2}. However, it should be noted that
this diagram inaccurately represents spatial infinity, \(\ii^0\), as a sphere rather than
as a point.

This definition of conformal infinity of Minkowski spacetime allows us to formulate
precise asymptotic conditions on physical fields representing the exterior field of
and/or the radiation resulting from an isolated source as follows: Depending on the
physical field and the stringency of the asymptotic conditions we wish to impose, we
require that a suitable power of \(\Omega^{-1}\) times the field can be extended to
conformal infinity, \(\partial\mathcal O\), in a sufficiently well behaved manner.
With these conditions imposed, quantities which used to be represented in Minkowski
spacetime as limits as \(r\to\infty\) or \(v\to\infty\) now can be represented as
ordinary tensor fields on \(\partial\mathcal O\). This provides a highly satisfactory
solution to problem (ii) for Minkowski spacetime.

We shall illustrate the power and utility of the conformal infinity construction for
Minkowski spacetime by using it to give a remarkably simple proof of the following
result.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:11.1.1}
Let \(\phi\) be a solution of the massless Klein-Gordon equation
\eqref{eq:4.2.19} in Minkowski spacetime with smooth data
\(\phi_0,\dot\phi_0\) of compact support at \(t=0\). Then
\(\phi=\order(1/\lambda)\) as \(\lambda\to\infty\) along every null geodesic and
\(\phi=\order(1/\tau^2)\) as \(\tau\to\infty\) along every timelike geodesic, where
\(\lambda\) and \(\tau\) denote, respectively, affine parameter along the null
geodesic and proper time along the timelike geodesic.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
According to Appendix~D, if \(\phi\) satisfies the Klein-Gordon equation in
Minkowski spacetime, then \(\tilde\phi=\Omega^{-1}\phi\) satisfies the equation
\begin{equation}
  \tilde g^{ab}\tilde\nabla_a\tilde\nabla_b\tilde\phi
  -\frac16\tilde R\tilde\phi=0
  \tag{11.1.15}\label{eq:11.1.15}
\end{equation}
in the region \(\mathcal O\) of the Einstein static universe where
\(\tilde\nabla_a\) and \(\tilde R\) are the derivative operator and scalar curvature
of \(\tilde g_{ab}\). Consider now, the initial data for Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.15} on
the Cauchy surface \(T=0\) of the Einstein static universe obtained by extending
\(\tilde\phi_0,\dot{\tilde\phi}_0\) to \lq\lq spatial infinity\rq\rq\ by setting these
quantities equal to zero at the point \(\ii^0\). Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.15} is of the
form for which Theorem~10.1.2 applies, it follows that there exists a smooth
solution \(\tilde\psi\) throughout the \emph{entire} Einstein static universe of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.15} with the given initial data. By the uniqueness and domain of
dependence properties of Theorem~10.1.2, this solution \(\tilde\psi\) must agree with
\(\tilde\phi\) in \(\mathcal O\). Thus, we have proven that \(\tilde\phi\) can be smoothly
extended to \(\partial\mathcal O\). The conclusions of the proposition follow
immediately by translating this result back to statements about asymptotic limits in
Minkowski spacetime. Indeed, the hypothesis of the theorem can be weakened
significantly---all that is needed is that the data induced by \(\tilde\phi\) at \(T=0\)
can be smoothly extended to \(\ii^0\)---and stronger conclusions than those stated in
the theorem can be drawn from the fact that \(\tilde\phi\) can be smoothly extended
to \(\partial\mathcal O\).
\end{proof}

We turn, now, to the issue of defining the notion of asymptotically flat curved
spacetimes. The key idea is that---as will be seen below---our ability to perform the
above construction of mapping Minkowski spacetime via a conformal isometry into a
bounded region of the Einstein static universe crucially depended upon the structure
of Minkowski spacetime \lq\lq at infinity.\rq\rq\ This suggests that we define a spacetime
to be asymptotically flat if a similar construction can be performed, i.e., if the
physical spacetime can be mapped into a new, \lq\lq unphysical\rq\rq\ spacetime via a
conformal isometry with properties similar to that of the Minkowski case. In this way
we solve both problems (i) and (ii) mentioned at the beginning of this section, since
we have a manifestly coordinate independent formulation of the notion of asymptotic
flatness, and, as in the Minkowski case, the boundary in the unphysical spacetime of
the image of the physical spacetime under the conformal isometry gives a precise
framework for describing infinity.

However, there are two important properties of the construction of conformal infinity
for Minkowski spacetime which do not carry over to curved spacetimes. First, we wish
to consider spacetimes which become flat as one goes to \lq\lq large distances in
spacelike or null directions.\rq\rq\ However, we do not wish to require the spacetimes
to become flat \lq\lq at a fixed position at early or late times,\rq\rq\ since we may wish to
describe spacetimes representing isolated bodies which may remain present at early
and late times. Therefore, we cannot expect the conformal infinity of curved
spacetime to be similar to that of Minkowski spacetime at past and future timelike
infinity, \(i^-\) and \(i^+\). For this reason, for curved spacetimes we do not impose
any requirements on the structure of conformal infinity corresponding to the presence
of these two points. Second, although the metric is required to become flat at spatial
infinity, for the reasons discussed below, smoothness or even differentiability of the
conformally rescaled metric \(\tilde g_{ab}\) at spatial infinity is too strong a
requirement. Thus, although conformal infinity of curved spacetime is required to
contain a point \(\ii^0\) representing spatial infinity, the smoothness properties which
hold for Minkowski spacetime must be weakened significantly.

Our task, now, is to define asymptotic flatness by extracting features of the
construction of conformal infinity of Minkowski spacetime---modulo the above
modifications---which are strong enough that they can be implemented only for
spacetimes which physically represent isolated systems but are not overly stringent
to the extent that physically reasonable examples are excluded. The motivation for
the particular choice of features given below comes from a study of some examples,
from the stability of the properties under linear perturbations (see Geroch and
Xanthopoulos 1978), and from the fact that it provides an appropriate framework for
defining total energy and radiated energy (see Section~\ref{sec:11.2}). Further
discussion of the motivation is given by Geroch (1977). We state, now, the rather
technical conditions of the definition of asymptotic flatness given by Ashtekar (1980)
and then discuss the meaning of these conditions.

A vacuum spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) is called to be \emph{asymptotically flat at null
and spatial infinity} if there exists a spacetime \((\tilde M,\tilde g_{ab})\)---with
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) \(C^\infty\) everywhere except possibly at a point \(\ii^0\) where it is
\(C^{>0}\) (defined below)---and a conformal isometry
\(\psi:M\to\psi[M]\subsetneq\tilde M\) with conformal factor \(\Omega\) (so that
\(g_{ab}=\Omega^{-2}\psi^*\tilde g_{ab}\) in \(\psi[M]\)) satisfying the following
conditions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=2.2em]
\item
\(\overline{J^+(\ii^0)}\cup\overline{J^-(\ii^0)}=\tilde M-M\). [Here
\(\overline{J^+(\ii^0)}\) is the closure of the causal future, \(J^+(\ii^0)\), of
\(\ii^0\) (see Chapter~8), and for notational simplicity we write \(M\) rather than
\(\psi[M]\) here and in the following.] Thus, \(\ii^0\) is spacelike related to all
points in \(M\) and the boundary, \(\partial M\), of \(M\) consists of the union of
\(\ii^0\), \(\mathcal I^+\equiv\overline{J^+(\ii^0)}-\ii^0\), and
\(\mathcal I^-\equiv\overline{J^-(\ii^0)}-\ii^0\).

\item
There exists an open neighborhood, \(V\), of
\(\partial M=\ii^0\cup\mathcal I^+\cup\mathcal I^-\) such that the spacetime
\((V,\tilde g_{ab})\) is strongly causal.

\item
\(\Omega\) can be extended to a function on all of \(\tilde M\) which is \(C^2\) at
\(\ii^0\) and \(C^\infty\) elsewhere.

\item
\(\text{(a)}\) On \(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\) we have \(\Omega=0\) and
\(\tilde\nabla_a\Omega\ne0\). (Here \(\tilde\nabla_a\) is the derivative operator
associated with \(\tilde g_{ab}\), although, clearly, this condition is independent
of choice of derivative operator.) \(\text{(b)}\) We have \(\Omega(\ii^0)=0\),
\(\lim_{i^0}\tilde\nabla_a\Omega=0\), and
\(\lim_{i^0}\tilde\nabla_a\tilde\nabla_b\Omega=2\tilde g_{ab}(\ii^0)\). (We take
limits at \(\ii^0\) since \(\tilde g_{ab}\) need not be \(C^1\) there, and thus
\(\tilde\nabla_a\) need not be defined at \(\ii^0\).)

\item
\(\text{(a)}\) The map of null directions at \(\ii^0\) into the space of integral curves
of \(n^a\equiv\tilde g^{ab}\tilde\nabla_b\Omega\) on \(\mathcal I^+\) and
\(\mathcal I^-\) is a diffeomorphism. \(\text{(b)}\) For a smooth function,
\(\omega\), on \(\tilde M-\ii^0\) with \(\omega>0\) on
\(M\cup\mathcal I^+\cup\mathcal I^-\) which satisfies
\(\tilde\nabla_a(\omega^4n^a)=0\) on \(\mathcal I^+\cup\mathcal I^-\), the vector
field \(\omega^{-1}n^a\) is complete on \(\mathcal I^+\cup\mathcal I^-\).
\end{enumerate}

We begin our discussion of this definition by explaining the meaning of the
condition \(C^{>0}\) imposed on \(\tilde g_{ab}\) at \(\ii^0\). A basic problem with the
construction of conformal infinity is that all of spatial infinity is represented by
only a single point. As one goes along curves in the physical spacetime which go to
spatial infinity along different angular directions (or along directions differing by
a boost), then physically the curves become farther and farther apart. However, in
the unphysical spacetime, the different curves all terminate at the same point \(\ii^0\).
Therefore, while it is entirely reasonable to require that certain physical tensor
fields (times suitable powers of \(\Omega\)) have well defined limits along
appropriate curves that go to spatial infinity, it is unreasonable to demand that
these limits along curves going to infinity in different directions define the same
limiting tensor at the point \(\ii^0\). Because of the homogeneity and isotropy of
Minkowski spacetime, it was possible in that case to define a conformal completion so
that the unphysical metric and conformal factor were smooth at \(\ii^0\). However,
when one considers physical fields on Minkowski spacetime, one soon encounters this
direction dependent phenomenon at \(\ii^0\). For example, one easily may verify that
for the Coulomb solution of Maxwell's equation, the tensor \(\Omega F_{ab}\)
approaches a well defined, finite limit as one approaches \(\ii^0\) along any spacelike
geodesic, but the tensor it defines at \(\ii^0\) depends upon the choice of spacelike
geodesic. (Roughly speaking, the \lq\lq electric lines of force\rq\rq\ of \(F_{ab}\) point
radially inward at \(\ii^0\).) The behavior of the spacetime metric at \lq\lq large
distances\rq\rq\ in curved spacetimes such as the Schwarzschild solution is analogous
to this behavior of the Coulomb field solution in flat spacetime. Therefore, it is
unreasonable to demand that the unphysical metric, \(\tilde g_{ab}\), be smooth at
\(\ii^0\).

It appears that an appropriate condition on \(\tilde g_{ab}\) is that it be \(C^{>0}\) at
\(\ii^0\). By \(C^{>0}\) at \(\ii^0\), we mean that \(\tilde g_{ab}\) is continuous
\((C^0)\) at \(\ii^0\) and, in addition, that its first derivatives have direction-dependent
limits at \(\ii^0\) which are smooth in their angular dependence. This latter notion can
be made precise as follows. Let \(x^\mu\) be a smooth coordinate system with origin
at \(\ii^0\). Define the \lq\lq radial function\rq\rq\ \(\rho\) by
\begin{equation*}
  \rho^2=\sum_{\mu=1}^{4}(x^\mu)^2,
\end{equation*}
and define the angular functions \(\theta^\alpha\) (\(\alpha=1,2,3\)) in terms of
the \(x^\mu\) by the same formulas as used to define the 3-sphere coordinates in
four-dimensional Euclidean space. We may use the coordinates \(\theta^\alpha\) to
characterize each tangent direction at \(\ii^0\). A function \(f\) is said to have a
\emph{regular direction-dependent limit} at \(\ii^0\) if the following three properties
are satisfied: (i) For each \(C^1\) curve \(\gamma\) ending at \(\ii^0\), the limit of
\(f\) along \(\gamma\) exists at \(\ii^0\). Furthermore, the value of this limit depends
only on the tangent direction to \(\gamma\) at \(\ii^0\). We define
\(F(\theta^\alpha)=\lim_{i^0}f\), where the limit is taken along a curve whose
tangent direction at \(\ii^0\) is characterized by \(\theta^\alpha\). (ii) \(F\) is a
smooth function on the 3-sphere. (iii) Along every \(C^1\) curve ending at \(\ii^0\),
we have for all \(n\geq1\),
\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{i^0}\frac{\partial^n f}{\partial\theta^n}
  =\frac{\partial^nF}{\partial\theta^n},
  \qquad
  \lim_{i^0}\rho^n\frac{\partial^nf}{\partial\rho^n}=0.
\end{equation*}
(Here \(\partial^n/\partial\theta^n\) denotes any \(n\)th order partial derivative
with respect to the \(\theta^\alpha\), with it understood that the same partial
derivative occurs on both sides of the equation.) Thus, a precise statement of the
condition that \(\tilde g_{ab}\) is \(C^{>0}\) at \(\ii^0\) is that---in addition to
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) being continuous---all the first partial derivatives of the components
of \(\tilde g_{ab}\) in a smooth chart covering \(\ii^0\) have regular direction
dependent limits at \(\ii^0\). It follows immediately that in a chart with
\(\{\tilde\nabla_ax^\mu\}\) orthonormal at \(\ii^0\), the components of
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) take the form
\(\tilde g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+\rho\,\ell_{\mu\nu}(\theta^\alpha)+o(\rho)\)
as \(\rho\to0\). Further discussion of the \(C^{>0}\) condition is given by
Ashtekar and Hansen (1978) and Ashtekar (1980). Since the metric is only
\(C^{>0}\) at \(\ii^0\), it is natural also to weaken the assumptions concerning
differential structure of the manifold \(\tilde M\) at \(\ii^0\) to what Ashtekar and
Hansen (1978) call \(C^{>1}\) differential structure. (Otherwise, one would be able
to define many inequivalent differential structures on \(\tilde M\) at \(\ii^0\)
compatible with all the above conditions.) We refer the reader to that reference for
the definition of this term.

Note that we have defined asymptotic flatness only for the case of vacuum
spacetimes, \(R_{ab}=0\). However, since only properties of the spacetime \lq\lq near
infinity\rq\rq\ will play any role in our analysis, we need only require \(R_{ab}=0\)
\lq\lq near infinity,\rq\rq\ e.g., in the intersection of \(M\) and the neighborhood
\(V\) of condition (2). Indeed, one may weaken this requirement further to allow
nonvanishing \(T_{ab}\) in \(V\) which goes to zero at infinity suitably
rapidly---more precisely, such that \(\Omega^{-2}T_{ab}\) is smooth on
\(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\) and has appropriate limiting behavior at \(\ii^0\).

According to Eq.~(D.8) of Appendix~D (with the roles of \(\tilde g_{ab}\) and
\(g_{ab}\) reversed), the physical Ricci tensor, \(R_{ab}\), is related to the
unphysical Ricci tensor, \(\tilde R_{ab}\), by
\begin{equation}
  R_{ab}=\tilde R_{ab}+2\Omega^{-1}\tilde\nabla_a\tilde\nabla_b\Omega
  +\tilde g_{ab}\tilde g^{cd}
  \left(\Omega^{-1}\tilde\nabla_c\tilde\nabla_d\Omega
  -3\Omega^{-2}\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\Omega\right).
  \tag{11.1.16}\label{eq:11.1.16}
\end{equation}
Thus, the vanishing of the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.16} is the vacuum
Einstein field equation expressed in terms of the unphysical variables. Note that we
have written, for example, \(\tilde g^{cd}\tilde\nabla_c\tilde\nabla_d\Omega\) rather
than \(\tilde\nabla^c\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\) in order to avoid confusion over whether
the physical or unphysical metric is used to raise and lower indices.

It should be emphasized that there is considerable arbitrariness in the association
of an unphysical spacetime \((\tilde M,\tilde g_{ab})\) with an asymptotically flat
physical spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\). If \((\tilde M,\tilde g_{ab})\) is an unphysical
spacetime satisfying the properties of the definition with conformal factor
\(\Omega\), then so is \((\tilde M,\omega^2\tilde g_{ab})\) with conformal factor
\(\omega\Omega\), provided only that the function \(\omega\) is strictly positive, is
smooth everywhere except possibly at \(\ii^0\), is \(C^{>0}\) at \(\ii^0\), and satisfies
\(\omega(\ii^0)=1\). Thus, there is considerable gauge freedom in the choice of the
unphysical metric.

Let us now discuss the meaning of the five conditions appearing in the definition of
asymptotic flatness. The first three conditions require in a fairly straightforward
manner that \((\tilde M,\tilde g_{ab})\) possess some of the basic features of the
conformal completion of Minkowski spacetime. The first, in effect, states that \(\ii^0\)
represents spatial infinity; the second states that no causal pathologies occur near
infinity; and the third states that \(\Omega\) is well behaved near infinity. The key
condition in the definition is condition (4). The requirement that \(\Omega\) vanish
at \(\mathcal I^+\), \(\mathcal I^-\), and \(\ii^0\) implies that at these places an
\lq\lq infinite amount of stretching\rq\rq\ is involved in going from the unphysical
metric \(\tilde g_{ab}\) to the physical metric \(g_{ab}\). This shows that
\(\mathcal I^+\), \(\mathcal I^-\), and \(\ii^0\) truly represent \lq\lq infinity\rq\rq\ of
the physical spacetime. Furthermore, the requirements on the derivatives of
\(\Omega\) at \(\mathcal I^+\), \(\mathcal I^-\), and \(\ii^0\) specified in conditions
4(a) and 4(b) imply that the physical metric, \(g_{ab}\), becomes flat (and
approaches flatness at the appropriate rate) as one goes to infinity.

To see this in more detail, we derive a coordinate form of the condition on the
asymptotic behavior of the physical metric as one approaches future null infinity,
\(\mathcal I^+\). (A similar analysis applies, of course, to \(\mathcal I^-\).) By
multiplying the vacuum Einstein field equation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.16} with
\(R_{ab}=0\)) by \(\Omega\) and taking the limit at \(\mathcal I^+\), we find (since
\(\tilde g_{ab}\), \(\tilde R_{ab}\), and \(\Omega\) are smooth there) that
\(\Omega^{-1}\tilde g^{cd}\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\Omega\) must be smooth
at \(\mathcal I^+\), i.e., more precisely (since \(\Omega=0\) at \(\mathcal I^+\) and
thus \(\Omega^{-1}\) is not defined there) that this quantity can be smoothly
extended to \(\mathcal I^+\). In particular, this implies that
\(n^a=\tilde g^{ab}\tilde\nabla_b\Omega\) is null at \(\mathcal I^+\), a result which
also follows from the fact that \(n^a\) must be normal to \(\mathcal I^+\) (since
\(\Omega\) is constant on \(\mathcal I^+\)) and
\(\mathcal I^+=[\overline{J^+(\ii^0)}-\ii^0]\) is a null surface.

We show now that by using the \lq\lq gauge freedom\rq\rq\ in the conformal factor
mentioned above, we can make
\(\Omega^{-1}\tilde g^{cd}\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\Omega\) vanish at
\(\mathcal I^+\). Namely, under the transformation
\(\Omega\to\Omega'=\omega\Omega\),
\(\tilde g_{ab}\to\tilde g'_{ab}=\omega^2\tilde g_{ab}\), we have
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  (\Omega')^{-1}(\tilde g')^{cd}
  \tilde\nabla'_c\Omega'\tilde\nabla'_d\Omega'
  ={}&\omega^{-3}\tilde g^{cd}\bigl[
  \Omega\tilde\nabla_c\omega\tilde\nabla_d\omega
  +2\omega\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\omega\\
  &\qquad\qquad\qquad
  +\omega^2\Omega^{-1}\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\Omega
  \bigr].
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{11.1.17}\label{eq:11.1.17}
\end{equation}
Therefore, by choosing \(\omega\) to satisfy
\begin{equation}
  n^a\tilde\nabla_a\ln\omega
  =-\frac12\Omega^{-1}\tilde g^{cd}
  \tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\Omega
  \tag{11.1.18}\label{eq:11.1.18}
\end{equation}
on \(\mathcal I^+\), we find a new unphysical metric and conformal factor which
satisfies the desired relation. (Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.18} is merely an ordinary
differential equation along each integral curve of \(n^a\), solutions always may be
obtained.\footnote{Note that solutions, \(\omega\), of Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.18} on
\(\mathcal I^+\) cannot have the correct limiting behavior at \(\ii^0\). Thus,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.18} really can be imposed only outside an (arbitrarily small)
neighborhood of \(\ii^0\). However, this need not concern us here since we presently
deal only with the asymptotic form of the physical metric on \(\mathcal I^+\).})
Thus, without loss of generality, we may assume that we have chosen the conformal
factor and unphysical metric so that
\(\Omega^{-1}\tilde g^{cd}\tilde\nabla_c\Omega\tilde\nabla_d\Omega=0\) on
\(\mathcal I^+\). The vacuum Einstein field equation then yields
\begin{equation}
  2\tilde\nabla_a\tilde\nabla_b\Omega+
  \tilde g_{ab}\tilde g^{cd}\tilde\nabla_c\tilde\nabla_d\Omega=0
  \quad\text{on }\mathcal I^+,
  \tag{11.1.19}\label{eq:11.1.19}
\end{equation}
which, in turn, implies
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\nabla_a\tilde\nabla_b\Omega=0
  \quad\text{on }\mathcal I^+.
  \tag{11.1.20}\label{eq:11.1.20}
\end{equation}
It follows trivially from Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.20} that the null tangent
\(n^a=\tilde g^{ab}\tilde\nabla_b\Omega\) to \(\mathcal I^+\) satisfies the affinely
parameterized geodesic equation on \(\mathcal I^+\) with respect to the unphysical
metric,
\begin{equation}
  n^a\tilde\nabla_an^b=0.
  \tag{11.1.21}\label{eq:11.1.21}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, since \(B^b{}_a=\tilde\nabla_an^b=0\) on \(\mathcal I^+\), we see that
in the gauge \eqref{eq:11.1.20}, the expansion, shear, and twist of the null geodesic
generators of \(\mathcal I^+\) all vanish (see Section~9.2).

The gauge choice \eqref{eq:11.1.18} on \(\omega\) which led to the gauge condition
\eqref{eq:11.1.20} still permits the additional freedom of choosing \(\omega\)
arbitrarily on any given cross section of \(\mathcal I^+\), i.e., on a two-dimensional
surface, \(\mathcal S\), in \(\mathcal I^+\) which intersects each null geodesic
generator of \(\mathcal I^+\) at precisely one point. It follows from condition 5(a)
that \(\mathcal I^+\) has topology \(S^2\times\mathbb R\), and thus, topologically,
\(\mathcal S\) must be a 2-sphere. The unphysical metric \(\tilde g_{ab}\) induces a
Riemannian metric \(\tilde h_{ab}\) on \(\mathcal S\). However, using arguments along
the lines of Problem~2 of Chapter~3, it can be seen that every Riemannian metric on
a two-dimensional sphere is equal to a conformal factor times the natural metric on
the sphere, i.e., \(\tilde h_{ab}\) is of the form \(\tilde h_{ab}=f^2h_{ab}\), with
\((\mathcal S,h_{ab})\) isometric to the unit sphere in \(\mathbb R^3\). Hence, we
shall use the remaining freedom in the choice of \(\omega\) to make \(\mathcal S\) a
metric sphere of unit radius. Since in the gauge \eqref{eq:11.1.20} the expansion and
shear of the null geodesic generators of \(\mathcal I^+\) vanish, it follows that in
this gauge, the induced metric on \emph{every} cross section of \(\mathcal I^+\) is
that of a unit sphere.

Having made the above gauge choices, we introduce coordinates in a neighborhood of
\(\mathcal I^+\) in the unphysical spacetime as follows. Since
\(\tilde\nabla_a\Omega\ne0\) on \(\mathcal I^+\), we may use \(\Omega\) itself as one
of the coordinates. We introduce the natural spherical coordinates \((\theta,\phi)\)
on the spherical cross section, \(\mathcal S\), and \lq\lq carry\rq\rq\ these coordinates to
other points of \(\mathcal I^+\) along the null geodesic generators of \(\mathcal I^+\).
We define the coordinate \(u\) on \(\mathcal I^+\) to be the affine parameter
(measured from \(\mathcal S\)) along the null geodesic generators of \(\mathcal I^+\)
with \(u\) scaled so that \(n^a\tilde\nabla_au=1\). Finally, we extend
\((u,\theta,\phi)\) off of \(\mathcal I^+\) by holding their values fixed along each
null geodesic of the family (other than that which generates \(\mathcal I^+\))
orthogonal to the 2-spheres of constant \(u\) on \(\mathcal I^+\).

It follows from the above that in these coordinates, the unphysical metric
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) takes on \(\mathcal I^+\) the form
\begin{equation}
  \left.\dd\tilde{s}^2\right|_{\mathcal I^+}
  =2\,\dd\Omega\,\dd u+\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2.
  \tag{11.1.22}\label{eq:11.1.22}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, the gauge condition \eqref{eq:11.1.20} on \(\Omega\) implies that
\(\tilde g_{uu}\), \(\tilde g_{u\theta}\), and \(\tilde g_{u\phi}\) all are
\(\order(\Omega^2)\) as \(\Omega\to0\) (see Problem~1). Thus, in a neighborhood of
\(\mathcal I^+\), the components of the physical metric,
\(g_{ab}=\Omega^{-2}\tilde g_{ab}\), take the form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \dd s^2={}&2\Omega^{-2}\dd\Omega\,\dd u
  +\Omega^{-2}(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2)\\
  &+\text{terms }\order(1)\text{ in }\dd u^2,\ \dd u\,\dd\theta,\ \dd u\,\dd\phi\\
  &+\text{terms }\order(\Omega^{-1})\text{ in }\dd\theta^2,\ \dd\theta\,\dd\phi,\
  \dd\phi^2,\ \dd\Omega\,\dd u,\ \dd\Omega^2,\ \dd\Omega\,\dd\theta,\ \dd\Omega\,\dd\phi.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{11.1.23}\label{eq:11.1.23}
\end{equation}
We make, now, the coordinate transformation \(v=2/\Omega\). In the coordinates
\(u,v,\theta,\phi\), the physical metric takes the form as \(v\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \dd s^2={}&-\dd v\,\dd u+\frac14v^2(\dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2)\\
  &+\text{terms }\order(1)\text{ in }\dd u^2,\ \dd u\,\dd\theta,\ \dd u\,\dd\phi\\
  &+\text{terms }\order(v)\text{ in }\dd\theta^2,\ \dd\theta\,\dd\phi,\ \dd\phi^2\\
  &+\text{terms }\order(1/v)\text{ in }\dd v\,\dd u,\ \dd v\,\dd\theta,\ \dd v\,\dd\phi\\
  &+\order(1/v^3)\,\dd u^2.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{11.1.24}\label{eq:11.1.24}
\end{equation}
By making a further coordinate transformation \(v\to v+f(u,\theta,\phi)\) the terms
of \(\order(1)\) in \(\dd u^2\) can be eliminated at only the expense of introducing
terms of \(\order(1)\) in \(\dd v\,\dd\theta\) and \(\dd v\,\dd\phi\). Having done this, we
transform to \lq\lq asymptotically Cartesian coordinates\rq\rq\ defined by
\[
  t=\tfrac12(u+v),\qquad
  x=\tfrac12(v-u)\sin\theta\cos\phi,\qquad
  y=\tfrac12(v-u)\sin\theta\sin\phi,\qquad
  z=\tfrac12(v-u)\cos\theta.
\]
Then it is easily verified that the components of the physical metric in these
coordinates differ from \(\diag(-1,1,1,1)\) only by terms at most of order \(1/v\) as
\(v\to\infty\). Thus, condition 4(a) together with Einstein's equation
\eqref{eq:11.1.16} indeed requires the physical spacetime to become asymptotically
Minkowskian as one goes toward null infinity. By judiciously imposing further
coordinate conditions (Tamburino and Winicour 1966) one can further simplify the
asymptotic formula for the metric to the type of expression originally postulated in
the axisymmetric case by Bondi, van der Burg, and Metzner (1962).

Similarly, condition 4(b) together with the differentiability requirements on
\(\Omega\) and \(\tilde g_{ab}\) at \(\ii^0\) imply that the metric is asymptotically
Minkowskian as one approaches spatial infinity. A precise coordinate form of this
statement is given by Ashtekar and Hansen (1978) (see Eq.~[C21] of that reference).
Ashtekar and Hansen also have proven that any three-dimensional spacelike
submanifold of the unphysical spacetime which passes through \(\ii^0\) and is
sufficiently well behaved (namely, \(C^{>1}\), i.e., its intrinsic metric
\(\tilde h_{ab}\) and unit normal \(\tilde n^a\) are \(C^{>0}\) at \(\ii^0\) and smooth
elsewhere) yields an asymptotically flat initial data surface in the following sense
(Geroch 1972b): An initial data set \((\Sigma,h_{ab},K_{ab})\) consisting of a
three-dimensional manifold, \(\Sigma\), a Riemannian metric, \(h_{ab}\), and a
symmetric tensor \(K_{ab}\) is said to be \emph{asymptotically flat} if there exists a
manifold \(\tilde\Sigma\) such that (i) there is a point \(\Lambda\in\tilde\Sigma\)
(called \lq\lq the point at infinity\rq\rq) such that there exists a conformal isometry
\(\psi:\Sigma\to[\tilde\Sigma-\Lambda]\) with
\(\psi^*\tilde h_{ab}=\Omega^2h_{ab}\) on \(\tilde\Sigma-\Lambda\); (ii) \(\Omega\)
is \(C^2\) at \(\Lambda\) and \(C^\infty\) elsewhere on \(\tilde\Sigma\), while
\(\tilde h_{ab}\) is \(C^{>0}\) at \(\Lambda\) and \(C^\infty\) elsewhere on
\(\tilde\Sigma\); (iii) \(\Omega\) satisfies conditions at \(\Lambda\) analogous to
condition 4(b) above, namely, \(\Omega(\Lambda)=0\) and
\(\lim_\Lambda\tilde D_a\Omega=0\) while
\(\Omega^{-1}(\tilde D_a\tilde D_b\Omega-2\tilde h_{ab})\) approaches a finite,
direction-dependent limit at \(\Lambda\), where \(\tilde D_a\) is the derivative
operator associated with \(\tilde h_{ab}\); and (iv) the unphysical Ricci tensor
\({}^{(3)}\tilde R_{ab}\) and \(\psi^*K_{ab}\) have appropriate limiting behavior at
\(\Lambda\), namely \(\Omega^{1/2}{}^{(3)}\tilde R_{ab}\) and
\(\Omega\psi^*K_{ab}\) approach finite, direction-dependent limits at \(\Lambda\).
For an asymptotically flat initial data set, one can construct an asymptotically
Euclidean coordinate system on the physical initial data surface \(\Sigma\) such that
the components of the physical metric differ from \(\diag(1,1,1)\) only by terms
\(\order(1/r)\) as \(r\to\infty\) and the first derivatives of these components are
\(\order(1/r^2)\) (see Problem~2). Furthermore, the components of the extrinsic
curvature, \(K_{ab}\), and the physical Ricci curvature tensor, \({}^{(3)}R_{ab}\),
are, respectively, \(\order(1/r^2)\) and \(\order(1/r^3)\). Conditions for
asymptotic flatness on an initial data surface formulated in this coordinate component
manner were previously given by Arnowitt, Deser, and Misner (1962).

Finally, condition (5) of our above definition of asymptotic flatness is a technical
condition which, roughly speaking, states that \(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\)
have the right size. First, condition 5(a) states that all the null geodesic generators
of \(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\) emanate from \(\ii^0\) in the appropriate way.
In particular, this implies that \(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\) have topology
\(S^2\times\mathbb R\). If \(\tilde g_{ab}\) were smooth at \(\ii^0\), the null tangents
at \(\ii^0\) automatically would be diffeomorphic to the null geodesic generators of
\(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\) if all these generators had endpoints at \(\ii^0\),
but since \(\tilde g_{ab}\) is only \(C^{>0}\) at \(\ii^0\), this property must be imposed
separately. Condition 5(b) requires \lq\lq all of \(\mathcal I^+\) and
\(\mathcal I^-\) to be present\rq\rq\ in the conformally completed spacetime. It is
equivalent to the statement that in the gauge \eqref{eq:11.1.20} the null geodesic
generators of \(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\) are complete. [The relation
\(\tilde\nabla_a(\omega^4n^a)=0\) is equivalent to Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.18} above.]
The completeness of the null geodesic generators of \(\mathcal I^+\) is equivalent in
turn to the statement that in our coordinate system \((u,v,\theta,\phi)\) constructed
above, the retarded time, \(u\), ranges from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\). Without a
condition of the form 5(b), Geroch and Horowitz (1978) have shown that asymptotic
flatness could cease to hold at a finite retarded time.

Note that our conditions (1)--(5) above define asymptotic flatness at \emph{both}
spatial and null infinity. However, for many purposes one requires only the notion
of asymptotic flatness at spatial infinity alone or at null infinity alone, so it is
useful to define these notions separately. These notions can be formulated by
eliminating the irrelevant parts of our definition above. More precisely, a spacetime
\((M,g_{ab})\) (not necessarily satisfying the vacuum field equation) is said to be
\emph{asymptotically flat at spatial infinity} if there is a spacetime
\((\tilde M,\tilde g_{ab})\) with \(\tilde g_{ab}\) \(C^\infty\) everywhere except
possibly at a point \(\ii^0\) where it is \(C^{>0}\) and a conformal isometry of \(M\)
into \(\tilde M\) with conformal factor \(\Omega\) such that conditions 1, 3, and 4(b)
are satisfied. A \emph{vacuum} spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) is said to be
\emph{asymptotically flat at null infinity} if there exists a manifold with boundary
(see Appendix~B), \(\tilde M\), with smooth metric \(\tilde g_{ab}\) and a conformal
isometry of \(M\) onto the interior of \(\tilde M\) with conformal factor \(\Omega\)
such that appropriate versions of conditions 1, 2, 3, 4(a), and 5 hold. More
precisely, we define \(\mathcal I\) to be the boundary of \(\tilde M\), and in place of
1 we require that \(\mathcal I\) can be written as the disjoint union of two pieces,
\(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\), such that
\(\mathcal I^+\cap J^-[\operatorname{int}(\tilde M)]=\varnothing\) and
\(\mathcal I^-\cap J^+[\operatorname{int}(\tilde M)]=\varnothing\). We again
require the unphysical spacetime to be strongly causal in a neighborhood of
\(\mathcal I\) (condition 2), that \(\Omega\) have a \(C^\infty\) extension to all of
\(\tilde M\) (condition 3), and that conditions 4(a) and 5(b) hold on \(\mathcal I\).
Finally we replace 5(a) by the requirement that \(\mathcal I^+\) and \(\mathcal I^-\)
each have the topology \(S^2\times\mathbb R\).

The above definition of asymptotic flatness at null infinity differs from the original
formulation given by Penrose (1963, 1965b). Penrose defined a spacetime to be
\emph{asymptotically simple} if in place of the above versions of conditions 1, 2, 3,
4(a), and 5 we require that \(\Omega\) have a \(C^\infty\) extension to all of
\(\tilde M\), that condition 4(a) hold, and that every maximally extended (in both
directions) null geodesic passing through any point of \(\operatorname{int}(\tilde M)\)
has past and future endpoints on \(\mathcal I\). This last condition is a very strong
global condition on the physical spacetime, involving much more than asymptotic
behavior at infinity. However, it can be modified to become an asymptotic
condition---thereby defining the notion of a \emph{weakly asymptotically simple
spacetime}---by requiring that a neighborhood of \(\mathcal I\) in
\((\tilde M,\tilde g_{ab})\) for the given spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) be isometric to a
neighborhood of \(\mathcal I\) for some asymptotically simple spacetime. Weak
asymptotic simplicity then was taken as the criterion for asymptotic flatness at null
infinity. Although this formulation is indirect and differs significantly in form from
the one given above, it follows that a weakly asymptotically simple spacetime
satisfies all the conditions of our definition except for 5(b); see Geroch and
Horowitz (1978) for a demonstration that condition 5(b) need not be satisfied and
see, e.g., Hawking and Ellis (1973) for the theorems needed to show that the other
conditions are satisfied.

We conclude this section by mentioning two important properties of asymptotically
flat spacetimes. The first has to do with the notion of asymptotic symmetries.
Minkowski spacetime \((\mathbb R^4,\eta_{ab})\) has a 10-parameter group of
isometries, the Poincar\'e group. This isometry group plays an important role in the
analysis of the behavior of physical fields on Minkowski spacetime, in particular in
the proof of conservation laws. In a general, curved spacetime one would not expect
any exact isometries to be present. However, in an asymptotically flat spacetime,
since the metric becomes flat as one approaches infinity, one might expect that it
would be possible to define the notion of an asymptotic symmetry. It turns out that
a natural notion of an asymptotic symmetry does indeed exist for asymptotically flat
spacetimes, but the group of asymptotic symmetries is \emph{not} the Poincar\'e
group. Rather, it is a much larger group, containing an infinite-dimensional subgroup
of \lq\lq angle dependent translations\rq\rq\ called supertranslations.

We shall give a brief discussion, here, of the nature of asymptotic symmetries at
null infinity, referring the reader to Sachs (1962c) and Geroch (1977) for further
discussion and details. Basically, we want an infinitesimal asymptotic symmetry at,
say, future null infinity to be represented by a vector field \(\xi^a\)---or, more
precisely, an equivalence class of vector fields---in the physical spacetime such that
Killing's equation \(\Lie_\xi g_{ab}=0\) is satisfied to \lq\lq as good an
approximation as possible\rq\rq\ as one goes to \(\mathcal I^+\). The appropriate
requirement for the rate of approach of \(\Lie_\xi g_{ab}\) to zero can be determined
by examining the general form of a metric of an asymptotically flat spacetime and
imposing the strongest condition which generally admits solutions. We may formulate
the resulting requirement as follows. First, we require that \(\xi^a\), viewed as a
vector field in the unphysical spacetime (i.e., \(\psi_*\xi^a\)), have a smooth
extension to \(\mathcal I^+\). Then we require in addition that the tensor field
\(\Omega^2\Lie_\xi g_{ab}\) also have a smooth extension to \(\mathcal I^+\) which
vanishes on \(\mathcal I^+\). Furthermore, two vector fields \(\xi^a\) and
\(\xi'^a\) on the physical spacetime satisfying these conditions are considered to
generate the same infinitesimal asymptotic symmetry if their extensions to
\(\mathcal I^+\) are equal there.

The asymptotic symmetry group thus defined is universal in the sense that one gets
the same abstract group for all asymptotically flat spacetimes. It is initially
surprising, however, that this group is not the 10-parameter Poincar\'e group but
rather an infinite dimensional group known as the Bondi-Metzner-Sachs (BMS) group.
Insight into the origin of these \lq\lq extra\rq\rq\ asymptotic symmetries can be obtained
by verifying that in Minkowski spacetime in the coordinates of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.4}, for an arbitrary function \(f=f(\theta,\phi)\) of the angular
variables, the vector field
\begin{equation}
  \xi^a=f(\partial/\partial u)^a
  +\frac{v}{2r^2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}(\partial/\partial\theta)^a
  +\frac{v}{2r^2\sin^2\theta}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}(\partial/\partial\phi)^a
  \tag{11.1.25}\label{eq:11.1.25}
\end{equation}
is nonvanishing at \(\mathcal I^+\) but \(\Omega^2\Lie_\xi\eta_{ab}\) vanishes at
\(\mathcal I^+\). Now, in a nonflat but asymptotically flat spacetime, one cannot, in
general, find any vector field which satisfies Killing's equation near infinity to a
better approximation than this. Hence, in a general curved spacetime an infinite
dimensional family of \lq\lq angle dependent translations\rq\rq\ of the above type come
just as close to satisfying Killing's equation as any other transformations.

We have introduced the BMS group here in terms of approximate symmetries in the
physical spacetime as one approaches \(\mathcal I^+\). However, the asymptotic
symmetry transformations of the physical spacetime extend to transformations of
\(\mathcal I^+\) in the unphysical spacetime, and it turns out that one can give an
equivalent characterization of the BMS group in terms of mappings of
\(\mathcal I^+\) into itself. In the unphysical spacetime, the unphysical metric
\(\tilde g_{ab}\) induces a degenerate metric \(\tilde h_{ab}\) on the null
hypersurface \(\mathcal I^+\). Furthermore, since the vector field
\(n^a=\tilde g^{ab}\tilde\nabla_b\Omega\) is tangent to \(\mathcal I^+\), \(n^a\) may
be viewed as a vector field on \(\mathcal I^+\). Under a change of conformal gauge
\(\Omega\to\omega\Omega\), \(\tilde h_{ab}\) and \(n^a\) transform as
\(\tilde h_{ab}\to\tilde h'_{ab}=\omega^2\tilde h_{ab}\),
\(n^a\to(n')^a=\omega^{-1}n^a\). The manifold \(\mathcal I^+\) together with
\(\tilde h_{ab}\) and \(n^a\) modulo conformal gauge are \emph{universal}, i.e.,
they are \lq\lq the same\rq\rq\ for all asymptotically flat spacetimes in the following
sense: Let \((\mathcal I_1^+,(\tilde h_1)_{ab},(n_1)^a)\) be the future null infinity,
induced degenerate metric, and induced vector field associated with the
asymptotically flat (physical) spacetime \((M_1,(g_1)_{ab})\) and let
\((\mathcal I_2^+,(\tilde h_2)_{ab},(n_2)^a)\) be the corresponding structure for the
asymptotically flat spacetime \((M_2,(g_2)_{ab})\). Then there exist choices of
conformal gauge in the two unphysical spacetimes such that there is a
diffeomorphism \(\psi:\mathcal I_1^+\to\mathcal I_2^+\) satisfying
\(\psi^*(\tilde h_2)_{ab}=(\tilde h_1)_{ab}\),
\(\psi^*(n_2)^a=(n_1)^a\). [Proof. We showed above that for any
\(\mathcal I^+\) there exists a conformal gauge and a choice of coordinates
\((u,\theta,\phi)\) such that
\(\tilde h_{ab}=(\dd\theta)_a(\dd\theta)_b+
\sin^2\theta(\dd\phi)_a(\dd\phi)_b\) and \(n^a=(\partial/\partial u)^a\). Let
\(\psi\) be the map which associates with each point on \(\mathcal I_1^+\) the point
on \(\mathcal I_2^+\) with the same value of these coordinates.] The BMS group may
be characterized as the group of diffeomorphisms of \(\mathcal I^+\) which preserves
this universal structure; i.e., the BMS group consists of the diffeomorphisms
\(\psi:\mathcal I^+\to\mathcal I^+\) such that \(\psi^*\tilde h_{ab}\) and
\(\psi^*n^a\) differ from \(\tilde h_{ab}\) and \(n^a\) at most by a rescaling
associated with a change of conformal gauge.

In terms of this characterization of the BMS group, the (infinitesimal)
\emph{supertranslations} are defined as the vector fields on \(\mathcal I^+\) of the
form \(\xi^a=\alpha n^a\) where \(\alpha\) is constant on each generator of
\(\mathcal I^+\) (i.e., \(n^a\tilde\nabla_a\alpha=0\)) but otherwise is an arbitrary
function. Thus, the \lq\lq angle dependent translations\rq\rq\ of Minkowski spacetime
considered above give rise to BMS supertranslations. The supertranslations comprise
an infinite-dimensional abelian normal subgroup of the BMS group, and the factor
group obtained by quotienting the BMS group by the supertranslations is isomorphic
to the Lorentz group.

Although for the reasons discussed above, the BMS group properly comprises the
asymptotic symmetry group of an asymptotically flat spacetime, one still may ask if
there is some natural procedure for \lq\lq recovering\rq\rq\ the Poincar\'e group by
imposing extra conditions on the asymptotic symmetries. It turns out that one can
partially succeed in doing this as follows. There exists a unique four-dimensional
subgroup of the supertranslations which is a normal subgroup of the BMS group. In
the case of Minkowski spacetime, this four-dimensional subgroup of the BMS group
consists precisely of the asymptotic symmetries associated with the exact
translational symmetries of Minkowski spacetime. Therefore, we may define the
\emph{asymptotic translations} of a general asymptotically flat spacetime as the BMS
elements belonging to this subgroup. (On \(\mathcal I^+\) in the gauge
\eqref{eq:11.1.20} with metric spherical cross sections, the infinitesimal translations
are precisely the supertranslations of the form \(\xi^a=\alpha n^a\) with \(\alpha\) a
linear combination of \(l=0\) and \(l=1\) spherical harmonics.) However, a similar
procedure for the rotations and boosts fails. There exists no normal subgroup of the
BMS group which is isomorphic to the Poincar\'e group. Thus, unless one makes use
of more structure than the behavior of asymptotic symmetries on \(\mathcal I^+\)
(such as, e.g., the behavior of the asymptotic symmetries at \(\ii^0\)) or imposes
stronger conditions on spacetimes than those given in our definition of asymptotic
flatness, there exists no natural way of picking out a preferred Poincar\'e subgroup of
asymptotic symmetries. The notion of a \lq\lq pure translation\rq\rq\ makes sense for a
general asymptotically flat spacetime, but the notion of a \lq\lq pure rotation\rq\rq\ or
\lq\lq pure boost\rq\rq\ (as opposed to a \lq\lq rotation plus supertranslation\rq\rq\ or
\lq\lq boost plus supertranslation\rq\rq) does not.

The group of asymptotic symmetries at spatial infinity---known as the \emph{Spi
group}---can be defined in a manner closely analogous to the BMS group. The
structure of this group is very similar to that of the BMS group. We refer the reader
to Ashtekar and Hansen (1978) and Ashtekar (1980) for a discussion of its properties.
Interestingly, by restricting consideration to spacetimes which satisfy a stronger
asymptotic falloff condition on the Weyl tensor at spatial infinity than that implied by
our definition of asymptotic flatness, it is possible to pick out a preferred Poincar\'e
subgroup of the Spi group (see Ashtekar 1980). A mathematically analogous condition
on the Weyl tensor at null infinity also would allow one to obtain a preferred
Poincar\'e subgroup of the BMS group. However, although this additional condition at
spatial infinity appears to be physically reasonable, the analogous condition at null
infinity excludes the possibility of gravitational radiation, and thus is much too
stringent a condition to impose upon general isolated systems.

Finally we mention an important consequence of our definition of asymptotic flatness
concerning the asymptotic behavior of the curvature as one goes to null infinity. The
physical Ricci tensor vanishes near infinity so the physical Weyl tensor,
\(C_{abc}{}^d\), contains all the information about the physical curvature. Since the
Weyl tensor is conformally invariant (see Appendix~D), we have
\(C_{abc}{}^d=\tilde C_{abc}{}^d\) on \(M\), where \(\tilde C_{abc}{}^d\) is the
unphysical Weyl tensor. However, it is possible to show (see theorem 11 of Geroch
1977) that \(\tilde C_{abc}{}^d\) must vanish at \(\mathcal I^+\). Now, let
\(\gamma\) be any null geodesic in \(\tilde M\) from a point \(p\) in the physical
spacetime to a point \(q\) on \(\mathcal I^+\). Let \(\lambda\) be a (physical) affine
parameter on \(\gamma\)---so that \(q\) is represented as the limit as \(\lambda\)
goes to infinity---and let \(k^a\) denote the tangent to \(\gamma\) in this
parameterization. The unphysical Weyl curvature on \(\gamma\) need not satisfy any
special property except that it must vanish at \(q\). However, in going from the
unphysical to the physical spacetime, an infinite amount of \lq\lq stretching\rq\rq\ in
the direction of \(k^a\) takes place. It turns out that this causes the physical Weyl
tensor to display the following \emph{peeling property}: As \(\lambda\to\infty\), we
have
\begin{equation}
  C_{abcd}=
  \frac{C^{(1)}_{abcd}}{\lambda}
  +\frac{C^{(2)}_{abcd}}{\lambda^2}
  +\frac{C^{(3)}_{abcd}}{\lambda^3}
  +\frac{C^{(4)}_{abcd}}{\lambda^4}
  +\order\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^5}\right),
  \tag{11.1.26}\label{eq:11.1.26}
\end{equation}
where each \(C^{(i)}_{abcd}\) has bounded components in an asymptotically
Minkowskian basis, and, in the algebraic classification of Section~7.3,
\(C^{(1)}_{abcd}\) is type IV, \(C^{(2)}_{abcd}\) is type III,
\(C^{(3)}_{abcd}\) is type II (or type II--II), all with repeated principal null
vector \(k^a\), while \(C^{(4)}_{abcd}\) is type I with \(k^a\) also being one of
its principal null directions. For further discussion and a proof of this peeling
property, we refer the reader to Penrose (1965b) and Geroch (1977).
