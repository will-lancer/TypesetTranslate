\subsection{Black Holes and Thermodynamics}\label{sec:12.5}
\setcounter{theorem}{0}

The area theorem~12.2.6 states that in any physically allowed process, the total area
of all black holes in the universe cannot decrease, \(\delta A\geq0\). This law bears a
strong resemblance to the second law of thermodynamics, which states that in any
physically allowed process the total entropy of all matter in the universe cannot
decrease, \(\delta S\geq0\). It might appear that this similarity is of a very
superficial nature. After all, the area theorem is a mathematically rigorous
consequence of general relativity, whereas the second law of thermodynamics is
believed not to be a rigorous consequence of the laws of nature but rather a law
which holds with overwhelming likelihood for systems with a large number of degrees
of freedom. Nevertheless, we shall show in this section that this formal analog for
black holes of the second law of thermodynamics extends to the other laws of
thermodynamics as well. We will return to this issue in chapter 14, where further
evidence will be presented that the relationship between the laws of black hole physics
and the laws of thermodynamics is of a fundamental nature.

Our first task is to introduce a quantity, \(\kappa\), defined on the horizon of an
arbitrary stationary black hole (not necessarily vacuum or electrovac in its exterior
region) and derive a number of properties of \(\kappa\). As mentioned at the end of
Section~12.3, for a stationary black hole, there exists a Killing field, \(\chi^a\),
which is normal to the horizon of the black hole. If \(\chi^a\) does not coincide with
the stationary Killing field \(\xi^a\), we obtain an axial Killing field \(\psi^a\) in
the spacetime by taking a linear combination of \(\chi^a\) and \(\xi^a\). Thus, in
general we may write \(\chi^a\) as
\begin{equation}
 \chi^a=\xi^a+\Omega_H\psi^a ,
 \tag{12.5.1}\label{eq:12.5.1}
\end{equation}
where (as in the case of a Kerr black hole, equation~[12.3.20]) the constant
\(\Omega_H\) is called the angular velocity of the horizon. Since the horizon is a
null surface and \(\chi^a\) is normal to the horizon, we have \(\chi^a\chi_a=0\) on
the horizon, so, in particular, \(\chi^a\chi_a\) is constant on the horizon. Hence
\(\nabla^a(\chi^b\chi_b)\) also is normal to the horizon, so on the horizon there
exists a function \(\kappa\) such that
\begin{equation}
 \nabla^a(\chi^b\chi_b)=-2\kappa\chi^a .
 \tag{12.5.2}\label{eq:12.5.2}
\end{equation}
Taking the Lie derivative of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.2} with respect to the Killing
field \(\chi^a\) we find
\begin{equation}
 \Lie_\chi\kappa=0 ,
 \tag{12.5.3}\label{eq:12.5.3}
\end{equation}
i.e., \(\kappa\) is a constant on the orbits of \(\chi^a\). In fact, we shall prove
below that \(\kappa\) is constant over the horizon, i.e., its value does not change
from orbit to orbit. For a charged Kerr black hole, the value of \(\kappa\) is
\begin{equation}
 \kappa=\frac{(M^2-a^2-e^2)^{1/2}}
 {2M\left[M+(M^2-a^2-e^2)^{1/2}\right]-e^2} .
 \tag{12.5.4}\label{eq:12.5.4}
\end{equation}
We may rewrite equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.2} in the form
\begin{equation}
 \chi^b\nabla_a\chi_b=-\chi^b\nabla_b\chi_a=-\kappa\chi_a ,
 \tag{12.5.5}\label{eq:12.5.5}
\end{equation}
which is just the geodesic equation in a non-affine parameterization. Thus, \(\kappa\)
measures the failure of the Killing parameter, \(v\), defined by
\begin{equation}
 \chi^a\nabla_av=1 ,
 \tag{12.5.6}\label{eq:12.5.6}
\end{equation}
to agree with the affine parameter, \(\lambda\), along the null geodesic generators
of the horizon. If we define \(k^a\) on the horizon by
\begin{equation}
 k^a=e^{-\kappa v}\chi^a ,
 \tag{12.5.7}\label{eq:12.5.7}
\end{equation}
we find
\begin{equation}
 k^b\nabla_bk^a=e^{-2\kappa v}
 \left[\chi^b\nabla_b\chi^a-\chi^a\chi^b\nabla_b(\kappa v)\right]=0 ,
 \tag{12.5.8}\label{eq:12.5.8}
\end{equation}
i.e., \(k^a\) is the affinely parameterized tangent to the null geodesic generators
of the horizon. This shows that on the horizon the relation between affine parameter
\(\lambda\) and Killing parameter \(v\), is given by
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd\lambda}{\dd v}\mathrel{\propto}e^{\kappa v} ,
 \tag{12.5.9}\label{eq:12.5.9}
\end{equation}
so that, if \(\kappa\ne0\), we have
\begin{equation}
 \lambda\mathrel{\propto}e^{\kappa v} .
 \tag{12.5.10}\label{eq:12.5.10}
\end{equation}

Since \(\chi^a\) is hypersurface orthogonal at the horizon, by Frobenius's theorem
(see appendix B) we have on the horizon
\begin{equation}
 \chi_{[a}\nabla_b\chi_{c]}=0 .
 \tag{12.5.11}\label{eq:12.5.11}
\end{equation}
Using Killing's equation \(\nabla_b\chi_c=-\nabla_c\chi_b\), this implies
\begin{equation}
 \chi_c\nabla_a\chi_b=-2\chi_{[a}\nabla_{b]}\chi_c
 \tag{12.5.12}\label{eq:12.5.12}
\end{equation}
on the horizon. Contracting with \(\nabla^a\chi^b\), we find
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \chi_c(\nabla^a\chi^b)(\nabla_a\chi_b)
 &=-2(\chi_a\nabla^a\chi^b)(\nabla_b\chi_c)\\
 &=-2\kappa\chi^b\nabla_b\chi_c\\
 &=-2\kappa^2\chi_c .
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.5.13}\label{eq:12.5.13}
\end{equation}
Thus, we obtain a simple explicit formula for \(\kappa\),
\begin{equation}
 \kappa^2=-\frac12(\nabla^a\chi^b)(\nabla_a\chi_b) ,
 \tag{12.5.14}\label{eq:12.5.14}
\end{equation}
where evaluation on the horizon is understood.

Equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.14} provides us with a physical interpretation of
\(\kappa\) as follows. We have everywhere (i.e., not just on the horizon)
\begin{equation}
 3(\chi^{[a}\nabla^b\chi^{c]})(\chi_{[a}\nabla_b\chi_{c]})
 =\chi^a\chi_a(\nabla^b\chi^c)(\nabla_b\chi_c)
 -2(\chi^a\nabla^b\chi^c)(\chi_b\nabla_a\chi_c) .
 \tag{12.5.15}\label{eq:12.5.15}
\end{equation}
Since \(\chi_{[a}\nabla_b\chi_{c]}=0\) on the horizon, the gradient of the
left-hand side vanishes on the horizon. On the other hand, by
equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.2}, \(\nabla_b(\chi^a\chi_a)\ne0\) on the horizon, provided
that \(\kappa\ne0\). Hence, by l'Hospital's rule, the left-hand side of
equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.15} divided by \(\chi^a\chi_a\) must approach zero on the
horizon. Thus, using equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.14}, we find
\begin{equation}
 \kappa^2=\lim\left\{-\frac{(\chi^b\nabla_b\chi^c)(\chi^a\nabla_a\chi_c)}
 {\chi^d\chi_d}\right\} ,
 \tag{12.5.16}\label{eq:12.5.16}
\end{equation}
where \lq\lq lim\rq\rq\ stands for the limit as one approaches the horizon. Now,
\begin{equation}
 a^c=\frac{\chi^b\nabla_b\chi^c}{-\chi^a\chi_a}
 \tag{12.5.17}\label{eq:12.5.17}
\end{equation}
is just the acceleration of an orbit of \(\chi^a\). Thus, we have
\begin{equation}
 \kappa=\lim(Va) ,
 \tag{12.5.18}\label{eq:12.5.18}
\end{equation}
where \(a=(a^ca_c)^{1/2}\) and \(V=(-\chi^a\chi_a)^{1/2}\). In the case of a
static black hole, we have \(\chi^a=\xi^a\). Then \(V\) is just the redshift factor,
and, by problem 4 of chapter 6, \(Va\) is the force that must be exerted at infinity
to hold a unit test mass in place. Thus, \(\kappa\) is the limiting value of this
force at the horizon; i.e., it is the \emph{surface gravity} of the black hole. (Of
course, the locally exerted force, \(a\), becomes infinite on the horizon.) For a
rotating black hole, \(\Omega_H\ne0\), a test mass cannot be held stationary with
respect to infinity near the black hole, but we shall continue to refer to \(\kappa\) as
the surface gravity.

Using equations~\eqref{eq:12.5.7} and~\eqref{eq:12.5.11}, we find on the horizon
\begin{equation}
 k_{[a}\nabla_{b]}k_c=-e^{-2\kappa v}\chi_c
 \left[\frac12\nabla_a\chi_b+\chi_{[a}\nabla_{b]}(\kappa v)\right] .
 \tag{12.5.19}\label{eq:12.5.19}
\end{equation}
Contracting equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.19} with any two vectors \(m^b\), \(n^c\)
tangent to the horizon (i.e., \(\chi^am_a=\chi^an_a=0\)), we obtain
\begin{equation}
 m^bn^c\nabla_bk_c=0 ,
 \tag{12.5.20}\label{eq:12.5.20}
\end{equation}
i.e., in the notation of Section~9.2, we have
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\nabla_a k_b}=0 .
 \tag{12.5.21}\label{eq:12.5.21}
\end{equation}
Thus, the expansion \(\theta\), twist \(\widehat{\omega}_{ab}\), and shear
\(\widehat{\sigma}_{ab}\) of the null geodesic generators of the horizon vanish.
From equations~(9.2.32) and~(9.2.33), we see also that on the horizon
\begin{equation}
 R_{ab}k^ak^b=0
 \tag{12.5.22}\label{eq:12.5.22}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{C}_{abcd}k^ak^d=0 .
 \tag{12.5.23}\label{eq:12.5.23}
\end{equation}
The latter equation states that \(k^a\) is a principal null vector of the Weyl tensor
(see Section~7.3). In fact, we shall see below that \(k^a\) must be a repeated
principal null vector.

It should be emphasized that most of the above equations---in particular, the
relation~\eqref{eq:12.5.2} defining \(\kappa\)---hold only on the horizon. Thus, we
may not simply apply \(\nabla_a\) to, say, equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.2} since we may
differentiate equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.2} only in directions tangent to the horizon. If
the horizon were a spacelike surface, we could apply \(h^{ab}\nabla_a\) to all
equations, where the projection operator \(h_{ab}\) was defined by equation~(10.2.10).
However, the horizon is a null surface and has no natural projection operator
associated with it. Nevertheless, the tensor \(\epsilon^{abcd}\chi_d\), where
\(\epsilon_{abcd}\) is the spacetime volume element (see appendix B) is tangent to
the horizon, since \((\epsilon^{abcd}\chi_d)\chi_c=0\). Thus, we may apply
\(\epsilon^{abcd}\chi_d\nabla_c\) to any equation holding on the horizon.
Equivalently, we may apply \(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\) to any such equation.

We now shall prove that the surface gravity, \(\kappa\), is constant over the event
horizon. Applying \(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\) to equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.5}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \chi_a\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\kappa+\kappa\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\chi_a
 &=\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}(\chi^b\nabla_b\chi_a)\\
 &=(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\chi^b)(\nabla_b\chi_a)
   +\chi^b\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\nabla_b\chi_a\\
 &=(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\chi^b)(\nabla_b\chi_a)
   -\chi^bR_{ba[c}{}^e\chi_{d]}\chi_e ,
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.5.24}\label{eq:12.5.24}
\end{equation}
where equation~(C.3.6) in appendix C was used in the last step. However, using
equations~\eqref{eq:12.5.12} and~\eqref{eq:12.5.5}, the first term on the right-hand
side of the last line of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.24} is found to be
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 (\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\chi^b)(\nabla_b\chi_a)
 &=-\frac12(\chi^b\nabla_d\chi_c)\nabla_b\chi_a\\
 &=-\frac12\kappa\chi_a\nabla_d\chi_c\\
 &=\kappa\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\chi_a ,
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.5.25}\label{eq:12.5.25}
\end{equation}
which cancels the second term on the left-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.24}.
Thus, we obtain
\begin{equation}
 \chi_a\chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\kappa
 =\chi^bR_{ab[c}{}^e\chi_{d]}\chi_e .
 \tag{12.5.26}\label{eq:12.5.26}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, if we apply \(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{e]}\) to
equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.12}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 (\chi_{[d}\nabla_{e]}\chi_c)\nabla_a\chi_b
 +\chi_c\chi_{[d}\nabla_{e]}\nabla_a\chi_b
 ={}&-2(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{e]}\chi_{[a})\nabla_{b]}\chi_c\\
 &-2(\chi_{[d}\nabla_{e]}\nabla_b\chi_{[c})\chi_{a]} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.5.27}\label{eq:12.5.27}
\end{equation}
Using equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.12} repeatedly, we find that the first term on the
left-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.27} cancels the first term on the
right-hand side. Thus, using equation~(C.3.6), we obtain
\begin{equation}
 -\chi_cR_{ab[e}{}^f\chi_{d]}\chi_f
 =2\chi_{[a}R_{b]c[e}{}^f\chi_{d]}\chi_f .
 \tag{12.5.28}\label{eq:12.5.28}
\end{equation}
If we multiply by \(g^{ce}\) and contract over \(c\) and \(e\), the left-hand side
vanishes, and we find
\begin{equation}
 -\chi_{[a}R_{b]}{}^f\chi_f\chi_d
 =\chi_{[a}R_{b]cd}{}^f\chi^c\chi_f .
 \tag{12.5.29}\label{eq:12.5.29}
\end{equation}
However, the term on the right-hand side of this equation is of the same form as the
right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.26}. Thus, comparison of these two
equations yields
\begin{equation}
 \chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\kappa=-\chi_{[d}R_{c]}{}^f\chi_f .
 \tag{12.5.30}\label{eq:12.5.30}
\end{equation}
Up to this point we have not used Einstein's equation anywhere in the above analysis.
However, we show now that Einstein's equation together with the dominant energy
condition (defined in Section~9.2) implies that the right-hand side of
equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.30} vanishes. Namely, the dominant energy condition states
that \(-T^a{}_b\chi^b\) must be a future directed timelike or null vector. However,
Einstein's equation together with equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.22} implies
\(T^a{}_b\chi^b\chi_a=0\). This implies that \(-T^a{}_b\chi^b\) must point in the
direction of \(\chi^a\), i.e., \(\chi_{[c}T_{a]b}\chi^b=0\). Hence using Einstein's
equation again, we find that the right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.30} also
must vanish, and we conclude that
\begin{equation}
 \chi_{[d}\nabla_{c]}\kappa=0 ,
 \tag{12.5.31}\label{eq:12.5.31}
\end{equation}
which states that \(\kappa\) is constant on the horizon. Note, incidentally, that the
vanishing of both sides of equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.29} implies by equation~(3.2.28)
that \(\chi_{[a}C_{b]cdf}\chi^c\chi^f=0\), i.e., that \(\chi^a\) is a twice repeated
principal null vector of the Weyl tensor on the horizon.

A simple formula for the mass of a stationary, axisymmetric spacetime containing a
black hole now may be obtained (Bardeen, Carter, and Hawking 1973). Let
\(\Sigma\) be an asymptotically flat spacelike hypersurface which intersects the
horizon, \(H\), on a 2-sphere, \(\mathcal H\), which forms the boundary of
\(\Sigma\). The calculation which led to equation~(11.2.10) of the previous chapter
is modified only by the presence of an additional boundary term due to \(\mathcal H\).
We obtain
\begin{equation}
 M=2\int_\Sigma\left(T_{ab}-\frac12Tg_{ab}\right)n^a\xi^b\dd V
 -\frac1{8\pi}\int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\xi^d .
 \tag{12.5.32}\label{eq:12.5.32}
\end{equation}
We may evaluate this boundary term by using equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.1} to write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\xi^d
 &=\int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\chi^d
   -\Omega_H\int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\psi^d\\
 &=\int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\chi^d-16\pi\Omega_HJ_H ,
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.5.33}\label{eq:12.5.33}
\end{equation}
where we may interpret \(J_H\equiv(1/16\pi)\int_{\mathcal H}
\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\psi^d\) as the angular momentum of the black hole (see
problem 6 of chapter 11). On the other hand, we may express the volume element,
\(\epsilon_{ab}\), on \(\mathcal H\) as
\begin{equation}
 \epsilon_{ab}=\epsilon_{abcd}N^c\chi^d ,
 \tag{12.5.34}\label{eq:12.5.34}
\end{equation}
where \(N^a\) is the \lq\lq ingoing\rq\rq\ future directed null normal to
\(\mathcal H\), normalized so that \(N^a\chi_a=-1\). Thus, we have
\begin{equation}
 \epsilon^{ab}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\chi^d
 =N_e\chi_f\epsilon^{abef}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\chi^d
 =-4N_c\chi_d\nabla^c\chi^d=-4\kappa ,
 \tag{12.5.35}\label{eq:12.5.35}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\nabla^c\chi^d
 =\frac12\int_{\mathcal H}(\epsilon^{ef}\epsilon_{efcd}\nabla^c\chi^d)\epsilon_{ab}
 =-2\kappa A ,
 \tag{12.5.36}\label{eq:12.5.36}
\end{equation}
where \(A=\int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{ab}\) is the area of the event horizon. Thus,
we obtain the following formula for \(M\):
\begin{equation}
 M=2\int_\Sigma\left(T_{ab}-\frac12Tg_{ab}\right)n^a\xi^b\dd V
 +\frac1{4\pi}\kappa A+2\Omega_HJ_H .
 \tag{12.5.37}\label{eq:12.5.37}
\end{equation}

Of greatest interest for the development of the analogy between the laws of black
hole physics and the laws of thermodynamics is the derivation of a differential
formula for \(M\), i.e., a formula for how \(M\) changes when a small stationary,
axisymmetric change is made in the solution. We may use the freedom of applying
diffeomorphisms to solutions to ensure that \(\xi^a\), \(\psi^a\), and the location of
the horizon on the manifold remain unchanged when we vary the spacetime metric.
For simplicity, we shall treat only the vacuum case\footnote{Actually, as discussed
at the end of Section~12.3, in the vacuum case, the only possible black hole solutions
are the Kerr metrics, so our formula~(12.5.44) below could be derived simply by
verifying that it holds for the Kerr metrics. However, as mentioned in the text, the
derivation we give generalizes to the case where matter is present outside the black
hole.}, \(T_{ab}=0\). A generalization of the differential mass formula to the case
of fluid matter outside the black hole was given in the original paper of Bardeen,
Carter, and Hawking (1973), and further generalizations have been given by Carter
(1973).

A formula for \(\delta M\) in the vacuum case can be obtained by varying
equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.37},
\begin{equation}
 \delta M=\frac1{4\pi}(A\,\delta\kappa+\kappa\,\delta A)
 +2(J_H\delta\Omega_H+\Omega_H\delta J_H) .
 \tag{12.5.38}\label{eq:12.5.38}
\end{equation}
However, this is not the desired formula. Another formula for \(\delta M\) can be
derived as follows. First, we note that in any spacetime if \(v^a\) and \(w^a\)
commute and satisfy \(\nabla_av^a=\nabla_aw^a=0\), then
\begin{equation}
 \nabla_a(v^{[a}w^{b]})=0
 \tag{12.5.39}\label{eq:12.5.39}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 \nabla_{[a}(\epsilon_{bc]\dd e}v^dw^e)=0 .
 \tag{12.5.40}\label{eq:12.5.40}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if we apply Stokes's theorem to a three-dimensional volume bounded
by two spheres \(S\) and \(S'\), we find that
\(\int_S\epsilon_{abcd}v^cw^d=\int_{S'}\epsilon_{abcd}v^cw^d\). We apply this
general result here, choosing \(S\) to be a \lq\lq sphere at infinity,\rq\rq\ \(S'\) to be a
sphere, \(\mathcal H\), on the horizon, \(w^a=\xi^a\), and
\begin{equation}
 v^a=\nabla_b(h^{ab}-g^{ab}h) ,
 \tag{12.5.41}\label{eq:12.5.41}
\end{equation}
where \(h_{ab}\) is the perturbed metric and \(h=g^{ab}h_{ab}\). The above
required properties are satisfied by \(\xi^a\) and \(v^a\) since for any stationary
perturbation we have \(\Lie_\xi v^a=0\) (i.e., \(v^a\) and \(\xi^a\) commute), we
have \(\nabla_a\xi^a=0\) by Killing's equation, and \(\nabla_av^a=0\) is just the
trace of the perturbed vacuum Einstein equation, \(\dot R=0\) (see equation~[7.5.15]).
Thus, we find
\begin{equation}
 \int_S\epsilon_{abcd}\xi^d\nabla_e(h^{ce}-g^{ce}h)
 =\int_{\mathcal H}\epsilon_{abcd}\xi^d\nabla_e(h^{ce}-g^{ce}h) .
 \tag{12.5.42}\label{eq:12.5.42}
\end{equation}
If both the ADM and Komar expressions for total mass, equations~(11.2.14) and
(11.2.9), are used, the left-hand side is evaluated to be \(8\pi\delta M\). A
significantly longer computation reveals that the right-hand side is equal to
\(-2A\delta\kappa-16\pi J_H\delta\Omega_H\) (see Bardeen, Carter, and Hawking
1973). Thus, we obtain
\begin{equation}
 \delta M=-\frac1{4\pi}A\delta\kappa-2J_H\delta\Omega_H .
 \tag{12.5.43}\label{eq:12.5.43}
\end{equation}
Adding equations~\eqref{eq:12.5.38} and~\eqref{eq:12.5.43}, we obtain the desired
formula,
\begin{equation}
 \delta M=\frac1{8\pi}\kappa\delta A+\Omega_H\delta J_H .
 \tag{12.5.44}\label{eq:12.5.44}
\end{equation}
The close mathematical analogy between laws of black hole physics derived above and
the ordinary laws of thermodynamics is displayed in Table~12.1. We noted at the
beginning of this section that the black hole area theorem is analogous to the second
law of thermodynamics. Now we have obtained the formula~\eqref{eq:12.5.44} for
\(\delta M\), which is closely analogous to the first law of thermodynamics. In
particular, the term \(\Omega_H\delta J_H\) is analogous to the \lq\lq work term\rq\rq\
\(P\delta V\) of the first law; indeed, for an ordinary rotating body a term of the
form \(\Omega\delta J\) would be present in the thermodynamic formula. The term
\(\delta A\) appears in equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.44} in the same manner as \(\delta S\)
appears in the first law of thermodynamics, except that it is multiplied by
\((1/8\pi)\kappa\) rather than \(T\), so \(\kappa\) plays the role of temperature in the
black hole laws. But we proved above that \(\kappa\) satisfies an important property
analogous to the property of temperature in the zeroth law of thermodynamics: it is
uniform over an \lq\lq equilibrium\rq\rq\ (i.e., stationary) black hole. Finally, we see
from equation~\eqref{eq:12.5.4} that for the charged Kerr black holes, \(\kappa\)
vanishes only for the \lq\lq extreme\rq\rq\ case \(M^2=a^2+e^2\). Explicit calculations
(see, e.g., Wald 1974a) show that the closer one gets to an \lq\lq extreme\rq\rq\ black
hole, the harder it is to get a further step closer, in a manner similar to the third law
of thermodynamics. (However, the analog of the alternate version of the third law of
thermodynamics, which states that \(S\to0\) as \(T\to0\), is not satisfied in black
hole physics, since \(A\) may remain finite as \(\kappa\to0\).)

Note that the analogous quantities in Table~12.1 are \(E\leftrightarrow M\),
\(T\leftrightarrow\alpha\kappa\), and \(S\leftrightarrow(1/8\pi\alpha)A\), where
\(\alpha\) is a constant. A hint that the relation between black hole laws and
thermodynamic laws might be more than just an analogy comes from the fact that
\(E\) and \(M\) are not merely analogs in the formulas but represent the same
physical quantity: total energy. However, the thermodynamic temperature of a black
hole in classical general relativity is absolute zero since a black hole is a perfect
absorber but does not emit anything. Thus, it would appear that \(\kappa\) could not
physically represent a temperature. Nevertheless, in 1974 Hawking discovered that
