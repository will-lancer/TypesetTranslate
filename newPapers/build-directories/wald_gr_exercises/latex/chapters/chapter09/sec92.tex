\subsection{Timelike and Null Geodesic Congruences}\label{sec:9.2}

\setcounter{theorem}{0}

Let \(M\) be a manifold and let \(O\subseteq M\) be open. A \emph{congruence} in \(O\) is a
family of curves such that through each \(p\in O\) there passes precisely one curve in
this family. Thus, the tangents to a congruence yield a vector field in \(O\), and,
conversely, as discussed in section~2.2, every continuous vector field generates a
congruence of curves. The congruence is said to be smooth if the corresponding vector
field is smooth. In this section we shall define the expansion, shear, and twist of
timelike and null geodesic congruences in a spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) and derive
equations for their rate of change as one moves along the curves in the congruence.

Consider, first, a smooth congruence of timelike geodesics. Without loss of
generality, we may assume that the geodesics are parameterized by proper time
\(\tau\), so that the vector field, \(\xi^a\), of tangents is normalized to unit length,
\(\xi^a\xi_a=-1\). Then the tensor field, \(B_{ab}\), defined by

\begin{equation}
  B_{ab}=\nabla_b\xi_a ,
  \tag{9.2.1}\label{eq:9.2.1}
\end{equation}

will be purely ``spatial,'' i.e.,

\begin{equation}
  B_{ab}\xi^a=B_{ab}\xi^b=0 .
  \tag{9.2.2}\label{eq:9.2.2}
\end{equation}

The physical interpretation of \(B_{ab}\) may be seen from the following considerations.
Consider a smooth one-parameter subfamily \(\gamma_s(\tau)\) of geodesics in the
congruence, and let \(\eta^a\) be the orthogonal deviation vector from \(\gamma_0\) for
this subfamily (see section~3.3). Then \(\eta^a\) represents an infinitesimal spatial
displacement from \(\gamma_0\) to a nearby geodesic in the subfamily. We have

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_{\xi}\eta^a=0 ,
  \tag{9.2.3}\label{eq:9.2.3}
\end{equation}

and thus

\begin{equation}
  \xi^b\nabla_b\eta^a
  =\eta^b\nabla_b\xi^a
  =B^a{}_b\eta^b .
  \tag{9.2.4}\label{eq:9.2.4}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(B^a{}_b\) measures the failure of \(\eta^a\) to be parallelly transported. An
observer on the geodesic \(\gamma_0\) would find the nearby geodesics surrounding him
to be stretched and rotated by the linear map \(B^a{}_b\).

We define the ``spatial metric'' \(h_{ab}\) by

\begin{equation}
  h_{ab}=g_{ab}+\xi_a\xi_b .
  \tag{9.2.5}\label{eq:9.2.5}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(h^a{}_b=g^{ac}h_{cb}\) is the projection operator onto the subspace of the
tangent space perpendicular to \(\xi^a\). We define the \emph{expansion} \(\theta\), \emph{shear}
\(\sigma_{ab}\), and \emph{twist} \(\omega_{ab}\) of the congruence by

\begin{equation}
  \theta=B^{ab}h_{ab} ,
  \tag{9.2.6}\label{eq:9.2.6}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{ab}=B_{(ab)}-\frac13\theta h_{ab} ,
  \tag{9.2.7}\label{eq:9.2.7}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \omega_{ab}=B_{[ab]} .
  \tag{9.2.8}\label{eq:9.2.8}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(B_{ab}\) is decomposed as

\begin{equation}
  B_{ab}=\frac13\theta h_{ab}+\sigma_{ab}+\omega_{ab} .
  \tag{9.2.9}\label{eq:9.2.9}
\end{equation}

Note that by virtue of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.2} and Frobenius's theorem, the congruence is
(locally) hypersurface orthogonal if and only if \(\omega_{ab}=0\) (see appendix~B).

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.2}, we see that \(\sigma_{ab}\) and \(\omega_{ab}\) are purely spatial,
i.e., \(\sigma_{ab}\xi^b=\omega_{ab}\xi^b=0\). From Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.4}, it follows that
along any geodesic in the congruence, \(\theta\) indeed measures the average expansion
of the infinitesimally nearby surrounding geodesics; \(\omega_{ab}\), being the
antisymmetric part of the linear map \(B_{ab}\), measures their rotation; and
\(\sigma_{ab}\) measures their shear, i.e., an initial sphere in the tangent space which is
Lie transported along \(\xi^a\) will distort toward an ellipsoid with principal axes given
by the eigenvectors of \(\sigma^a{}_b\), with rate given by the eigenvalues of
\(\sigma^a{}_b\).

It is clear that the geodesic deviation equation (3.3.18) will yield equations for the
rate of change of \(\theta\), \(\sigma_{ab}\), and \(\omega_{ab}\) along each geodesic in the
congruence. However, it is just as easy to derive these equations from scratch. We
have

\begin{align}
  \xi^c\nabla_cB_{ab}
    &=\xi^c\nabla_c\nabla_b\xi_a
      =\xi^c\nabla_b\nabla_c\xi_a+R_{cba}{}^d\xi^c\xi_d \notag\\
    &=\nabla_b(\xi^c\nabla_c\xi_a)
      -(\nabla_b\xi^c)(\nabla_c\xi_a)
      +R_{cba}{}^d\xi^c\xi_d \notag\\
    &=-B^c{}_bB_{ac}+R_{cba}{}^d\xi^c\xi_d .
  \tag{9.2.10}\label{eq:9.2.10}
\end{align}

Taking the trace of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.10}, we obtain

\begin{equation}
  \xi^c\nabla_c\theta
  =\frac{\dd\theta}{\dd\tau}
  =-\frac13\theta^2-\sigma_{ab}\sigma^{ab}
   +\omega_{ab}\omega^{ab}-R_{cd}\xi^c\xi^d .
  \tag{9.2.11}\label{eq:9.2.11}
\end{equation}

The trace-free, symmetric part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.10} yields

\begin{align}
  \xi^c\nabla_c\sigma_{ab}
    ={}&-\frac23\theta\sigma_{ab}-\sigma_{ac}\sigma^c{}_b
        -\omega_{ac}\omega^c{}_b
        +\frac13h_{ab}(\sigma_{cd}\sigma^{cd}-\omega_{cd}\omega^{cd})\notag\\
      &+C_{cbad}\xi^c\xi^d+\frac12\widetilde R_{ab} ,
  \tag{9.2.12}\label{eq:9.2.12}
\end{align}

where \(\widetilde R_{ab}\) is the spatial, trace-free part of \(R_{ab}\), i.e.,

\begin{equation}
  \widetilde R_{ab}
  =h_{ac}h_{bd}R^{cd}-\frac13h_{ab}h_{cd}R^{cd} .
  \tag{9.2.13}\label{eq:9.2.13}
\end{equation}

Finally, the antisymmetric part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.10} yields,

\begin{equation}
  \xi^c\nabla_c\omega_{ab}
  =-\frac23\theta\omega_{ab}-2\sigma^c{}_{[b}\omega_{a]c} .
  \tag{9.2.14}\label{eq:9.2.14}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:9.2.11} is known as Raychaudhuri's equation and is the key equation
used in the proof of the singularity theorems. We turn our attention, now, to
investigate the positivity property of the last term on the right-hand side of this
equation. Using Einstein's equation, we may write this term as

\begin{equation}
  R_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b
  =8\pi\left[T_{ab}-\frac12Tg_{ab}\right]\xi^a\xi^b
  =8\pi\left[T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b+\frac12T\right] .
  \tag{9.2.15}\label{eq:9.2.15}
\end{equation}

Now, \(T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b\) physically represents the energy density of matter as
measured by an observer whose 4-velocity is \(\xi^a\). It is generally believed that
for all physically reasonable classical matter this energy density is nonnegative, i.e.,

\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b\geq0
  \tag{9.2.16}\label{eq:9.2.16}
\end{equation}

for all timelike \(\xi^a\). This assumption is known as the \emph{weak energy condition}.
However, it also seems physically reasonable that the stresses of matter will not
become so large and negative as to make the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.15}
negative. This assumption that

\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b\geq-\frac12T
  \tag{9.2.17}\label{eq:9.2.17}
\end{equation}

for all unit timelike \(\xi^a\) is known as the \emph{strong energy condition}. For
completeness, we also mention another energy condition which is believed to hold
for physically reasonable matter: For all future directed, timelike \(\xi^a\),
\(-T^a{}_b\xi^b\) should be a future directed timelike or null vector. Since for an
observer with 4-velocity \(\xi^a\) the quantity \(-T^a{}_b\xi^b\) physically represents
the energy-momentum 4-current density of matter as seen by him, this condition,
known as the \emph{dominant energy condition}, can be interpreted as saying that the
speed of energy flow of matter is always less than the speed of light. Indeed, this
interpretation can be made precise: One can prove that if \(T_{ab}\) is conserved
(i.e., \(\nabla^aT_{ab}=0\)), satisfies the dominant energy condition, and vanishes
on a closed, achronal set \(S\), then it also vanishes in \(D(S)\) (see lemma~4.3.1 of
Hawking and Ellis 1973). Note that the dominant energy condition implies the weak
energy condition, but otherwise the above three energy conditions are mathematically
independent assumptions. In particular, the strong energy condition does not imply
the weak energy condition.\footnote{In some references, the terminology ``weak energy condition'' is defined to mean
\(T_{ab}k^ak^b\geq0\) for all null \(k^a\). With this definition, the strong energy condition \emph{does}
imply the weak energy condition.}
It is ``stronger'' only in the sense that it appears to be a stronger physical
requirement to assume Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.17} rather than Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.16}.

The stress tensor \(T_{ab}\) is symmetric in its two indices, but since \(g_{ab}\) is not
positive definite, the linear map \(T^a{}_b\) from vectors into vectors need not be
diagonalizable, i.e., it need not have four linearly independent eigenvectors. However,
with the exception of a stress-tensor representing a null fluid (specifically, \(T_{ab}\)
of the form \(T_{ab}=\rho l_al_b+p_1x_ax_b+p_2y_ay_b\) where \(l^a\) is null and \(x^a\)
and \(y^a\) are orthonormal spacelike vectors orthogonal to \(l^a\)), all stress-tensors
representing what is believed to be physically reasonable matter are diagonalizable.
The eigenvectors of \(T^a{}_b\) with different eigenvalues are automatically
orthogonal, while eigenvectors with the same eigenvalue can be chosen to be
orthogonal, so in the diagonal case, \(T_{ab}\) takes the form

\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}=\rho t_at_b+p_1x_ax_b+p_2y_ay_b+p_3z_az_b ,
  \tag{9.2.18}\label{eq:9.2.18}
\end{equation}

where \(\{t^a,x^a,y^a,z^a\}\) is an orthonormal basis, with \(t^a\) timelike. The
eigenvalue \(\rho\) may be interpreted as the rest energy density of the matter, while
the eigenvalues \(p_1\), \(p_2\), \(p_3\) are called the \emph{principal pressures}. For
\(T_{ab}\) of the form Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.18}, the above energy conditions are equivalent
to the following conditions. The weak energy condition will be satisfied if and only if

\begin{equation}
  \rho\geq0
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \rho+p_i\geq0
  \quad (i=1,2,3) .
  \tag{9.2.19}\label{eq:9.2.19}
\end{equation}

The strong energy condition is equivalent to

\begin{equation}
  \rho+\sum_{i=1}^3p_i\geq0
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \rho+p_i\geq0
  \quad (i=1,2,3) .
  \tag{9.2.20}\label{eq:9.2.20}
\end{equation}

Thus, the weak and strong energy conditions will be satisfied provided only that
\(\rho\geq0\) and there do not exist negative pressures (i.e., tensions) comparable in
magnitude or larger than \(\rho\). Finally, the dominant energy condition is equivalent to

\begin{equation}
  \rho\geq\lvert p_i\rvert
  \quad (i=1,2,3) .
  \tag{9.2.21}\label{eq:9.2.21}
\end{equation}

Let us return now to Raychaudhuri's equation~\eqref{eq:9.2.11}. As discussed above,
if Einstein's equation holds, and the strong energy condition is satisfied by \(T_{ab}\),
then the last term of the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.11} will be negative.
This can be interpreted, physically, as a manifestation of the attractiveness of
gravity. If the congruence is hypersurface orthogonal, we have \(\omega_{ab}=0\), so
the third term vanishes. The second term, \(-\sigma_{ab}\sigma^{ab}\), is manifestly
nonpositive. Thus, under these assumptions we find

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\theta}{\dd\tau}+\frac13\theta^2\leq0 ,
  \tag{9.2.22}\label{eq:9.2.22}
\end{equation}

which implies

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}(\theta^{-1})\geq\frac13
  \tag{9.2.23}\label{eq:9.2.23}
\end{equation}

and hence

\begin{equation}
  \theta^{-1}(\tau)\geq\theta_0^{-1}+\frac13\tau ,
  \tag{9.2.24}\label{eq:9.2.24}
\end{equation}

where \(\theta_0\) is the initial value of \(\theta\). Suppose, now, that \(\theta_0\)
is negative, i.e., the congruence is initially converging. Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.24}
implies that \(\theta^{-1}\) must pass through zero, i.e., \(\theta\to-\infty\), within a
proper time \(\tau\leq3/\lvert\theta_0\rvert\). Thus, we have proven the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:9.2.1}
Let \(\xi^a\) be the tangent field of a hypersurface orthogonal timelike geodesic
congruence. Suppose \(R_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b\geq0\), as will be the case if Einstein's
equation holds in the spacetime and the strong energy condition is satisfied by
the matter. If the expansion \(\theta\) takes the negative value \(\theta_0\) at any
point on a geodesic in the congruence, then \(\theta\) goes to \(-\infty\) along that
geodesic within proper time \(\tau\leq3/\lvert\theta_0\rvert\).
\end{lemma}

In general, the singularity in \(\theta\) implied by Lemma~\ref{lem:9.2.1}, of course,
represents merely a singularity in the congruence, not a singularity in the structure
of spacetime. It simply states that caustics will develop in a congruence if convergence
occurs anywhere. The conclusions of Lemma~\ref{lem:9.2.1} hold for congruences in
Minkowski spacetime and in many other singularity free spacetimes. However, in the
next three sections we shall show that in certain spacetimes, the conclusions of
Lemma~\ref{lem:9.2.1} in conjunction with properties derived from global arguments
prove the existence of spacetime singularities.


We turn our attention, now, to null geodesic congruences. Again, we parameterize
the geodesics by an affine parameter \(\lambda\), but, unlike the timelike case, we now
have no natural way of normalizing the tangent field \(k^a\) and thereby adjusting the
scale of \(\lambda\) on different geodesics. In the timelike case, we restricted
consideration to deviation vectors \(\eta^a\) orthogonal to \(\xi^a\). There actually were
two independent (though related) reasons for doing so. (1) We have
\(\xi^a\nabla_a(\xi_b\eta^b)=\xi^a\xi_b\nabla_a\eta^b
=\xi_b\mathcal L_\xi\eta^b+\eta^a\xi_b\nabla_a\xi^b=0\)
provided \(\xi_a\xi^a\) is normalized to be constant. Thus, \(\xi_a\eta^a\) is constant
along each geodesic, and the behavior of the ``nonorthogonal'' part of \(\eta^a\) is
uninteresting. (2) Deviation vectors which differ only by a multiple of \(\xi^a\)
represent a displacement to the same nearby geodesic. Orthogonality fixes a natural
``gauge condition'' on \(\eta^a\).

In the case of a null geodesic congruence, the above reasons for restricting the
choice of deviation vector still apply, but now they lead to two independent
restrictions. First, for any deviation vector \(\eta^a\), we again have
\(k^a\nabla_a(k_b\eta^b)=k_b\mathcal L_k\eta^b+\eta^ak_b\nabla_ak^b=0\), so
\(k_b\eta^b\) does not vary along each geodesic. This implies that an arbitrary
deviation vector \(\eta^a\) may be written as the sum of a vector not orthogonal to
\(k^a\) which is parallelly propagated along the geodesic, plus a vector perpendicular
to \(k^a\). (Note, however, that there is no natural, unique way of decomposing
\(\eta^a\) in this manner.) Thus, the behavior of the ``nonorthogonal'' part of \(\eta^a\)
again is uninteresting, and we may restrict consideration to deviation vectors
satisfying \(\eta^ak_a=0\). Second, deviation vectors which differ only by a multiple
of \(k^a\) again represent a displacement to the same nearby geodesic. Thus, the
physically interesting quantity is really the equivalence class of deviation vectors,
where two deviation vectors are considered equivalent if their difference is a multiple
of \(k^a\). Since \(k^a\) is null and thus is orthogonal to itself, this second restriction
is independent of the first restriction, and it reduces the physically interesting class
of deviation vectors to a two-dimensional subspace, as we now shall describe with
more precision.

Let \(T_pM\) denote the tangent space at a point \(p\in M\). The tangent vectors in
\(T_pM\) which are orthogonal to \(k^a\) form a three-dimensional subspace, which
we shall denote by \(\widetilde{T_pM}\). We define \(\widehat{T_pM}\) to be the vector
space of equivalence classes of vectors in \(\widetilde{T_pM}\), where
\(x^a,y^a\in\widetilde{T_pM}\) are defined to be equivalent if there exists a number
\(c\in\mathbb R\) such that \(x^a-y^a=ck^a\). Then \(\widehat{T_pM}\) is a
two-dimensional vector space, although it cannot be identified as a subspace of
\(T_pM\) in any natural way. As explained above, the deviation vectors of interest
live in the vector space \(\widehat{T_pM}\).

A tangent vector \(t^a\in T_pM\) does not naturally give rise to a tangent vector in
\(\widehat{T_pM}\), because there is no natural way of decomposing it as a sum of a
vector in \(\widetilde{T_pM}\) and a vector not lying in \(\widetilde{T_pM}\). However,
if \(t^a\in\widetilde{T_pM}\) (i.e., if \(t^ak_a=0\)), then \(t^a\) naturally gives rise to a
vector \(\widehat t^a\) in \(\widehat{T_pM}\) by taking its equivalence class. On the
other hand, a covector \(\mu_a\in T_p^\vee M\) naturally gives rise to a covector
\(\widetilde\mu_a\in(\widetilde{T_pM})^\vee\) by restricting its action to vectors in
\(\widetilde{T_pM}\). However, \(\widetilde\mu_a\) naturally gives rise to a covector
\(\widehat\mu_a\in(\widehat{T_pM})^\vee\) if and only if
\(\widetilde\mu_ak^a=\mu_ak^a=0\). More generally, a tensor
\(T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) over \(T_pM\) naturally gives rise to a tensor
\(\widehat T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) over \(\widehat{T_pM}\) if and only if
the result of contracting any one of its indices with \(k_a\) or \(k^a\) and contracting
its remaining indices with vectors or covectors which give rise to elements of
\(\widehat{T_pM}\) or \((\widehat{T_pM})^\vee\) is always zero. For tensors satisfying
this property, the operation of taking outer products commutes with the
``projection'' into tensors over \(\widehat{T_pM}\). Furthermore, for a tensor satisfying
the stronger property that contraction of any one of its indices into \(k^a\) or \(k_a\)
gives zero, then projection into \(\widehat{T_pM}\) commutes with contraction.

The spacetime metric \(g_{ab}\) satisfies the above property and thus gives rise to a
tensor over \(\widehat{T_pM}\), which we shall denote as \(\widehat h_{ab}\). It is not
difficult to see that \(\widehat h_{ab}\) is a positive definite (i.e., signature \(++\))
metric on \(\widehat{T_pM}\). (On the other hand, the tensor \(\widetilde h_{ab}\) over
\(\widetilde{T_pM}\) obtained by restricting the action of \(g_{ab}\) to vectors in
\(\widetilde{T_pM}\) is degenerate, since \(\widetilde h_{ab}k^b=0\), so
\(\widetilde h_{ab}\) is not a metric on \(\widetilde{T_pM}\).) Note that the inverse
metric \(\widehat h^{ab}\) is just the ``hatted'' tensor associated with \(g^{ab}\).

Consider, now, a congruence of null geodesics with tangent field \(k^a\). The tensor
field

\begin{equation}
  B_{ab}=\nabla_bk_a
  \tag{9.2.25}\label{eq:9.2.25}
\end{equation}

also satisfies the above property, and thus gives rise to a ``hatted'' tensor field
\(\widehat B_{ab}\). We can decompose \(\widehat B_{ab}\) as

\begin{equation}
  \widehat B_{ab}
  =\frac12\theta\widehat h_{ab}+\widehat\sigma_{ab}+\widehat\omega_{ab}
  \tag{9.2.26}\label{eq:9.2.26}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
  \theta=\widehat h^{ab}\widehat B_{ab} ,
  \tag{9.2.27}\label{eq:9.2.27}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \widehat\sigma_{ab}
  =\widehat B_{(ab)}-\frac12\theta\widehat h_{ab} ,
  \tag{9.2.28}\label{eq:9.2.28}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \widehat\omega_{ab}=\widehat B_{[ab]} ,
  \tag{9.2.29}\label{eq:9.2.29}
\end{equation}

so that \(\theta\), \(\widehat\sigma_{ab}\), and \(\widehat\omega_{ab}\) again have the
physical interpretation as, respectively, the expansion, shear, and twist of the
congruence. The change in the numerical factor in the term
\(\frac12\theta\widehat h_{ab}\) (as compared with \(\frac13\theta h_{ab}\) in the
timelike case) arises simply because the relevant vector space is now two-dimensional
rather than three-dimensional.

The same derivation as led to Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.10} in the timelike case now yields

\begin{equation}
  k^c\nabla_cB_{ab}+B^c{}_bB_{ac}=R_{cba}{}^dk_dk^c .
  \tag{9.2.30}\label{eq:9.2.30}
\end{equation}

``Hatting'' this equation, we obtain

\begin{equation}
  k^c\nabla_c\widehat B_{ab}
  +\widehat B^c{}_b\widehat B_{ac}
  =\widehat R_{cbad}k^ck^d .
  \tag{9.2.31}\label{eq:9.2.31}
\end{equation}

Finally, substituting our decomposition~\eqref{eq:9.2.26} for \(\widehat B_{ab}\) and
taking the trace, symmetric trace-free, and antisymmetric parts of the equation, we
obtain, respectively,

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\theta}{\dd\lambda}
  =-\frac12\theta^2-\widehat\sigma_{ab}\widehat\sigma^{ab}
   +\widehat\omega_{ab}\widehat\omega^{ab}-R_{cd}k^ck^d ,
  \tag{9.2.32}\label{eq:9.2.32}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  k^c\nabla_c\widehat\sigma_{ab}
  =-\theta\widehat\sigma_{ab}+\widehat C_{cbad}k^ck^d ,
  \tag{9.2.33}\label{eq:9.2.33}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  k^c\nabla_c\widehat\omega_{ab}
  =-\theta\widehat\omega_{ab} .
  \tag{9.2.34}\label{eq:9.2.34}
\end{equation}

Notice that the last term on the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.33} vanishes if
and only if \(C_{cbad}k^ck^dx^ay^b=0\) for all \(x^a\), \(y^b\) orthogonal to \(k^a\).
This is equivalent to \(k_{[e}C_{b]cd[a}k_{f]}k^ck^d=0\), which is just the condition
that \(k^a\) be a principal null vector of the Weyl tensor\footnote{Thus, it follows immediately from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.33} that if the shear, \(\widehat\sigma_{ab}\), of a null geodesic congruence
vanishes, then \(k^a\) is a principal null vector. In the case of a vacuum spacetime,
\(R_{ab}=0\), considerably stronger results hold. If \(\widehat\sigma_{ab}=0\), then
\(k^a\) must be a \emph{repeated} principal null vector. Conversely, if \(k^a\) is a repeated
principal null vector in a vacuum, algebraically special spacetime, then it is tangent to
a shear-free, null geodesic congruence. These results are known as the Goldberg--Sachs
theorem, and a proof can be found in Newman and Penrose (1962).}
(see section~7.3).

The nature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.32} for the expansion of null geodesics is very similar
to that of Raychaudhuri's equation~\eqref{eq:9.2.11}. The only significant change is
that, using Einstein's equation, we now obtain

\begin{equation}
  R_{ab}k^ak^b=8\pi T_{ab}k^ak^b .
  \tag{9.2.35}\label{eq:9.2.35}
\end{equation}

Thus, all that is needed to ensure that the last term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.32} is
nonpositive is that for all null \(k^a\),

\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}k^ak^b\geq0 .
  \tag{9.2.36}\label{eq:9.2.36}
\end{equation}

If the strong energy condition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.17}, holds, then for all timelike
\(\xi^a\) we have \(T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b-\frac12T\xi^a\xi_a\geq0\), and by continuity
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.36} will hold for all null \(k^a\). Similarly, if the weak energy
condition~\eqref{eq:9.2.16} holds, then by continuity Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.36} also will
be satisfied. For a diagonalizable \(T_{ab}\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.18}, the necessary and
sufficient requirement for satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.36} for all null \(k^a\) is

\begin{equation}
  \rho+p_i\geq0
  \quad (i=1,2,3) .
  \tag{9.2.37}\label{eq:9.2.37}
\end{equation}

Thus, the requirements on \(T_{ab}\) needed to ensure that the last term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.2.32} is nonpositive are weaker than the corresponding requirements
in the timelike case. By the same arguments as led to Lemma~\ref{lem:9.2.1} we obtain
the following result.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:9.2.2}
Let \(k^a\) be the tangent field of a hypersurface orthogonal null geodesic
congruence. Suppose \(R_{ab}k^ak^b\geq0\), as will be the case if Einstein's equation
holds in the spacetime and the weak or strong energy condition is satisfied by the
matter. If the expansion \(\theta\) takes the negative value \(\theta_0\) at any point
on a geodesic in the congruence, then \(\theta\) goes to \(-\infty\) along that
geodesic within affine length \(\lambda\leq2/\lvert\theta_0\rvert\).
\end{lemma}
