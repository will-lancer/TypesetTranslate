\noindent\textbf{Theorem A.} \textit{The integer $\deg(f; y)$ does not depend on the choice of regular value $y$.}

It will be called the \emph{degree} of $f$ (denoted $\deg f$).

\noindent\textbf{Theorem B.} \textit{If $f$ is smoothly homotopic to $g$, then $\deg f = \deg g$.}

The proof will be essentially the same as that in §4. It is only necessary to keep careful control of orientations.

First consider the following situation: Suppose that $M$ is the boundary of a compact oriented manifold $X$ and that $M$ is oriented as the boundary of $X$.

\noindent\textbf{Lemma 1.} \textit{If $f : M \to N$ extends to a smooth map $F : X \to N$, then $\deg(f; y) = 0$ for every regular value $y$.}

\noindent\textit{Proof.}
First suppose that $y$ is a regular value for $F$, as well as for $f = F \mid M$. The compact $1$-manifold $F^{-1}(y)$ is a finite union of arcs and circles, with only the boundary points of the arcs lying on $M = \partial X$. Let $A \subset F^{-1}(y)$ be one of these arcs, with $\partial A = \{a\} \cup \{b\}$. We will show that
\[
\operatorname{sign} df_a + \operatorname{sign} df_b = 0,
\]
and hence (summing over all such arcs) that $\deg(f; y) = 0$.

The orientations for $X$ and $N$ determine an orientation for $A$ as follows: Given $x \in A$, let $(v_1, \ldots, v_{n+1})$ be a positively oriented basis for $TX_x$ with $v_1$ tangent to $A$. Then $v_1$ determines the required orientation for $TA_x$ if and only if $dF_x$ carries $(v_2, \ldots, v_{n+1})$ into a positively oriented basis for $TN_y$.

Let $v_1(x)$ denote the positively oriented unit vector tangent to $A$ at $x$. Clearly $v_1$ is a smooth function, and $v_1(x)$ points outward at one boundary point (say $b$) and inward at the other boundary point $a$.


% PDF page 41

It follows immediately that
\[
\operatorname{sign} df_a = -1, \qquad \operatorname{sign} df_b = +1;
\]
with sum zero. Adding up over all such arcs $A$, we have proved that $\deg(f; y) = 0$.

More generally, suppose that $y_0$ is a regular value for $f$, but not for $F$. The function $\deg(f; y)$ is constant within some neighborhood $U$ of $y_0$. Hence, as in §4, we can choose a regular value $y$ for $F$ within $U$ and observe that
\[
\deg(f; y_0) = \deg(f; y) = 0.
\]
This proves Lemma 1.

Now consider a smooth homotopy
\[
F : [0, 1] \times M \to N
\]
between two mappings
\[
f(x) = F(0, x), \qquad g(x) = F(1, x).
\]

\noindent\textbf{Lemma 2.} \textit{The degree $\deg(g; y)$ is equal to $\deg(f; y)$ for any common regular value $y$.}

\noindent\textit{Proof.}
The manifold $[0, 1] \times M^n$ can be oriented as a product, and will then have boundary consisting of $1 \times M^n$ (with the correct orientation) and $0 \times M^n$ (with the wrong orientation). Thus the degree of $F \mid \partial([0, 1] \times M^n)$ at a regular value $y$ is equal to the difference
\[
\deg(g; y) - \deg(f; y).
\]
According to Lemma 1 this difference must be zero.

The remainder of the proof of Theorems A and B is completely analogous to the argument in §4. If $y$ and $z$ are both regular values for $f : M \to N$, choose a diffeomorphism $h : N \to N$ that carries $y$ to $z$ and is isotopic to the identity. Then $h$ will preserve orientation, and
\[
\deg(f; y) = \deg(h \circ f; h(y))
\]
by inspection. But $f$ is homotopic to $h \circ f$; hence
\[
\deg(h \circ f; z) = \deg(f; z)
\]
by Lemma 2. Therefore $\deg(f; y) = \deg(f; z)$, which completes the proof.

\noindent\textbf{Examples.} The complex function $z \mapsto z^k$, $z \neq 0$, maps the unit circle onto itself with degree $k$. (Here $k$ may be positive, negative, or zero.) The degenerate mapping
\[
f : M \longrightarrow \text{constant} \in N
\]


% PDF page 42

has degree zero. A diffeomorphism $f : M \to N$ has degree $+1$ or $-1$ according as $f$ preserves or reverses orientation. Thus \emph{an orientation reversing diffeomorphism of a compact boundaryless manifold is not smoothly homotopic to the identity.}

One example of an orientation reversing diffeomorphism is provided by the reflection $r_i : S^n \to S^n$, where
\[
r_i(x_1, \ldots, x_{n+1}) = (x_1, \ldots, -x_i, \ldots, x_{n+1}).
\]

The antipodal map of $S^n$ has degree $(-1)^{n+1}$, as we can see by noting that the antipodal map is the composition of $n+1$ reflections:
\[
-x = r_1 \circ r_2 \circ \cdots \circ r_{n+1}(x).
\]

\emph{Thus if $n$ is even, the antipodal map of $S^n$ is not smoothly homotopic to the identity,} a fact not detected by the degree modulo 2.

As an application, following Brouwer, we show that $S^n$ \emph{admits a smooth field of nonzero tangent vectors if and only if $n$ is odd.} (Compare Figures~\ref{fig:milnor-10} and~\ref{fig:milnor-11}.)

\textsc{Definition.} A smooth \emph{tangent vector field} on $M \subset \mathbb{R}^k$ is a smooth map $v : M \to \mathbb{R}^k$ such that $v(x) \in TM_x$ for each $x \in M$. In the case of the sphere $S^n \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ this is clearly equivalent to the condition
\[
\text{1)} \quad v(x) \cdot x = 0 \quad \text{for all} \quad x \in S^n,
\]
using the euclidean inner product. A tangent vector field is called \emph{nonzero} if $v(x) \neq 0$ for every $x \in M$.
