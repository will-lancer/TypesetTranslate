\textsc{Definition.} The vector field $v$ is \emph{nondegenerate} at $z$ if the linear transformation $\dd v_z$ is nonsingular.

It follows that $z$ is an isolated zero.

\noindent\textbf{Lemma 4.} \textit{The index of $v$ at a nondegenerate zero $z$ is either $+1$ or $-1$ according as the determinant of $\dd v_z$ is positive or negative.}

\noindent\textit{Proof.}
Think of $v$ as a diffeomorphism from some convex neighborhood $U_0$ of $z$ into $\mathbb{R}^m$. We may assume that $z = 0$. If $v$ preserves orientation, we have seen that $v \mid U_0$ can be deformed smoothly into the identity without introducing any new zeros. (See Lemmas 1, 2.) Hence the index is certainly equal to $+1$.

If $v$ reverses orientation, then similarly $v$ can be deformed into a reflection; hence $\iota = -1$.

More generally consider a zero $z$ of a vector field $w$ on a manifold $M \subset \mathbb{R}^k$. Think of $w$ as a map from $M$ to $\mathbb{R}^k$ so that the derivative $\dd w_z : TM_z \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is defined.

\noindent\textbf{Lemma 5.} \textit{The derivative $\dd w_z$ actually carries $TM_z$ into the subspace $TM_z \subset \mathbb{R}^k$, and hence can be considered as a linear transformation from $TM_z$ to itself. If this linear transformation has determinant $D \neq 0$ then $z$ is an isolated zero of $w$ with index equal to $+1$ or $-1$ according as $D$ is positive or negative.}

\noindent\textit{Proof.}
Let $h : U \to M$ be a parametrization of some neighborhood of $z$. Let $e^i$ denote the $i$-th basis vector of $\mathbb{R}^m$ and let
\[
t^i = \dd h_u(e^i) = \frac{\partial h}{\partial u_i}
\]
so that the vectors $t^1, \ldots, t^m$ form a basis for the tangent space $TM_{h(u)}$. We must compute the image of $t^i = t^i(u)$ under the linear transformation $\dd w_{h(u)}$. First note that
\[
\text{1)} \quad \dd w_{h(u)}(t^i) = \dd(w \circ h)_u(e^i) = \frac{\partial w(h(u))}{\partial u_i}.
\]
Let $v = \sum v_i e^i$ be the vector field on $U$ which corresponds to the vector field $w$ on $M$. By definition $v = \dd h^{-1} \circ w \circ h$, so that
\[
w(h(u)) = \dd h_u(v) = \sum v_i t^i.
\]
Therefore
\[
\text{2)} \quad \frac{\partial w(h(u))}{\partial u_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial u_i} t^j + \sum_j v_j \frac{\partial t^j}{\partial u_i}.
\]


% PDF page 50

Combining 1) and 2), and then evaluating at the zero $h^{-1}(z)$ of $v$, we obtain the formula
\[
\text{3)} \quad \dd w_z(t^i) = \sum_j \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial u_i} t^j.
\]
Thus $\dd w_z$ maps $TM_z$ into itself, and the determinant $D$ of this linear transformation $TM_z \to TM_z$ is equal to the determinant of the matrix $(\partial v_j / \partial u_i)$. Together with Lemma 4 this completes the proof.

Now consider a compact, boundaryless manifold $M \subset \mathbb{R}^k$. Let $N_\epsilon$ denote the closed $\epsilon$-neighborhood of $M$ (i.e., the set of all $x \in \mathbb{R}^k$ with $\|x - y\| \leq \epsilon$ for some $y \in M$). For $\epsilon$ sufficiently small one can show that $N_\epsilon$ is a smooth manifold with boundary. (See Problem~11 at the end of this section.)

\noindent\textbf{Theorem 1.} \textit{For any vector field $v$ on $M$ with only nondegenerate zeros, the index sum $\sum \iota$ is equal to the degree of the Gauss mapping}\footnote{A different interpretation of this degree has been given by Allendoerfer and Fenchel: the degree of $g$ can be expressed as the integral over $M$ of a suitable curvature scalar, thus yielding an $m$-dimensional version of the classical Gauss--Bonnet theorem. (References [1], [9]. See also Chern [6].)}
\[
g : \partial N_\epsilon \to S^{k-1}.
\]
\textit{In particular this sum does not depend on the choice of vector field.}

\noindent\textit{Proof.}
For $x \in N_\epsilon$, let $r(x) \in M$ denote the closest point of $M$. (Compare Problem~12 at the end of this section.) Note that the vector $x - r(x)$ is perpendicular to the tangent space of $M$ at $r(x)$, for otherwise $r(x)$ would not be the closest point of $M$. If $\epsilon$ is sufficiently small, then the function $r(x)$ is smooth and well defined.

We will also consider the squared distance function
\[
\phi(x) = \|x - r(x)\|^2.
\]
An easy computation shows that the gradient of $\phi$ is given by
\[
\operatorname{grad} \phi = 2(x - r(x)).
\]
Hence, for each point $x$ of the level surface $\partial N_\epsilon = \phi^{-1}(\epsilon^2)$, the outward unit normal vector is given by
\[
g(x) = \operatorname{grad} \phi / \|\operatorname{grad} \phi\| = (x - r(x))/\epsilon.
\]
Extend $v$ to a vector field $w$ on the neighborhood $N_\epsilon$ by setting
\[
w(x) = (x - r(x)) + v(r(x)).
\]
Then $w$ points outward along the boundary, since the inner product $w(x) \cdot g(x)$ is equal to $\epsilon > 0$. Note that $w$ can vanish only at the zeros of $v$ in $M$; this is clear since the two summands $(x - r(x))$ and $v(r(x))$ are mutually orthogonal. Computing the derivative of $w$ at a zero $z \in M$, we see that
\[
\dd w_z(h) = \dd v_z(h) \quad \text{for all } h \in TM_z,
\]
\[
\dd w_z(h) = h \quad \text{for } h \in TM_z^{\perp}.
\]
Thus the determinant of $\dd w_z$ is equal to the determinant of $\dd v_z$. Hence the index of $w$ at the zero $z$ is equal to the index $\iota$ of $v$ at $z$. Now according to Lemma 3 the index sum $\sum \iota$ is equal to the degree of $g$. This proves Theorem 1.

\noindent\textbf{Examples.} On the sphere $S^m$ there exists a vector field $v$ which points ``north'' at every point.\footnote{For example, $v$ can be defined by the formula $v(x) = p - (p \cdot x)x$, where $p$ is the north pole. (See Figure~\ref{fig:milnor-11}.)} At the south pole the vectors radiate outward; hence the index is $+1$. At the north pole the vectors converge inward; hence the index is $(-1)^m$. Thus the invariant $\sum \iota$ is equal to $0$ or $2$ according as $m$ is odd or even. This gives a new proof that every vector field on an even sphere has a zero.

For any odd-dimensional, boundaryless manifold the invariant $\sum \iota$ is zero. For if the vector field $v$ is replaced by $-v$, then each index is multiplied by $(-1)^m$, and the equality
\[
\sum \iota = (-1)^m \sum \iota,
\]
for $m$ odd, implies that $\sum \iota = 0$.
