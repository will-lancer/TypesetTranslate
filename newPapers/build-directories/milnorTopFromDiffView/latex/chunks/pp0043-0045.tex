% PDF page 43

If $v(x)$ is nonzero for all $x$, then we may as well suppose that
\[
\text{2)} \quad v(x) \cdot v(x) = 1 \quad \text{for all} \quad x \in S^n.
\]
For in any case $\bar{v}(x) = v(x) / \|v(x)\|$ would be a vector field which does satisfy this condition. Thus we can think of $v$ as a smooth function from $S^n$ to itself.

Now define a smooth homotopy
\[
F : S^n \times [0, \pi] \to S^n
\]
by the formula $F(x, \theta) = x \cos \theta + v(x) \sin \theta$. Computation shows that
\[
F(x, \theta) \cdot F(x, \theta) = 1
\]
and that
\[
F(x, 0) = x, \qquad F(x, \pi) = -x.
\]
Thus the antipodal map of $S^n$ is homotopic to the identity. But for $n$ even we have seen that this is impossible.

On the other hand, if $n = 2k - 1$, the explicit formula
\[
v(x_1, \ldots, x_{2k}) = (x_2, -x_1, x_4, -x_3, \ldots, x_{2k}, -x_{2k-1})
\]
defines a nonzero tangent vector field on $S^n$. This completes the proof.

It follows, incidentally, that the antipodal map of $S^n$ is homotopic to the identity for $n$ odd. A famous theorem due to Heinz Hopf asserts that two mappings from a connected $n$-manifold to the $n$-sphere are smoothly homotopic \emph{if and only if} they have the same degree. In §7 we will prove a more general result which implies Hopf's theorem.

\paragraph{Problem 1.}
Show that the degree of a composition $g \circ f$ is equal to the product $(\deg g)(\deg f)$.

\paragraph{Problem 2.}
Show that every complex polynomial of degree $n$ gives rise to a smooth map from the Gauss sphere $S^2$ to itself of degree $n$.

Show that any map $S^n \to S^n$ with degree different from $(-1)^{n+1}$ must have a fixed point.

\paragraph{Problem 7.}
Show that any map $S^n \to S^n$ of odd degree must carry some pair of antipodal points into a pair of antipodal points.

\paragraph{Problem 13.}
Given disjoint manifolds $M, N \subset \mathbb{R}^{k+1}$, the linking map
\[
\lambda : M \times N \to S^k
\]
is defined by $\lambda(x, y) = (x - y) / \|x - y\|$. If $M$ and $N$ are compact, oriented, and boundaryless, with total dimension $m + n = k$, then the degree of $\lambda$ is called the \emph{linking number} $l(M, N)$. Prove that
\[
l(N, M) = (-1)^{(m+1)(n+1)} l(M, N).
\]
If $M$ bounds an oriented manifold $X$ disjoint from $N$, prove that $l(M, N) = 0$. Define the linking number for disjoint manifolds in the sphere $S^{m+n+1}$.

\paragraph{Problem 14. The Hopf invariant.}
If $y \neq z$ are regular values for a map $f : S^{2p-1} \to S^p$, then the manifolds $f^{-1}(y)$, $f^{-1}(z)$ can be oriented as above; hence the linking number $l(f^{-1}(y), f^{-1}(z))$ is defined.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Prove that this linking number is locally constant as a function of $y$.
\item[(b)] If $y$ and $z$ are regular values of $g$ also, where
\[
\|f(x) - g(x)\| < \|y - z\|
\]
for all $x$, prove that
\[
l(f^{-1}(y), f^{-1}(z)) = l(g^{-1}(y), f^{-1}(z)) = l(g^{-1}(y), g^{-1}(z)).
\]
\item[(c)] Prove that $l(f^{-1}(y), f^{-1}(z))$ depends only on the homotopy class of $f$, and does not depend on the choice of $y$ and $z$.
\end{enumerate}

This integer $H(f) = l(f^{-1}(y), f^{-1}(z))$ is called the \emph{Hopf invariant} of $f$. (Reference [15].)

\paragraph{Problem 15.}
If the dimension $p$ is odd, prove that $H(f) = 0$. For a composition
\[
S^{2p-1} \xrightarrow{f} S^p \xrightarrow{g} S^p
\]
prove that $H(g \circ f)$ is equal to $H(f)$ multiplied by the square of the degree of $g$.

The \emph{Hopf fibration} $\pi : S^3 \to S^2$ is defined by
\[
\pi(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4) = h^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x_1 + \ii x_2}{x_3 + \ii x_4}\right)
\]
where $h$ denotes stereographic projection to the complex plane. Prove that $H(\pi) = 1$.

\section{Vector Fields and the Euler Number}

As a further application of the concept of degree, we study vector fields on other manifolds.

Consider first an open set $U \subset \mathbb{R}^m$ and a smooth vector field
\[
v : U \to \mathbb{R}^m
\]
with an isolated zero at the point $z \in U$. The function
\[
\bar{v}(x) = v(x) / \|v(x)\|
\]
maps a small sphere centered at $z$ into the unit sphere.\footnote{Each sphere is to be oriented as the boundary of the corresponding disk.} The degree of this mapping is called the \emph{index} $\iota$ of $v$ at the zero $z$.

Some examples, with indices $-1$, $0$, $1$, $2$, are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:milnor-12}. (Intimately associated with $v$ are the curves ``tangent'' to $v$ which are obtained by solving the differential equations $\dd x_i/\dd t = v_i(x_1, \ldots, x_m)$. It is these curves which are actually sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:milnor-12}.)

A zero with arbitrary index can be obtained as follows: In the plane of complex numbers the polynomial $z^k$ defines a smooth vector field with a zero of index $k$ at the origin, and the function $\bar{z}^k$ defines a vector field with a zero of index $-k$.

We must prove that this concept of index is invariant under diffeomorphism of $U$. To explain what this means, let us consider the more general situation of a map $f : M \to N$, with a vector field on each manifold.

\textsc{Definition.} The vector fields $v$ on $M$ and $v'$ on $N$ \emph{correspond} under $f$ if the derivative $\dd f_x$ carries $v(x)$ into $v'(f(x))$ for each $x \in M$.
