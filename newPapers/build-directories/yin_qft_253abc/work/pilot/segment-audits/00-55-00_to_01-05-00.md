Probably in your freshman mechanics class you learned this Poincare symmetry as a transformation of spacetime points. In quantum mechanics, a symmetry is supposed to be represented by a unitary operator. Here we are doing quantum mechanics, so this Poincare symmetry should also be represented by an operator. I am using shorthand notation; the matrix indices are implicit in this expression. That is the statement that \(\hat\phi(x)\) and \(\hat\phi(x')\) are related by the symmetry. Any questions so far?

One way to study a continuous symmetry is to view a finite symmetry transformation as composed of infinitesimal transformations. It will be convenient to begin by studying the infinitesimal version of the symmetry. In fact, in this case it suffices to focus on that. Let me consider the infinitesimal version, where

$$
\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}=\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}+\omega^\mu{}_{\nu},
\qquad
a^\mu=\epsilon^\mu,

with \(\omega\) and \(\epsilon\) small, keeping only first order.

**01:02:16--01:03:15**

You can verify this for yourself. If you plug \(\Lambda=\delta+\omega\) into the metric-preserving condition and expand to first order in \(\omega\), you find that \(\omega_{\mu\nu}\), defined by lowering an index with \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\), is antisymmetric:

$$
\omega_{\mu\nu}=-\omega_{\nu\mu}.
$$

This is probably something you have seen in the context of special relativity. Anyway, for the infinitesimal Poincare transformation, the unitary operator \(U\) is very close to the identity.

**01:03:15--01:04:40**

We can expand to first order in the parameters \(\omega\) and \(\epsilon\). Since \(U\) is unitary, its first-order part is anti-Hermitian. We write

$$
U(\Lambda,a)
=1-i\epsilon^\mu\hat P_\mu
+\frac{i}{2}\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu}.
$$

There is a term proportional to \(\epsilon^\mu\), and another multiplying \(\omega_{\mu\nu}\). The factor of one half accompanies the antisymmetric pair. This is a definition. All I am saying is that, for an infinitesimal Poincare transformation, the associated symmetry operator can be expanded as the identity plus an anti-Hermitian operator. After pulling out the factor of \(i\), \(\hat P_\mu\) and \(\hat J^{\mu\nu}\) are Hermitian operators.
