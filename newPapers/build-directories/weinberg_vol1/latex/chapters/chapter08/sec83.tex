\subsection{Quantization in Coulomb Gauge}

There is still an impediment to the canonical quantization of
electrodynamics in the Coulomb gauge. Even after we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.2.9} to
eliminate $A^0$ (and $\Pi_0$) from the list of canonical variables, we cannot
apply the usual canonical commutation relations to $A^i$ and $\Pi_i$, because
there are two remaining constraints on these variables.\footnote{In this
section $i,j$, etc. run over the values $1,2,3$. We continue the practice of
taking all operators at the same time, and omitting the time argument.} One
of them is the Coulomb gauge condition
\begin{equation}
  \chi_{1\mathbf x}\equiv\partial_iA^i(\mathbf x)=0.
  \tag{8.3.1}\label{eq:8.3.1}
\end{equation}
The other is the secondary constraint Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.2.3}, which requires that
\begin{equation}
  \chi_{2\mathbf x}\equiv\partial_i\Pi^i(\mathbf x)+J^0(\mathbf x)=0.
  \tag{8.3.2}\label{eq:8.3.2}
\end{equation}
Neither constraint is consistent with the usual commutation relations
$[A^i(\mathbf x),\Pi_j(\mathbf y)]=\ii\delta^i_j\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)$,
because operating on the right-hand side with either $\partial/\partial x^i$
or $\partial/\partial y^j$ does not give zero.

These constraints are of a type known as \textit{second class}, for which
there is a universal prescription for the commutation relations, discussed
in Section 7.6. Note that the constraint functions have the Poisson brackets
\begin{align}
  C_{1\mathbf x,2\mathbf y}
  &=-C_{2\mathbf y,1\mathbf x}
  \equiv[\chi_{1\mathbf x},\chi_{2\mathbf y}]_{\mathrm P}
  =-\nabla^2\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y),
  \notag\\
  C_{1\mathbf x,1\mathbf y}
  &\equiv[\chi_{1\mathbf x},\chi_{1\mathbf y}]_{\mathrm P}=0,
  \tag{8.3.3}\label{eq:8.3.3}\\
  C_{2\mathbf x,2\mathbf y}
  &\equiv[\chi_{2\mathbf x},\chi_{2\mathbf y}]_{\mathrm P}=0,
  \notag
\end{align}
where here, for any functionals $U$ and $V$,
\[
  [U,V]_{\mathrm P}\equiv\int \dd^3x\left[
  \frac{\delta U}{\delta A^i(\mathbf x)}
  \frac{\delta V}{\delta\Pi_i(\mathbf x)}
  -\frac{\delta V}{\delta A^i(\mathbf x)}
  \frac{\delta U}{\delta\Pi_i(\mathbf x)}\right].
\]
The `matrix' $C_{N\mathbf x,M\mathbf y}$ is non-singular, which identifies
these as second class constraints. Also, the field variables $A^i$ may be
expressed in terms of independent canonical variables, which may, for
instance, be taken as $Q_{1\mathbf x}=A^1(\mathbf x)$,
$Q_{2\mathbf x}=A^2(\mathbf x)$, with $A^3$ given by the solution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.3.1}:
\[
  A^3(\mathbf x)=-\int^{x^3}\dd s\,
  [\partial_1A^1(x^1,x^2,s)+\partial_2A^2(x^1,x^2,s)].
\]
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.3.2}, the canonical conjugates $\Pi_i$ to $A^i$ may likewise be
expressed in terms of the canonical conjugates $P_{\mathbf x1}$ and
$P_{\mathbf x2}$, to $Q_{1\mathbf x}$ and $Q_{2\mathbf x}$. In such cases,
Part B of the Appendix to the previous chapter tells us that if the
independent variables $Q_{1\mathbf x}$, $Q_{2\mathbf x}$,
$P_{1\mathbf x}$, and $P_{2\mathbf x}$ satisfy the usual canonical
commutation relations, then the commutators of the constrained variables
and their canonical conjugates are given (aside from a factor $i$) by the
corresponding Dirac brackets \eqref{eq:7.6.20}. This prescription has the great
advantage that we do not have to do use explicit expressions for the dependent
variables in terms of the independent ones.

To calculate the Dirac brackets, we note that the matrix $C$ has the inverse
\begin{align}
  (C^{-1})_{1\mathbf x,2\mathbf y}
  &=-(C^{-1})_{2\mathbf y,1\mathbf x}
  =-\int\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,
  \frac{e^{\ii\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}}{\mathbf k^2}
  =-\frac{1}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rvert},
  \notag\\
  (C^{-1})_{1\mathbf x,1\mathbf y}
  &=(C^{-1})_{2\mathbf x,2\mathbf y}=0.
  \tag{8.3.4}\label{eq:8.3.4}
\end{align}
Also, the non-vanishing Poisson brackets of the $A^i$ and $\Pi_i$ with the
constraint functions are
\[
  [A^i(\mathbf x),\chi_{2\mathbf y}]_{\mathrm P}
  =-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)
\]
and
\[
  [\Pi_i(\mathbf x),\chi_{1\mathbf y}]_{\mathrm P}
  =+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y).
\]
Hence according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.6.19} and \eqref{eq:7.6.20}, the equal-time commutators are
\begin{align}
  [A^i(\mathbf x),\Pi_j(\mathbf y)]
  &=\ii\delta^i_j\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)
  +\ii\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}
  \left(\frac{1}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rvert}\right),
  \notag\\
  [A^i(\mathbf x),A^j(\mathbf y)]
  &=[\Pi_i(\mathbf x),\Pi_j(\mathbf y)]=0.
  \tag{8.3.5}\label{eq:8.3.5}
\end{align}
Note that these are consistent with the Coulomb gauge conditions \eqref{eq:8.3.1}
and \eqref{eq:8.3.2}, as is guaranteed by the general properties of the Dirac bracket.

Now, what is $\boldsymbol\Pi$ in electrodynamics? For the class of theories
discussed in the previous section where only the kinematic term
$-\tfrac14\int \dd^3x\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ in the Lagrangian depends on
$\dot{\mathbf A}$, varying the Lagrangian with respect to $\dot{\mathbf A}$
without worrying about the constraint
$\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf A}=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_i=\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot A^i(\mathbf x)}
  =\dot A^i(\mathbf x)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}A^0(\mathbf x).
  \tag{8.3.6}\label{eq:8.3.6}
\end{equation}
But with $\mathbf A$ constrained by the condition
$\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, variational derivatives with respect to
$\dot{\mathbf A}$ are not really well defined. If the variation of $L$ under a
change $\delta\dot{\mathbf A}$ in $\dot{\mathbf A}$ is
$\delta L=\int \dd^3x\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}\cdot\delta\dot{\mathbf A}$, then
since $\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\delta\dot{\mathbf A}=0$, we also have
$\delta L=\int \dd^3x\,[\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}+\boldsymbol\nabla\mathcal F]
\cdot\delta\dot{\mathbf A}$ for any scalar function $\mathcal F(\mathbf x)$.
Thus all we can conclude from inspection of the Lagrangian is that
$\boldsymbol\Pi$ equals
$\dot{\mathbf A}(\mathbf x)+\boldsymbol\nabla A^0(\mathbf x)$ plus the gradient
of some scalar. This ambiguity is removed by condition \eqref{eq:8.3.2}, which requires
that $\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\Pi=-J^0=\nabla^2A^0$. Because
$\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf A}=0$, we conclude that Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.3.6} does
indeed give the correct formula for $\Pi^i$.

Although the commutation relations \eqref{eq:8.3.5} are reasonably simple, we must
face the complication that $\boldsymbol\Pi$ does not commute with matter
fields and their canonical conjugates. If $F$ is any functional of these matter
degrees of freedom, then its Dirac bracket with $\mathbf A$ vanishes, but its
Dirac bracket with $\boldsymbol\Pi$ is
\begin{align*}
  [F,\boldsymbol\Pi(\mathbf z)]_{\mathrm D}
  &=-\int \dd^3x\,\dd^3y\,[F,\chi_{2\mathbf x}]_{\mathrm P}
  \frac{1}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rvert}
  [\chi_{1\mathbf y},\boldsymbol\Pi(\mathbf z)]_{\mathrm P}\\
  &=-\int \dd^3x\,\dd^3y\,[F,J^0(\mathbf x)]_{\mathrm P}
  \frac{1}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rvert}
  \boldsymbol\nabla\delta^3(\mathbf y-\mathbf z)\\
  &=-\int \dd^3y\,[F,A^0(\mathbf y)]_{\mathrm P}
  \boldsymbol\nabla\delta^3(\mathbf y-\mathbf z)\\
  &=[F,\boldsymbol\nabla A^0(\mathbf z)]_{\mathrm P}
  =[F,\boldsymbol\nabla A^0(\mathbf z)]_{\mathrm D}.
\end{align*}
In order to facilitate the transition to the interaction picture, instead of
expressing the Hamiltonian in terms of $\mathbf A$ and $\boldsymbol\Pi$, we
shall write it in terms of $\mathbf A$ and $\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp$, where
$\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp$ is the solenoidal part of $\boldsymbol\Pi$:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\Pi_\perp\equiv\boldsymbol\Pi-\boldsymbol\nabla A^0
  =\dot{\mathbf A},
  \tag{8.3.7}\label{eq:8.3.7}
\end{equation}
for which $[F,\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp(\mathbf z)]$ vanishes. By using the facts
that $\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp(\mathbf x)$ commutes with
$\boldsymbol\Pi(\mathbf y)-\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp(\mathbf y)
=\boldsymbol\nabla A^0(\mathbf y)$ and that $\partial_iA^0(\mathbf x)$
commutes with $\partial_jA^0(\mathbf y)$, it is easy to see that
$\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp(\mathbf x)$ satisfies the same commutation relations
\eqref{eq:8.3.5} as $\boldsymbol\Pi(\mathbf x)$, and also the simple constraint
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp=0.
  \tag{8.3.8}\label{eq:8.3.8}
\end{equation}

Now we need to construct a Hamiltonian. According to the general results of
the Appendix to Chapter 7, we can apply the usual relation between the
Hamiltonian and Lagrangian using the constrained variables $\mathbf A$ and
$\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp$, without first having explicitly to write the
Hamiltonian in terms of the unconstrained $Q$s and $P$s. In electrodynamics,
this gives
\begin{equation}
  H=\int \dd^3x\,[\Pi_{\perp i}\dot A^i+P_n\dot Q^n-\mathcal L],
  \tag{8.3.9}\label{eq:8.3.9}
\end{equation}
where, as mentioned earlier, $Q^n$ and $P_n$ are to be understood as the
matter canonical fields and their canonical conjugates. (We can use
$\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp$ in place of $\boldsymbol\Pi$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.3.9} because
$\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf A}=0$.)

To be specific, consider a theory with a Lagrangian density of the form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+J_\mu A^\mu
  +\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}},
  \tag{8.3.10}\label{eq:8.3.10}
\end{equation}
where $J_\mu$ is a current that does not involve $A^\mu$, and
$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}}$ is the Lagrangian for whatever other fields do
appear in $J^\mu$, aside from their electromagnetic interactions, which are
given explicitly by the term $J_\mu A^\mu$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.3.10}. (The
electrodynamics of spin $\tfrac12$ particles has a Lagrangian of this form,
but the electrodynamics of spinless particles is more complicated.) Replacing
$\dot{\mathbf A}$ everywhere with $\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp$, this gives a
Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:8.3.9} of the form
\[
  H=\int \dd^3x\left[
  \boldsymbol\Pi_\perp^2
  +\frac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2
  -\frac12(\boldsymbol\Pi_\perp+\boldsymbol\nabla A^0)^2
  -\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A+J^0A^0\right]+H_M,
\]
