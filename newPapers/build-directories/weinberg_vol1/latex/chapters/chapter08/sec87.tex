\subsection{Compton Scattering}

As an example of the methods described in this chapter, we shall consider
here the scattering of a photon by an electron (or other particle of spin
$\tfrac12$ and charge $-e$), to lowest order in $e$. We label the initial and
final photon momenta and polarization vectors by $k^\mu$, $e^\mu$ and
$k'{}^\mu$, $e'{}^\mu$, where $k^0=|\mathbf{k}|$ and
$k'{}^0=|\mathbf{k}'|$. Also, the initial and final electron momenta and spin
$z$-components are labelled $p^\mu,\sigma$ and $p'{}^\mu,\sigma'$, where
$p^0=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$ and $p'{}^0=\sqrt{\mathbf p'{}^2+m^2}$, with $m$
the electron mass. The lowest order Feynman diagrams for this process are
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:8.1}. Using the rules outlined in the previous section, the
corresponding $S$-matrix element is
\begin{align}
&S(\mathbf p,\sigma+\mathbf k,e\to
       \mathbf p',\sigma'+\mathbf k',e') \notag\\
&={\bar u(\mathbf p',\sigma')_{\beta'}\over(2\pi)^{3/2}}
 {e'^*_{\nu}\over(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2k'{}^0}}
 {u(\mathbf p,\sigma)_\alpha\over(2\pi)^{3/2}}
 {e_\mu\over(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2k^0}}
 \int d^4q\left[\frac{-i}{(2\pi)^4}\right]
 \left[\frac{-i\sl{q}+m}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon}\right]_{\alpha'\beta}
 \notag\\
&\quad\cdot\Bigl\{
 [e(2\pi)^4\gamma^\nu_{\beta'\alpha'}\delta^4(q-p'-k')]
 [e(2\pi)^4\gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}\delta^4(q-p-k)]
 \notag\\
&\qquad\quad+
 [e(2\pi)^4\gamma^\mu_{\beta'\alpha'}\delta^4(q+k-p')]
 [e(2\pi)^4\gamma^\nu_{\beta\alpha}\delta^4(q+k'-p)]
 \Bigr\}.
\tag{8.7.1}\label{eq:8.7.1}
\end{align}
Performing the (trivial) $q$-integral, collecting factors of $i$ and $2\pi$,
and rewriting the result in matrix notation, we have more simply
\begin{align}
S={}&{-ie^2\delta^4(p'+k'-p-k)\over
 (2\pi)^2\sqrt{2k'{}^0\,2k^0}}\bar u(\mathbf p',\sigma')
 \left[\sl{e'}{}^*
 { -i(\sl{p}+\sl{k})+m\over(p+k)^2+m^2}\sl{e}\right.
 \notag\\[-2pt]
&\left.\hspace{43mm}+\sl{e}
 { -i(\sl{p}-\sl{k'})+m\over(p-k')^2+m^2}
 \sl{e'}{}^*\right]u(\mathbf p,\sigma).
\tag{8.7.2}\label{eq:8.7.2}
\end{align}
(Here $\sl{e}^{\,*}$ means $e^*_\mu\gamma^\mu$, not $(\sl{e})^*$.
Also, we drop the $-i\epsilon$, because the denominators here do not
vanish.) Because $p^2=-m^2$ and $k^2=k'^2=0$, the denominators can be
simplified
\begin{align}
(p+k)^2+m^2&=2p\cdot k,
\tag{8.7.3}\label{eq:8.7.3}\\
(p-k')^2+m^2&=-2p\cdot k'.
\tag{8.7.4}\label{eq:8.7.4}
\end{align}
Also, the 'Feynman amplitude' $M$ is defined in general by Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.3.2},
which (because some scattering is assumed to take place) here reads
\begin{equation}
S=-2\pi i\delta^4(p'+k'-p-k)M,
\tag{8.7.5}\label{eq:8.7.5}
\end{equation}
so
\begin{align}
M={e^2\over4(2\pi)^3\sqrt{k^0k'{}^0}}\bar u(\mathbf p',\sigma')
\Biggl\{&\sl{e'}{}^*[-i(\sl{p}+\sl{k})+m]\sl{e}/(p\cdot k)
\notag\\
&-\sl{e}[-i(\sl{p}-\sl{k'})+m]\sl{e'}{}^*/(p\cdot k')\Biggr\}
u(\mathbf p,\sigma).
\tag{8.7.6}\label{eq:8.7.6}
\end{align}
The differential cross-section is given in terms of $M$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.15},
which here reads
\begin{equation}
d\sigma=(2\pi)^4u^{-1}|M|^2\delta^4(p'+k'-p-k)d^3p'\,d^3k'.
\tag{8.7.7}\label{eq:8.7.7}
\end{equation}
Since one of the particles here is massless, Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.4.17} for the initial
velocity gives
\begin{equation}
u=|p\cdot k|/p^0k^0.
\tag{8.7.8}\label{eq:8.7.8}
\end{equation}

To go further, it will be convenient to adopt a specific coordinate frame.
Since electrons in atoms move non-relativistically, the laboratory frame for
high-energy (X ray or gamma ray) photon--electron scattering experiments is
usually (though not always) one in which the initial electron can be taken to
be at rest. We will adopt this frame here, so that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p=0,\qquad p^0=m.
\tag{8.7.9}\label{eq:8.7.9}
\end{equation}
The velocity \eqref{eq:8.7.8} is then simply
\begin{equation}
u=1.
\tag{8.7.10}\label{eq:8.7.10}
\end{equation}
To save writing, we denote the photon energies by
\begin{align}
\omega&=k^0=|\mathbf k|=-p\cdot k/m,
\tag{8.7.11}\label{eq:8.7.11}\\
\omega'&=k'{}^0=|\mathbf k'|=-p\cdot k'/m.
\tag{8.7.12}\label{eq:8.7.12}
\end{align}
The three-momentum delta function in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.7} just serves to eliminate
the differential $d^3p'$, setting $\mathbf p'=\mathbf k-\mathbf k'$. This
leaves the remaining energy delta function
\begin{equation}
\delta(p'{}^0+k'{}^0-p^0-k^0)
=\delta\!\left(\sqrt{(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')^2+m^2}+\omega'-m-\omega\right).
\tag{8.7.13}\label{eq:8.7.13}
\end{equation}
This fixes $\omega'$ to satisfy
\[
\sqrt{\omega^2-2\omega\omega'\cos\theta+\omega'^2+m^2}
=\omega+m-\omega',
\]
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k'$. Squaring
both sides and cancelling $\omega'^2$ terms gives\footnote{Equivalently,
there is an increase in wavelength
\[
{1\over\omega'}-{1\over\omega}={1-\cos\theta\over m}.
\]}\,\footnote{The verification of this formula in the scattering of X rays by
electrons by A. H. Compton in 1922--3 played a key role in confirming
Einstein's 1905 proposal of a quantum of light, which soon after Compton's
experiments came to be known as the photon.}
\begin{equation}
\omega'=\omega{m\over m+\omega(1-\cos\theta)}\equiv\omega_c(\theta).
\tag{8.7.14}\label{eq:8.7.14}
\end{equation}
The energy delta function \eqref{eq:8.7.13} can be written
\begin{align}
\delta(p'{}^0+k'{}^0-p^0-k^0)
&={\delta(\omega'-\omega_c(\theta))\over
 |\partial[\sqrt{\omega^2-2\omega\omega'\cos\theta+\omega'^2+m^2}
        +\omega']/\partial\omega'|}
\notag\\
&={\delta(\omega'-\omega_c(\theta))\over
 |(\omega'-\omega\cos\theta)/p'{}^0+1|}
={p'{}^0\omega'\over m\omega}\delta(\omega'-\omega_c(\theta)).
\tag{8.7.15}\label{eq:8.7.15}
\end{align}
Also, the differential $d^3k'$ can be written
\begin{equation}
d^3k'=\omega'^2d\omega' d\Omega,
\tag{8.7.16}\label{eq:8.7.16}
\end{equation}
where $d\Omega$ is the solid angle into which the final photon is scattered.
The final delta function in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.15} just serves to eliminate the
differential $d\omega'$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.16}, leaving us with a differential
cross-section
\begin{equation}
d\sigma=(2\pi)^4|M|^2{p'{}^0\omega'^3\over m\omega}\,d\Omega
\tag{8.7.17}\label{eq:8.7.17}
\end{equation}
with $p'{}^0=m+\omega-\omega'$, and $\omega'$ given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.14}.

Usually we do not measure the spin $z$-component of the initial or final
electron. In such cases, we must sum over $\sigma'$ and average over $\sigma$,
or in other words take half the sum over $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$:
\begin{equation}
d\bar\sigma(\mathbf p+\mathbf k,e\to\mathbf p'+\mathbf k',e')
\equiv{1\over2}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}d\sigma(\mathbf p,\sigma+\mathbf k,e
\to\mathbf p',\sigma'+\mathbf k',e').
\tag{8.7.18}\label{eq:8.7.18}
\end{equation}
To calculate this, we use the standard formula
\begin{equation}
\sum_\sigma u_\alpha(\mathbf p,\sigma)\bar u_\beta(\mathbf p,\sigma)
={(-i\sl{p}+m)_{\alpha\beta}\over2p^0}
\tag{8.7.19}\label{eq:8.7.19}
\end{equation}
and likewise for the sum over $\sigma'$. It follows that for an arbitrary
$4\times4$ matrix $A$
\begin{align}
\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}|\bar u(\mathbf p',\sigma')Au(\mathbf p,\sigma)|^2
&=\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}(\bar u'A u)(\bar u\,\beta A^\dagger\beta u')
\notag\\
&=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left\{A\left({-i\sl{p}+m\over2p^0}\right)
\beta A^\dagger\beta\left({-i\sl{p'}+m\over2p'{}^0}\right)\right\}.
\tag{8.7.20}\label{eq:8.7.20}
\end{align}
Recalling that $\beta\gamma_\mu^\dagger\beta=-\gamma_\mu$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.6}
gives now
\begingroup\small
\begin{align}
\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}|M|^2
={e^4\over64(2\pi)^6\omega\omega'p^0p'{}^0}
\operatorname{Tr}\Biggl[
&\left\{\sl{e'}{}^*
 {[-i(\sl{p}+\sl{k})+m]\over p\cdot k}\sl{e}
-\sl{e}{[-i(\sl{p}-\sl{k'})+m]\over p\cdot k'}\sl{e'}{}^*\right\}
(-i\sl{p}+m)
\notag\\[-2pt]
\cdot&\left\{\sl{e}^*{[-i(\sl{p}+\sl{k})+m]\over p\cdot k}\sl{e'}
-\sl{e'}{[-i(\sl{p}-\sl{k'})+m]\over p\cdot k'}\sl{e}^*\right\}
(-i\sl{p'}+m)\Biggr].
\tag{8.7.21}\label{eq:8.7.21}
\end{align}
\endgroup
(Recall again that $\sl{e}^{\,*}$ means $e^*_\mu\gamma^\mu$, not
$(e_\mu\gamma^\mu)^*$, and likewise for $\sl{e'}{}^*$.) We work in a
'gauge' in which
\begin{equation}
e\cdot p=e^*\cdot p=e'\cdot p=e'^*\cdot p=0,
\tag{8.7.22}\label{eq:8.7.22}
\end{equation}
such as for instance Coulomb gauge in the laboratory frame, where
$e^0=e'{}^0=0$ and $\mathbf p=0$. This implies that
\[
[-i\sl{p}+m]\sl{e}[-i\sl{p}+m]
=\sl{e}[i\sl{p}+m][-i\sl{p}+m]
=\sl{e}(p^2+m^2)=\sl{e}(p_\mu p^\mu+m^2)=0
\]
and likewise for $\sl{e}^*$, $\sl{e'}$, and $\sl{e'}{}^*$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.21}
can therefore be written in the greatly simplified form
\begin{align}
\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}|M|^2
={-e^4\over64(2\pi)^6\omega\omega'p^0p'{}^0}
\operatorname{Tr}\Biggl[&\left\{{\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{k}\sl{e}\over p\cdot k}
+{\sl{e}\sl{k'}\sl{e'}{}^*\over p\cdot k'}\right\}(-i\sl{p}+m)
\notag\\[-2pt]
\cdot&\left\{{\sl{e}^*\sl{k}\sl{e'}\over p\cdot k}
+{\sl{e'}\sl{k'}\sl{e}^*\over p\cdot k'}\right\}(-i\sl{p'}+m)\Biggr].
\tag{8.7.23}\label{eq:8.7.23}
\end{align}
The trace of any product of an odd number of gamma matrices vanishes, so this
breaks up into terms of zeroth and second order in $m$:
\begin{align}
\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}|M|^2={e^4\over64(2\pi)^6\omega\omega'p^0p'{}^0}
\Biggl(&{T_1\over(p\cdot k)^2}+{T_2\over(p\cdot k)(p\cdot k')}
+{T_3\over(p\cdot k)(p\cdot k')}+{T_4\over(p\cdot k')^2}
\notag\\[-2pt]
&-{m^2t_1\over(p\cdot k)^2}-{m^2t_2\over(p\cdot k)(p\cdot k')}
-{m^2t_3\over(p\cdot k)(p\cdot k')}-{m^2t_4\over(p\cdot k')^2}\Biggr),
\tag{8.7.24}\label{eq:8.7.24}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
T_1&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{k}\sl{e}\sl{p}\sl{e}^*\sl{k}\sl{e'}\sl{p'}\},
\tag{8.7.25}\label{eq:8.7.25}\\
T_2&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{k}\sl{e}\sl{p}\sl{e'}\sl{k'}\sl{e}^*\sl{p'}\},
\tag{8.7.26}\label{eq:8.7.26}\\
T_3&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e}\sl{k'}\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{p}\sl{e}^*\sl{k}\sl{e'}\sl{p'}\},
\tag{8.7.27}\label{eq:8.7.27}\\
T_4&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e}\sl{k'}\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{p}\sl{e'}\sl{k'}\sl{e}^*\sl{p'}\},
\tag{8.7.28}\label{eq:8.7.28}\\
t_1&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{k}\sl{e}\sl{e}^*\sl{k}\sl{e'}\},
\tag{8.7.29}\label{eq:8.7.29}\\
t_2&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{k}\sl{e}\sl{e'}\sl{k'}\sl{e}^*\},
\tag{8.7.30}\label{eq:8.7.30}\\
t_3&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e}\sl{k'}\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{e}^*\sl{k}\sl{e'}\},
\tag{8.7.31}\label{eq:8.7.31}\\
t_4&=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e}\sl{k'}\sl{e'}{}^*\sl{e'}\sl{k'}\sl{e}^*\}.
\tag{8.7.32}\label{eq:8.7.32}
\end{align}
The Appendix to this chapter shows how to calculate any trace
$\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{a}\sl{b}\sl{c}\sl{d}\cdots\}$ as a sum of products
of scalar products of the four-vectors $a,b,c,d,\ldots$. In general, traces
of products of 6 or 8 gamma matrices like the $t_k$ or $T_k$ would be given by
a sum of 15 or 105 terms, respectively, but fortunately here most scalar
products vanish; in addition to Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.22}, we also have
$k\cdot k=k'\cdot k'=0$. (Furthermore,
$e\cdot e^*=e'\cdot e'^*=1$.) To simplify the calculation further, let us
specialize to the case of \emph{linear} polarization, where $e^\mu$ and
$e'{}^\mu$ are real. Dropping the asterisks in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:8.7.25}--\eqref{eq:8.7.32}, we
have then
\[
T_1=\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}\sl{k}\sl{e}\sl{p}\sl{e}\sl{k}\sl{e'}\sl{p'}\}.
\]
Since $e^\mu p_\mu=0$ and $e^\mu e_\mu=1$, we have
\[
\sl{e}\sl{p}\sl{e}=-\sl{p}\sl{e}\sl{e}=-\sl{p},
\]
so
\[
T_1=-\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}\sl{k}\sl{p}\sl{k}\sl{e'}\sl{p'}\}.
\]
Also, $k^\mu k_\mu=0$, so
\[
\sl{k}\sl{p}\sl{k}=-\sl{k}\sl{k}\sl{p}+2\sl{k}\,p\cdot k
=2\sl{k}\,p\cdot k
\]
and hence
\[
T_1=-2p\cdot k\operatorname{Tr}\{\sl{e'}\sl{k}\sl{e'}\sl{p'}\}.
\]
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.A.6}, this is
\[
T_1=-8p\cdot k[2e'\cdot k\,e'\cdot p'-k\cdot p'].
\]
It is convenient to make the substitutions
\begin{align*}
e'\cdot p'&=e'\cdot[p+k-k']=e'\cdot k,\\
k\cdot p'&=-\tfrac12(p'-k)^2-\tfrac12m^2
=-\tfrac12(p-k')^2-\tfrac12m^2=p\cdot k',
\end{align*}
so
\begin{equation}
T_1=-16p\cdot k(e'\cdot k)^2+8p\cdot k\,p\cdot k'.
\tag{8.7.33}\label{eq:8.7.33}
\end{equation}
A similar (though more lengthy) calculation gives
\begin{align}
T_2=T_3={}&-8(e\cdot k')^2(p\cdot k)
+16(e\cdot e')^2p\cdot k'\,p\cdot k
+8(e\cdot e')^2k\cdot k'\,m^2
\notag\\
&-8(e\cdot e')m^2(k\cdot e')(k'\cdot e)
+8(e'\cdot k)^2p\cdot k'
-4(k\cdot p)^2
\notag\\
&+4(k\cdot k')(p\cdot p')-4(k\cdot p')(p\cdot k'),
\tag{8.7.34}\label{eq:8.7.34}\\
T_4={}&-16p\cdot k'(e\cdot k')^2+8(p\cdot k)(p\cdot k'),
\tag{8.7.35}\label{eq:8.7.35}\\
t_1=t_4={}&0,
\tag{8.7.36}\label{eq:8.7.36}\\
t_2=t_3={}&-8(e\cdot e')\,(k\cdot e')\,(k'\cdot e)
+8(p\cdot k')(e\cdot e')^2-4(k\cdot k').
\tag{8.7.37}\label{eq:8.7.37}
\end{align}
Combining all these terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.24} gives
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}|M|^2
={e^4\over64(2\pi)^6\omega\omega'p^0p'{}^0}
\left[{8(k\cdot k')^2\over(k\cdot p)(k'\cdot p)}+32(e\cdot e')^2\right].
\tag{8.7.38}\label{eq:8.7.38}
\end{equation}
All this applies in any Lorentz frame. In the laboratory frame, we have the
special results
\[
k\cdot k'=\omega\omega'(\cos\theta-1)
=m\omega\omega'\left({1\over\omega}-{1\over\omega'}\right),
\qquad p\cdot k=-m\omega,\quad p\cdot k'=-m\omega'.
\]
Combining Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.38} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.17}, the laboratory frame cross-section
is
\begin{align}
{1\over2}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}d\sigma(\mathbf p,\sigma+\mathbf k,e
\to\mathbf p',\sigma'+\mathbf k',e')
={e^4\omega'^2d\Omega\over64\pi^2m^2\omega^2}
\left[{\omega\over\omega'}+{\omega'\over\omega}-2+4(e\cdot e')^2\right].
\tag{8.7.39}\label{eq:8.7.39}
\end{align}
This is the celebrated formula derived (using old-fashioned perturbation
theory) by O. Klein and Y. Nishina~\hyperref[ref:8.4]{[4]} in 1929.

As discussed in Section 8.6, if the incoming photon is (as usual) not prepared
in a state with any particular polarization, then we must average over two
orthonormal values of $\mathbf e$. This average gives
\[
{1\over2}\sum_e e_i e_j={1\over2}(\delta_{ij}-\hat k_i\hat k_j)
\]
and the differential cross-section is then
\begin{equation}
{1\over4}\sum_{e,\sigma,\sigma'}d\sigma(\mathbf p,\sigma+\mathbf k,e
\to\mathbf p',\sigma'+\mathbf k',e')
={e^4\omega'^2d\Omega\over64\pi^2m^2\omega^2}
\left[{\omega\over\omega'}+{\omega'\over\omega}
-2(\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf e')^2\right].
\tag{8.7.40}\label{eq:8.7.40}
\end{equation}
We see that the scattered photon is preferentially polarized in a direction
perpendicular to the incident as well as the final photon direction, i.e.,
perpendicular to the plane in which the scattering takes place. This is a
well-known result, responsible among other things for the polarization of
light from eclipsing binary stars.\footnote{The light from one of the stars
is polarized when it is scattered by free electrons in the outer atmosphere
of the other, cooler, star when both are along the same line of sight. This
polarization is normally undetectable because it cancels when the astronomer
adds up light from all parts of the star's disk. The polarization has been
observed in eclipsing binary stars at times when the cooler star blocks the
light from just one side of the hotter star.}

To calculate the cross-section for experiments in which the final photon
polarization is not measured, we must sum Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.40} over $e'$, using
\[
\sum_{e'}e'_i e'_j=\delta_{ij}-\hat k'_i\hat k'_j.
\]
This gives
\begin{equation}
{1\over4}\sum_{e,e',\sigma,\sigma'}d\sigma(\mathbf p,\sigma+\mathbf k,e
\to\mathbf p',\sigma'+\mathbf k',e')
={e^4\omega'^2d\Omega\over32\pi^2m^2\omega^2}
\left[{\omega\over\omega'}+{\omega'\over\omega}-1+\cos^2\theta\right],
\tag{8.7.41}\label{eq:8.7.41}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\hat{\mathbf k}$ and
$\hat{\mathbf k}'$. In the non-relativistic case, $\omega\ll m$,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.7.41} gives
\begin{equation}
{1\over4}\sum_{e,e',\sigma,\sigma'}d\sigma
={e^4d\Omega\over32\pi^2m^2}(1+\cos^2\theta).
\tag{8.7.42}\label{eq:8.7.42}
\end{equation}
The solid angle integral is
\[
\int[1+\cos^2\theta]d\Omega
=\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^\pi[1+\cos^2\theta]\sin\theta\,d\theta
={16\pi\over3},
\]
giving a total cross-section for $\omega\ll m$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_T={e^4\over6\pi m^2}.
\tag{8.7.43}\label{eq:8.7.43}
\end{equation}
This is often written $\sigma_T=8\pi r_0^2/3$, where
$r_0=e^2/4\pi m=2.818\cdot10^{-13}$ cm is known as the \emph{classical
electron radius}. Expression \eqref{eq:8.7.43} is called the \emph{Thomson
cross-section}, after J. J. Thomson, the discoverer of the electron.
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:8.7.42} and \eqref{eq:8.7.43} were originally derived using classical mechanics
and electrodynamics, by calculating the reradiation of light by a
non-relativistic point charge in a plane wave electromagnetic field.
