The original approach to quantum electrodynamics was to take for granted
Maxwell's classical theory of electromagnetism, and quantize it. It will
probably not surprise the reader that this book will follow a different path.
We shall first infer the need for a principle of gauge invariance from the
peculiar difficulties that arise in formulating a quantum theory of massless
particles with spin, and then deduce the main features of electrodynamics
from the gauge invariance principle. After that we shall follow a more
conventional modern approach, in which one takes gauge invariance as
the starting point and uses it to deduce the existence of a vector potential
describing massless particles of unit spin.

It is too soon to tell which of these two alternatives corresponds to
the logical order of nature. Most theorists have tended to take gauge
invariance as a starting point, but in modern string theories~\hyperref[ref:8.1]{[1]} the argument
runs the other way; one first notices a state of mass zero and unit spin
among the normal modes of a string, and then from that deduces the
gauge invariance of the effective field theory that describes such particles.
At any rate, as we shall see, using either approach one is led to the
quantized version of Maxwell's theory, still the paradigmatic example of
a successful quantum field theory.

\subsection{Gauge Invariance}

Let's start by recalling the problems encountered in constructing covariant
free fields for a massless particle of helicity $\pm1$. We saw in Section 5.9
that there is no difficulty in constructing an antisymmetric tensor free
field $f_{\mu\nu}(x)$ for such particles. This field can be expressed in terms of the
four-potential $a_\mu(x)$, given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.9.23}, through the familiar relation
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mu\nu}(x)=\partial_\mu a_\nu(x)-\partial_\nu a_\mu(x).
  \tag{8.1.1}\label{eq:8.1.1}
\end{equation}
However, Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.9.23} shows that the $a_\mu(x)$ transforms as a four-vector
only up to a gauge transformation
\begin{equation}
  U_0(\Lambda)a_\mu(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda)
  =\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu a_\nu(\Lambda x)+\partial_\mu\Omega(x,\Lambda).
  \tag{8.1.2}\label{eq:8.1.2}
\end{equation}
There is, in fact, no way to construct a true four-vector as a linear
combination of the creation and annihilation operators for helicity $\pm1$.
This is one way of understanding the presence of singularities at $m=0$ in
the propagator of a massive vector field
\[
  \Delta_{\mu\nu}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4q\,e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}
  \frac{\eta_{\mu\nu}+q_\mu q_\nu/m^2}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon},
\]
which prevent us from dealing with massless particles of helicity $\pm1$ by
simply passing to the limit $m\to0$ of the theory of a massive particle of
spin one.

We could avoid these problems by demanding that all interactions
involve only\footnote{We now use $A_\mu$ and $F_{\mu\nu}$ for the electromagnetic
potential vector and the field strength tensor because these are interacting
fields.} $F_{\mu\nu}(x)\equiv\partial_\mu A_\nu(x)-\partial_\nu A_\mu(x)$ and its
derivatives, not $A_\mu(x)$, but this is not the most general possibility, and
not the one realized in nature. Instead of banishing $A_\mu(x)$ from the
action, we shall require instead that the part of the action $I_M$ for matter
and its interaction with radiation be invariant under the general gauge
transformation
\begin{equation}
  A_\mu(x)\longrightarrow A_\mu(x)+\partial_\mu\epsilon(x)
  \tag{8.1.3}\label{eq:8.1.3}
\end{equation}
(at least when the matter fields satisfy the field equations) so that the extra
term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.2} should have no effect. The change in the matter action
under the transformations \eqref{eq:8.1.3} may be written
\begin{equation}
  \delta I_M=\int d^4x\,\frac{\delta I_M}{\delta A_\mu(x)}
  \partial_\mu\epsilon(x).
  \tag{8.1.4}\label{eq:8.1.4}
\end{equation}
Hence the Lorentz invariance of $I_M$ requires that
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\mu\frac{\delta I_M}{\delta A_\mu(x)}=0.
  \tag{8.1.5}\label{eq:8.1.5}
\end{equation}
This is trivially true if $I_M$ involves only $F_{\mu\nu}(x)$ and its derivatives,
along with matter fields. In this case
\[
  \frac{\delta I_M}{\delta A_\mu(x)}
  =2\partial^\nu\frac{\delta I_M}{\delta F_{\mu\nu}(x)}.
\]
But if $I_M$ involves $A_\mu(x)$ itself then Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.5} is a non-trivial
constraint on the theory.

Now, what sort of theory will provide conserved currents to which we
can couple the field $A^\mu(x)$? We saw in Section 7.3 that infinitesimal internal
symmetries of the action imply the existence of conserved currents. In
particular, if the transformation\footnote{Because the field transformation
matrix is taken now to be diagonal it is not convenient here to use the
summation convention for sums over field indices, so there is no sum over
$\ell$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.6}.}
\begin{equation}
  \delta\Psi^\ell(x)=i\epsilon(x)q_\ell\Psi^\ell(x)
  \tag{8.1.6}\label{eq:8.1.6}
\end{equation}
leaves the matter action invariant for a constant $\epsilon$, then for general
infinitesimal functions $\epsilon(x)$ the change in the matter action must take the
form
\begin{equation}
  \delta I_M=-\int d^4x\,J^\mu(x)\partial_\mu\epsilon(x).
  \tag{8.1.7}\label{eq:8.1.7}
\end{equation}
When the matter fields satisfy their field equations, the matter action is
stationary with respect to \textit{any} variation of the $\Psi_\ell$, so in this case \eqref{eq:8.1.7}
must vanish, and hence
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\mu J^\mu=0.
  \tag{8.1.8}\label{eq:8.1.8}
\end{equation}
In particular, we saw in Section 7.3 that if $I_M$ is the integral of a function
$\mathcal L_M$ of $\Psi^\ell$ and $\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell$, then the conserved current is given by\footnote{Here
$\Psi^\ell$ is understood to run over all independent fields other than
$A^\mu$. We use a capital psi to indicate that these are Heisenberg-picture
fields, whose time-dependence includes the effects of interactions. Of course,
this $\Psi^\ell$ is not to be confused with a state-vector or wave function.}
\[
  J^\mu=-i\sum_\ell
  \frac{\partial\mathcal L_M}{\partial(\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell)}
  q_\ell\Psi^\ell,
\]
and this generates the transformations \eqref{eq:8.1.6} in the sense that
\begin{equation}
  [Q,\Psi^\ell(x)]=-q_\ell\Psi^\ell(x),
  \tag{8.1.9}\label{eq:8.1.9}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the time-independent charge operator
\begin{equation}
  Q=\int d^3x\,J^0.
  \tag{8.1.10}\label{eq:8.1.10}
\end{equation}
We can therefore construct a Lorentz-invariant theory by coupling the
vector field $A_\mu$ to the conserved current $J^\mu$, in the sense that
$\delta I_M/\delta A_\mu(x)$ is taken to be proportional to $J^\mu(x)$. Any
constant of proportionality may be absorbed into the definition of the
overall scale of the charges $q_\ell$, so we may simply set these quantities
equal:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta I_M}{\delta A_\mu(x)}=J^\mu(x).
  \tag{8.1.11}\label{eq:8.1.11}
\end{equation}
The conservation of electric charge only allows us to fix the values of all
charges in terms of the value of any one of them, conventionally taken to
be the electron charge, denoted $-e$. It is Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.11} that gives a definite
meaning\footnote{Of course, Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.11} fixes the definition of $e$ only after
we have defined how we are normalizing $A_\mu(x)$. The question of
electromagnetic field normalization is taken up in Section 10.4.} to the value
of $e$.

The requirement \eqref{eq:8.1.11} may be restated as a principle of invariance~\hyperref[ref:8.1a]{[1a]}:
the matter action is invariant under the joint transformations
\begin{align}
  \delta A_\mu(x)&=\partial_\mu\epsilon(x),
  \tag{8.1.12}\label{eq:8.1.12}\\
  \delta\Psi_\ell(x)&=i\epsilon(x)q_\ell\Psi_\ell(x).
  \tag{8.1.13}\label{eq:8.1.13}
\end{align}
A symmetry of this type with an arbitrary function $\epsilon(x)$ is called a
\textit{local symmetry}, or a gauge invariance of the second kind. A symmetry
under a transformation with $\epsilon$ constant is called a \textit{global
symmetry}, or a gauge invariance of the first kind. Several exact local
symmetries are now known, but the only purely global symmetries appear to be
accidents enforced by other principles. (See Section 12.5.)

We have not yet said anything about the action for photons themselves.
As a guess, we can take this to be the same as for massive vector fields,
but with $m=0$:
\begin{equation}
  I_\gamma=-\frac14\int d^4x\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}.
  \tag{8.1.14}\label{eq:8.1.14}
\end{equation}
This is the same as the action used in classical electrodynamics, but its
real justification is that it is (up to a constant) the unique gauge-invariant
functional that is quadratic in $F_{\mu\nu}$, without higher derivatives. Also,
as we will see in the next section, it leads to a consistent quantum theory.
If there are any terms in the action of $A$ with higher derivatives and/or
of higher order in $F_{\mu\nu}$ they can be lumped into what we have called
the matter action. Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:8.1.11} and \eqref{eq:8.1.14}, the field equation for
electromagnetism now reads
\begin{equation}
  0=\frac{\delta}{\delta A_\nu}[I_\gamma+I_M]
  =\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+J^\nu.
  \tag{8.1.15}\label{eq:8.1.15}
\end{equation}
We recognize these as the usual inhomogeneous Maxwell equations, with
current $J^\nu$. There are also other, homogeneous, Maxwell equations
\begin{equation}
  0=\partial_\mu F_{\nu\rho}+\partial_\rho F_{\mu\nu}
  +\partial_\nu F_{\rho\mu},
  \tag{8.1.16}\label{eq:8.1.16}
\end{equation}
which follow directly from the definition
$F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu$.

In the above discussion, we have started with the existence of massless
spin one particles, and have been led to infer the invariance of the matter
action under a local gauge transformation \eqref{eq:8.1.12}, \eqref{eq:8.1.13}. As usually
presented, the derivation runs in the opposite direction. That is, one starts
with a global internal symmetry
\begin{equation}
  \delta\Psi^\ell(x)=i\epsilon q_\ell\Psi^\ell(x)
  \tag{8.1.17}\label{eq:8.1.17}
\end{equation}
and asks what must be done to promote this to a local symmetry
\begin{equation}
  \delta\Psi^\ell(x)=i\epsilon(x)q_\ell\Psi^\ell(x).
  \tag{8.1.18}\label{eq:8.1.18}
\end{equation}
If the Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$ depended only on fields $\Psi^\ell(x)$ and
not on their derivatives then it would make no difference whether $\epsilon$ is
constant or not; invariance with $\epsilon$ constant would imply invariance with
$\epsilon$ a function of spacetime position. But all realistic Lagrangians do
involve field derivatives, and here we have the problem that derivatives of
fields transform differently from fields themselves:
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x)
  =i\epsilon(x)q_\ell\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x)
  +iq_\ell\Psi^\ell(x)\partial_\mu\epsilon(x).
  \tag{8.1.19}\label{eq:8.1.19}
\end{equation}
In order to cancel the second term here, we `invent' a vector field $A_\mu(x)$
with transformation rule
\begin{equation}
  \delta A_\mu(x)=\partial_\mu\epsilon(x)
  \tag{8.1.20}\label{eq:8.1.20}
\end{equation}
and require that the Lagrangian density depend on $\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell$ and
$A_\mu$ only in the combination
\begin{equation}
  D_\mu\Psi^\ell\equiv\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell-iq_\ell A_\mu\Psi^\ell,
  \tag{8.1.21}\label{eq:8.1.21}
\end{equation}
which transforms just like $\Psi^\ell$
\begin{equation}
  \delta D_\mu\Psi^\ell(x)=i\epsilon(x)q_\ell D_\mu\Psi^\ell(x).
  \tag{8.1.22}\label{eq:8.1.22}
\end{equation}
A matter Lagrangian density $\mathcal L_M(\Psi,D\Psi)$ that is formed only
out of $\Psi^\ell$ and $D_\mu\Psi^\ell$ will be invariant under the
transformations \eqref{eq:8.1.18}, \eqref{eq:8.1.20}, with $\epsilon(x)$ an arbitrary function,
if it is invariant with $\epsilon$ a constant. With the Lagrangian of this form,
we have
\[
  \frac{\delta I_M}{\delta A_\mu}
  =\sum_\ell\frac{\partial\mathcal L_M}{\partial D_\mu\Psi^\ell}
  (-iq_\ell\Psi^\ell)
  =-i\sum_\ell\frac{\partial\mathcal L_M}{\partial\,\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell}
  q_\ell\Psi^\ell,
\]
which is the same as Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.1.11}. (We could also include $F_{\mu\nu}$ and its
derivatives in $\mathcal L_M$.) From this point of view, the masslessness of the
particles described by $A_\mu$ is a consequence of gauge invariance rather
than an assumption: a term $-\tfrac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu$ in the Lagrangian density
would violate gauge invariance.
