\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Transition to the $S$-Matrix}{Transition to the S-Matrix}}

As already mentioned, we can easily convert the general quantum mechanical
results of Section 9.1 to a notation appropriate to quantum field theory, by
letting the index $a$ run over points $\mathbf{x}$ in space and over a
spin-and-species index $m$, and replacing $Q_a(t)$ and $P_a(t)$ with
$Q_m(\mathbf{x},t)$ and $P_m(\mathbf{x},t)$, respectively. Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38}
then reads\footnote{We are now writing $H$ and the $\mathcal{O}$s with
square brackets, to remind us that $H[q(t),p(t)]$ and
$\mathcal{O}[p(t),q(t)]$ are functionals of $q_m(\mathbf{x},t)$ and
$p_m(\mathbf{x},t)$ at a fixed time $t$.}
\begin{align}
  &\bra{q';t'}T\left\{\mathcal{O}_A[P(t_A),Q(t_A)],
  \mathcal{O}_B[P(t_B),Q(t_B)],\ldots\right\}\ket{q;t}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \int_{\substack{q_m(\mathbf{x},t)=q_m(\mathbf{x})\\
                  q_m(\mathbf{x},t')=q'_m(\mathbf{x})}}
  \prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\dd q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  \prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\frac{\dd p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)}{2\pi}
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot
  \mathcal{O}_A[p(t_A),q(t_A)]\mathcal{O}_B[p(t_B),q(t_B)]\cdots
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\left[\ii\int_t^{t'}\dd\tau
  \left\{\int \dd^3x\,\dot q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  -H[q(\tau),p(\tau)]\right\}\right].
  \tag{9.2.1}\label{eq:9.2.1}
\end{align}

However, in field theory Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.1} is not exactly what we want.
Experimentalists do not measure probability amplitudes for transitions
between eigenstates $\bra{q';t'}$ and $\ket{q;t}$ of the quantum field $Q$,
but rather $S$-matrix elements, the probability amplitudes for transitions
between states that at $t\to-\infty$ or $t\to+\infty$ contain definite
numbers of particles of various types. These are called `in' and `out' states,
$\InKet{\alpha}$ and $\OutKet{\beta}$, where $\alpha$
and $\beta$ denote sets of particles characterized by the various particles'
momenta, spin $z$-component (or helicity), and species. To calculate a matrix
element of a time-ordered product (perhaps empty) between such states, we
need to multiply Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.1} by the `wave functions'
$\OutBra{\beta}\ket{q';t'}$ and
$\bra{q;t}\InKet{\alpha}$ at any fixed times $t$ and $t'$, taken
for convenience here to be $-\infty$ and $+\infty$, respectively, and then
perform an integral over the `arguments' $q_m(\mathbf{x})$ and
$q'_m(\mathbf{x})$ of these wave functions. But instead of constraining the
path integral over $q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)$ by the conditions
\begin{equation}
  q_m(\mathbf{x},+\infty)=q'_m(\mathbf{x}),
  \qquad
  q_m(\mathbf{x},-\infty)=q_m(\mathbf{x}),
  \tag{9.2.2}\label{eq:9.2.2}
\end{equation}
and then integrating over $q'_m(\mathbf{x})$ and $q_m(\mathbf{x})$, we can
just as well do an unconstrained integral over $q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)$ (and
also over $p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)$), and set the arguments of the wave
functions equal to the values given by Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.2}:
\begin{align}
  &\OutBra{\beta}T\left\{
  \mathcal{O}_A[P(t_A),Q(t_A)],
  \mathcal{O}_B[P(t_B),Q(t_B)],\ldots\right\}\InKet{\alpha}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \int\prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\dd q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  \prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\frac{\dd p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)}{2\pi}
  \mathcal{O}_A[p(t_A),q(t_A)]\mathcal{O}_B[p(t_B),q(t_B)]\cdots
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\left[\ii\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd\tau
  \left\{\int \dd^3x\,\dot q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  -H[q(\tau),p(\tau)]\right\}\right]
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot
  \OutBra{\beta}\ket{q(+\infty);+\infty}
  \bra{q(-\infty);-\infty}\InKet{\alpha}.
  \tag{9.2.3}\label{eq:9.2.3}
\end{align}
Incidentally, this result leads immediately\footnote{It is only necessary to
note that, for a Hamiltonian
$H[P(t),Q(t)]+\sum_A\int \dd^3x\,\epsilon_A(\mathbf{x},t)
\mathcal{O}_A(\mathbf{x},t)$, the $S$-matrix is given by Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3} as
\begin{align*}
  \OutBra{\beta}\InKetWith{\alpha}{\epsilon}
  ={}&\int\prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\dd q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  \prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\frac{\dd p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)}{2\pi}
  \\
  &\cdot\exp\left[\ii\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd t
  \left\{\int \dd^3x\,\dot q_m(\mathbf{x},t)p_m(\mathbf{x},t)
  -H[q(t),p(t)]
  -\sum_A\int \dd^3x\,\epsilon_A(\mathbf{x},t)
  \mathcal{O}_A(\mathbf{x},t)\right\}\right]
  \\
  &\cdot\OutBra{\beta}\ket{q(+\infty);+\infty}
  \bra{q(-\infty);-\infty}\InKet{\alpha}.
\end{align*}
The left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6.4.3} is the derivative of this expression with
respect to $\epsilon_A$, $\epsilon_B$, etc., at $\epsilon=0$, which yields
the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3}, and using Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3} again then
immediately gives the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6.4.3}.} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6.4.3}, a
theorem that we use repeatedly to relate sums of off-shell Feynman graphs to
matrix elements of Heisenberg-picture operators between exact energy
eigenstates.

It is necessary now to consider how to calculate the wave functions appearing
as the final pair of factors in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3}. Let's first consider the simplest
and most important case, the vacuum. (We saw in Section 6.4 that $S$-matrix
elements may be easily calculated from the vacuum expectation values of
time-ordered products.) We assume as usual that for $t\to\mp\infty$, matrix
elements may be calculated as if there were no interactions. The `in' and
`out' vacua may thus be defined by the conditions
\begin{equation}
  a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n,\mathrm{in}}\InKet{\Omega}=0,
  \qquad
  a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n,\mathrm{out}}\OutKet{\Omega}=0,
  \tag{9.2.4}\label{eq:9.2.4}
\end{equation}
where $a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n,\mathrm{in}}$ and
$a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n,\mathrm{out}}$ are the operators appearing
in the coefficients of $\exp(\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\ii Et)$ in the
plane-wave expansion of the operator $Q_m(\mathbf{x},t)$ at
$t\to-\infty$ and $t\to+\infty$, respectively. For instance, for the real
scalar field of a neutral spinless particle, we have in effect
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\mathbf{x},t)\underset{t\to\mp\infty}{\longrightarrow}
  (2\pi)^{-3/2}\int \dd^3p\,(2E)^{-1/2}
  \left[a_{\substack{\mathrm{in}\\\mathrm{out}}}(\mathbf{p})
  e^{\ii p\cdot x}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right],
  \tag{9.2.5}\label{eq:9.2.5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Pi(\mathbf{x},t)\underset{t\to\mp\infty}{\longrightarrow}
  \dot\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
  \underset{t\to\mp\infty}{\longrightarrow}
  -\ii(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int \dd^3p\,(E/2)^{1/2}
  \left[a_{\substack{\mathrm{in}\\\mathrm{out}}}(\mathbf{p})
  e^{\ii p\cdot x}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right],
  \tag{9.2.6}\label{eq:9.2.6}
\end{equation}
where $p^0\equiv E\equiv\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}$, and we here use the
conventional $\Phi$ and $\Pi$ rather than $Q$ and $P$ for a scalar field,
and drop the unnecessary labels $m,\sigma,n$. Inverting the Fourier
transforms and taking a linear combination of the resulting expressions, we
have
\begin{align}
  a_{\substack{\mathrm{in}\\\mathrm{out}}}(\mathbf{p})
  ={}&\lim_{t\to\mp\infty}\frac{e^{\ii Et}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}
  \int \dd^3x\,e^{-\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
  \left[\sqrt{\frac{E}{2}}\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
  +\ii\sqrt{\frac{1}{2E}}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t)\right].
  \tag{9.2.7}\label{eq:9.2.7}
\end{align}
As mentioned in Section 9.1, the `momentum' $\Pi(\mathbf{x},t)$ acts on
wave-functions in a $\phi$-basis as the variational derivative
$-\ii\delta/\delta\phi(\mathbf{x},t)$, so in this basis the conditions \eqref{eq:9.2.4}
read
\begin{equation}
  0=\int \dd^3x\,e^{-\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
  \left[\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi(\mathbf{x})}+E(\mathbf{p})\phi(\mathbf{x})\right]
  \bra{\phi(\mathbf{x});\mp\infty}\InOutKet{\Omega}.
  \tag{9.2.8}\label{eq:9.2.8}
\end{equation}
The analogous ordinary differential equation has a well-known Gaussian
solution, so let's try a Gaussian ansatz here:
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\phi(\mathbf{x});\mp\infty}\InOutKet{\Omega}
  =\mathcal{N}\exp\left(-\frac12\int \dd^3x\,\dd^3y\,
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\phi(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{y})\right),
  \tag{9.2.9}\label{eq:9.2.9}
\end{equation}
with kernel $\mathcal{E}$ and constant $\mathcal{N}$ to be determined.
Substituting this in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.8}, we see that the functional differential
equation for the vacuum wave functional is satisfied if for all $\phi$
\begin{equation}
  0=\int \dd^3x\,e^{-\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
  \left[\int \dd^3y\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\phi(\mathbf{y})
  -E(\mathbf{p})\phi(\mathbf{x})\right]
  \tag{9.2.10}\label{eq:9.2.10}
\end{equation}
or, in other words, if
\begin{equation}
  \int \dd^3x\,e^{-\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
  =E(\mathbf{p})e^{-\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{y}}.
  \tag{9.2.11}\label{eq:9.2.11}
\end{equation}
The solution is easily found by inverting the Fourier transform
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(2\pi)^{-3}
  \int \dd^3p\,e^{\ii\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})}E(\mathbf{p}).
  \tag{9.2.12}\label{eq:9.2.12}
\end{equation}
(Recall that $E(\mathbf{p})\equiv\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}$.) This is
actually the most useful representation for the kernel $\mathcal{E}$, but we
may note in passing that for $\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{y}$, $\mathcal{E}$ may
also be written in terms of a Hankel function of negative order
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=
  \frac{m}{2\pi^2r}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(\frac{1}{r}K_{-1}(\ii mr)\right),
  \tag{9.2.13}\label{eq:9.2.13}
\end{equation}
where $r\equiv|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|$. The constant $\mathcal{N}$ in
Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.9} may be formally obtained from the normalization condition for
the vacuum state, but we will not need this result.

According to Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.9}, in calculating vacuum expectation values in the
theory of a scalar field, the product of the last two factors in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3} is
\begin{align}
  &\OutBra{\Omega}\ket{\phi(+\infty);+\infty}
  \bra{\phi(-\infty);-\infty}\InKet{\Omega}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}|\mathcal{N}|^2\exp\left(-\frac12\int \dd^3x\,\dd^3y\,
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
  \left[\phi(\mathbf{x},+\infty)\phi(\mathbf{y},+\infty)
  +\phi(\mathbf{x},-\infty)\phi(\mathbf{y},-\infty)\right]\right)
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}|\mathcal{N}|^2\exp\left(-\frac12\epsilon
  \int \dd^3x\,\dd^3y\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd\tau\,
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
  \phi(\mathbf{x},\tau)\phi(\mathbf{y},\tau)e^{-\epsilon|\tau|}\right),
  \tag{9.2.14}\label{eq:9.2.14}
\end{align}
where $\epsilon$ is a positive infinitesimal. To obtain the final expression,
we have used the fact that for any reasonably smooth function $f(\tau)$,
\begin{equation}
  f(+\infty)+f(-\infty)=\lim_{\epsilon\to0+}\epsilon
  \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd\tau\,f(\tau)e^{-\epsilon|\tau|}.
  \tag{9.2.15}\label{eq:9.2.15}
\end{equation}
Inserting Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.14} in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3} now gives
\begin{align}
  &\OutBra{\Omega}T\left\{
  \mathcal{O}_A[\Pi(t_A),\Phi(t_A)],
  \mathcal{O}_B[\Pi(t_B),\Phi(t_B)],\ldots\right\}\InKet{\Omega}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}|\mathcal{N}|^2\int
  \prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x}}\dd\phi(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  \prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x}}\frac{\dd\pi(\mathbf{x},\tau)}{2\pi}
  \mathcal{O}_A[\pi(t_A),\phi(t_A)]
  \mathcal{O}_B[\pi(t_B),\phi(t_B)]\cdots
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\left[\ii\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd\tau
  \left\{\begin{aligned}
  &\int \dd^3x\,\dot\phi(\mathbf{x},\tau)\pi(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  -H[\phi(\tau),\pi(\tau)]\\
  &\quad+\frac12\ii\epsilon\int \dd^3x\,\dd^3y\,
  \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})e^{-\epsilon|\tau|}
  \phi(\mathbf{x},\tau)\phi(\mathbf{y},\tau)
  \end{aligned}\right\}\right].
  \tag{9.2.16}\label{eq:9.2.16}
\end{align}
We shall see in Section 9.4 that the whole effect of the last term in the
argument of the exponential in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.16} is to provide the $-\ii\epsilon$
in the denominator of the scalar field propagator in momentum space,
$[p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}$. We will not go into the corresponding details
for fields of general spin, but will simply state that in general
\begin{align}
  &\OutBra{\Omega}T\left\{
  \mathcal{O}_A[P_A(t_A),Q(t_A)],
  \mathcal{O}_B[P_B(t_B),Q(t_B)],\ldots\right\}\InKet{\Omega}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}|\mathcal{N}|^2\int
  \left[\prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\dd q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)\right]
  \left[\prod_{\tau,\mathbf{x},m}\frac{\dd p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)}{2\pi}\right]
  \mathcal{O}_A[p(t_A),q(t_A)]\mathcal{O}_B[p(t_B),q(t_B)]\cdots
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\left[\ii\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd\tau
  \left\{\int \dd^3x\sum_m\dot q_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)p_m(\mathbf{x},\tau)
  -H[q(\tau),p(\tau)]+\ii\epsilon\text{ terms}\right\}\right],
  \tag{9.2.17}\label{eq:9.2.17}
\end{align}
where the `$\ii\epsilon$ terms' just have the effect of putting the correct
$-\ii\epsilon$ in the denominators of all propagators.

This is a good place to mention that field-independent factors in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.17},
like the constant $|\mathcal{N}|^2$, are not important. This is because such
factors contribute also to the matrix element
$\OutBra{\Omega}\InKet{\Omega}$. In calculating the
\emph{connected} part of vacuum expectation values of time-ordered products
(or the $S$-matrix) we eliminate the contribution of disconnected vacuum
fluctuation subgraphs by dividing by
$\OutBra{\Omega}\InKet{\Omega}$, and any constant
factors in the vacuum expectation values cancel in this ratio.

We could go on and calculate matrix elements between multi-particle states,
by inserting the appropriate `wave functionals' in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.3}. These can be
calculated by applying the adjoints of annihilation operators such as \eqref{eq:9.2.7}
to the vacuum state; just as for the harmonic oscillator, these wave
functionals turn out to be Hermite polynomials in the field times the vacuum
Gaussian. We do not need to work all this out here, because as shown in
Section 6.4, the vacuum expectation values \eqref{eq:9.2.17} are all we need in order
to be able to calculate $S$-matrix elements.
