\subsection{The Dirac Formalism}

Among all the representations of the homogeneous Lorentz group, there is one
that plays a special role in physics. As we saw in Section 1.1, this
representation was introduced into the theory of the electron by Dirac~\hyperref[ref:5.3]{[3]},
but as so often happens it was already known to mathematicians~\hyperref[ref:5.4]{[4]}, because it
provides the basis of one of the two broad classes of representations of the
rotation or Lorentz groups (actually, of their covering groups---see Section
2.7) in any number of dimensions. From the point of view we are following
here, the structure and properties of any quantum field are dictated by the
representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group under which it transforms, so
it will be natural for us to describe the Dirac formalism as it first appeared
in mathematics, rather than as it was introduced by Dirac.

By a representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group, we mean a set of
matrices $D(\Lambda)$ satisfying the group multiplication law
\[
D(\bar\Lambda)D(\Lambda)=D(\bar\Lambda\Lambda).
\]
Just as for the unitary operators $U(\Lambda)$, we can study the properties of
these matrices by considering the infinitesimal case,
\begin{equation}
\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu=\delta^\mu{}_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu,
\tag{5.4.1}\label{eq:5.4.1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\omega_{\mu\nu}=-\omega_{\nu\mu},
\tag{5.4.2}\label{eq:5.4.2}
\end{equation}
for which
\begin{equation}
D(\Lambda)=\mathbf{1}+\frac{i}{2}\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{J}^{\mu\nu},
\tag{5.4.3}\label{eq:5.4.3}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{J}^{\mu\nu}=-\mathcal{J}^{\nu\mu}$ a set of matrices satisfying
the commutation relations \eqref{eq:2.4.12}:
\begin{equation}
i[\mathcal{J}^{\mu\nu},\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}]
=\eta^{\nu\rho}\mathcal{J}^{\mu\sigma}
-\eta^{\mu\rho}\mathcal{J}^{\nu\sigma}
-\eta^{\nu\sigma}\mathcal{J}^{\mu\rho}
+\eta^{\mu\sigma}\mathcal{J}^{\nu\rho}.
\tag{5.4.4}\label{eq:5.4.4}
\end{equation}
To find such a set of matrices, suppose we first construct matrices $\gamma^\mu$
that satisfy the anticommutation relations
\begin{equation}
\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}
\tag{5.4.5}\label{eq:5.4.5}
\end{equation}
and tentatively define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{i}{4}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu].
\tag{5.4.6}\label{eq:5.4.6}
\end{equation}
It is elementary, using Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.5}, to show that
\begin{equation}
[\mathcal{J}^{\mu\nu},\gamma^\rho]
=-i\gamma^\mu\eta^{\nu\rho}+i\gamma^\nu\eta^{\mu\rho}
\tag{5.4.7}\label{eq:5.4.7}
\end{equation}
and from this we easily see that Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.6} does indeed satisfy the desired
commutation relation Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.4}. We shall further assume that the matrices
$\gamma_\mu$ are \emph{irreducible}; that is, that there is no proper subspace
that is left invariant by all these matrices. Otherwise we could choose some
smaller set of field components, which would transform as in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.4.3} and
\eqref{eq:5.4.6}, with an irreducible set of $\gamma_\mu$s.

Any set of matrices satisfying a relation like Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.5} (or its Euclidean
analog, with $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ replaced with a Kronecker delta) is called a
\emph{Clifford algebra}. The importance in mathematics of this particular
representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group (or, more accurately, its
covering group) arises from the fact (shown in Section 5.6) that the most
general irreducible representation of the Lorentz group is either a tensor, or
a spinor transforming as in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.4.3} and \eqref{eq:5.4.6}, or a direct product of a
spinor and a tensor.

The commutation relation \eqref{eq:5.4.7} can be summarized by saying that $\gamma^\rho$
is a \emph{vector}, in the sense that Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.3} satisfies
\begin{equation}
D(\Lambda)\gamma^\rho D^{-1}(\Lambda)=\Lambda_\sigma{}^\rho\gamma^\sigma.
\tag{5.4.8}\label{eq:5.4.8}
\end{equation}
In the same sense, the unit matrix is trivially a \emph{scalar}
\begin{equation}
D(\Lambda)\mathbf{1}D^{-1}(\Lambda)=\mathbf{1}
\tag{5.4.9}\label{eq:5.4.9}
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.4} shows that $\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}$ is an antisymmetric
\emph{tensor}
\begin{equation}
D(\Lambda)\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}D^{-1}(\Lambda)
=\Lambda_\mu{}^\rho\Lambda_\nu{}^\sigma\mathcal{J}^{\mu\nu}.
\tag{5.4.10}\label{eq:5.4.10}
\end{equation}
The matrices $\gamma^\mu$ can be used to construct other totally
antisymmetric tensors
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}^{\rho\sigma\tau}\equiv\gamma^{[\rho}\gamma^\sigma\gamma^{\tau]},
\tag{5.4.11}\label{eq:5.4.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}^{\mu\rho\sigma\tau}\equiv
\gamma^{[\mu}\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma^{\tau]}.
\tag{5.4.12}\label{eq:5.4.12}
\end{equation}
The brackets here are a standard notation, indicating that we are to sum over
all permutations of the indices within the brackets, with a plus or minus sign
for even or odd permutations, respectively. For instance, Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.11} is
shorthand for
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{A}^{\rho\sigma\tau}\equiv{}&
\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\tau
-\gamma^\rho\gamma^\tau\gamma^\sigma
-\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\rho\gamma^\tau\\
&+\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\tau\gamma^\rho
+\gamma^\tau\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma
-\gamma^\tau\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\rho.
\end{align*}
By repeated use of Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.5} we can write any product of $\gamma$s as a sum
of antisymmetrized products of $\gamma$s times a product of metric tensors, so
the totally antisymmetric tensors form a complete basis for the set of all
matrices that can be constructed from the Dirac matrices.

This formalism automatically contains a parity transformation, conventionally
taken as
\begin{equation}
\beta\equiv i\gamma^0.
\tag{5.4.13}\label{eq:5.4.13}
\end{equation}
Applied to the Dirac matrices, this gives
\begin{equation}
\beta\gamma^i\beta^{-1}=-\gamma^i,
\qquad
\beta\gamma^0\beta^{-1}=+\gamma^0.
\tag{5.4.14}\label{eq:5.4.14}
\end{equation}
(We here label indices so that $\mu$ runs over values $0,1,2,\ldots$.) The same
similarity transformation, applied to any product of $\gamma$-matrices, then
yields just a plus or minus sign, according to whether the product contains an
even or an odd number of $\gamma$s with space-like indices, respectively. In
particular,
\begin{equation}
\beta\mathcal{J}^{ij}\beta^{-1}=\mathcal{J}^{ij},
\tag{5.4.15}\label{eq:5.4.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\beta\mathcal{J}^{i0}\beta^{-1}=-\mathcal{J}^{i0}.
\tag{5.4.16}\label{eq:5.4.16}
\end{equation}

Everything so far in this section applies in any number of spacetime
dimensions and for any `metric' $\eta_{\mu\nu}$. In four spacetime dimensions,
however, there is a special feature, that no totally antisymmetric tensor can
have more than four indices, so the sequence of tensors $\mathbf{1}$,
$\gamma^\rho$, $\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}$,
$\mathcal{A}^{\rho\sigma\tau},\ldots$ terminates with the tensor \eqref{eq:5.4.12}.
Furthermore, each of these tensors transforms differently under Lorentz and/or
parity transformations so they are all linearly independent.\footnote{Alternatively,
these matrices can be shown to be linearly independent by noting that they
form an orthogonal set, with the scalar product of two matrices defined by the
trace of their product. Note that none of these matrices can vanish, because
each component of each of these tensors is proportional to a product of
different $\gamma$-matrices, and such a product has a square equal to plus or
minus the product of the corresponding squares, and hence equal to
$\mathbin{\pm}\mathbf{1}$.} The number of linearly independent components of
these tensors is one for $\mathbf{1}$, four for $\gamma^\rho$, six for
$\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}$, four for $\mathcal{A}^{\rho\sigma\tau}$, and one
for $\mathcal{P}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$, or 16 independent components in all.
(The general rule is that a totally antisymmetric tensor with $n$ indices in
$d$ dimensions has a number of independent components equal to the binomial
coefficient $d!/n!(d-n)!$.) There are at most $v^2$ independent $v\cdot v$
matrices, so they must have at least $\sqrt{16}=4$ rows and columns. Dirac
matrices of the minimum dimensionality are necessarily irreducible; if
reducible, the subspace left invariant by these matrices would furnish a
representation of lower dimensionality. We shall therefore take the
$\gamma^\mu$ to be $4\times4$ matrices.

(More generally, in any even number $d$ of spacetime dimensions, one can form
antisymmetric tensors with $0,1,\ldots,d$ indices, which contain altogether a
number of independent components equal to
\[
\sum_{n=0}^{d}\frac{d!}{n!(d-n)!}=2^d,
\]
so the $\gamma$-matrices must have at least $2^{d/2}$ rows and columns. In
spaces or spacetimes with odd dimensionality, the totally antisymmetric
tensors of rank $n$ and $d-n$ can be linearly related by the
conditions\footnote{This constraint does not interfere with the inclusion of
space inversion in the Dirac representation of the Lorentz group in
odd-dimensional spacetime, because here the tensor
$\epsilon^{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_d}$ is even under inversion of space
coordinates. If we don't care about space inversion, we can also construct
$2^{(d-2)/2}$-dimensional irreducible representations of the proper
orthochronous Lorentz group in even spacetime dimensions by imposing the above
condition relating antisymmetrized products of $r$ and $d-r$ Dirac matrices.
An example is provided by the submatrices in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.4.19} and \eqref{eq:5.4.20}
below.}
\[
\gamma^{[\mu_1}\gamma^{\mu_2}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_r]}
\propto\epsilon^{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_d}
\gamma_{[\mu_{r+1}}\gamma_{\mu_{r+2}}\cdots\gamma_{\mu_d]},
\]
for $r=0,1,2,\ldots,d-1$, with $\epsilon^{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_d}$ totally
antisymmetric, and the left-hand side taken as the unit matrix for $r=0$.
Under these conditions there are only $2^{d-1}$ independent tensors,
requiring $\gamma$-matrices of dimensionality at least $2^{(d-1)/2}$.)

Returning now to four spacetime dimensions, we shall choose an explicit set of
$4\times4$ $\gamma$-matrices. One very convenient choice is
\begin{equation}
\gamma^0=-i\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{1}\\ \mathbf{1}&0\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=-i\begin{bmatrix}0&\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
-\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0\end{bmatrix},
\tag{5.4.17}\label{eq:5.4.17}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}$ is the unit $2\times2$ matrix, and the components of
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ are the usual Pauli matrices
\begin{equation}
\sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{5.4.18}\label{eq:5.4.18}
\end{equation}
(The $\sigma_i$ are just the $2\times2$ $\gamma$-matrices in three
dimensions.) It can be shown~\hyperref[ref:5.5]{[5]} that any other irreducible set of
$\gamma$-matrices are related to these by a similarity transformation. From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.17}, we can easily calculate the Lorentz group generators \eqref{eq:5.4.6}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}^{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}
\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_k&0\\0&\sigma_k\end{bmatrix},
\tag{5.4.19}\label{eq:5.4.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}^{i0}=+\frac{i}{2}
\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_i&0\\0&-\sigma_i\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{5.4.20}\label{eq:5.4.20}
\end{equation}
(Here $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the totally antisymmetric tensor in three dimensions,
with $\epsilon_{123}=+1$.) We note that these are block-diagonal, so the Dirac
matrices provide a \emph{reducible} representation of the proper orthochronous
Lorentz group, the direct sum of two irreducible representation with
$\mathcal{J}^{ij}=\mathbin{\pm}i\epsilon_{ijk}\mathcal{J}^{k0}$.

It is convenient to write the totally antisymmetric tensors \eqref{eq:5.4.11} and
\eqref{eq:5.4.12} in a somewhat simpler way. The matrix \eqref{eq:5.4.12} is totally
antisymmetric, and therefore proportional to the pseudotensor
$\epsilon^{\rho\sigma\tau\eta}$, defined as a totally antisymmetric quantity
with $\epsilon^{0123}=+1$. Setting $\rho,\sigma,\tau,\eta$ equal to $0,1,2,3$,
respectively, we see that
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}^{\rho\sigma\tau\eta}=4!i\epsilon^{\rho\sigma\tau\eta}\gamma^5,
\tag{5.4.21}\label{eq:5.4.21}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\gamma^5\equiv-i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3.
\tag{5.4.22}\label{eq:5.4.22}
\end{equation}
The matrix $\gamma^5$ is a pseudoscalar in the sense that
\begin{equation}
[\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma},\gamma^5]=0,
\tag{5.4.23}\label{eq:5.4.23}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\beta\gamma^5\beta^{-1}=-\gamma^5.
\tag{5.4.24}\label{eq:5.4.24}
\end{equation}
Similarly, $\mathcal{A}^{\rho\sigma\tau}$ must be proportional to
$\epsilon^{\rho\sigma\tau\eta}$ contracted with some matrix $\mathcal{A}_\eta$,
and by setting $\rho,\sigma,\tau$ equal in turn to $0,1,2$ or $0,1,3$ or
$0,2,3$ or $1,2,3$, we find
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}^{\rho\sigma\tau}=3!i\epsilon^{\rho\sigma\tau\eta}\gamma^5\gamma_\eta.
\tag{5.4.25}\label{eq:5.4.25}
\end{equation}
The 16 independent $4\times4$ matrices can therefore be taken as the
components of the scalar $\mathbf{1}$, the vector $\gamma^\rho$, the
antisymmetric tensor $\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}$, the `axial' vector
$\gamma^5\gamma_\eta$, and the pseudoscalar $\gamma^5$. It is easy to see that
the matrix $\gamma^5$ has unit square
\begin{equation}
(\gamma^5)^2=\mathbf{1}
\tag{5.4.26}\label{eq:5.4.26}
\end{equation}
and anticommutes with all $\gamma^\mu$
\begin{equation}
\{\gamma^5,\gamma^\mu\}=0.
\tag{5.4.27}\label{eq:5.4.27}
\end{equation}
The notation $\gamma^5$ is particularly appropriate, because the
anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:5.4.26} and \eqref{eq:5.4.27}, together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.5}
show that $\gamma^0,\gamma^1,\gamma^2,\gamma^3,\gamma^5$ provide a Clifford
algebra in five spacetime dimensions. For the particular $4\times4$
representation \eqref{eq:5.4.17} of the $\gamma$-matrices, the matrix $\gamma^5$ is
\begin{equation}
\gamma^5=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}&0\\0&-\mathbf{1}\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{5.4.28}\label{eq:5.4.28}
\end{equation}
This representation is convenient because it reduces $\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}$
and $\gamma^5$ to block-diagonal form. As we shall see, this makes it
particularly useful for dealing with particles in the ultra-relativistic
limit, $v\to c$. (It is not, however, the representation described in Section
1.1 that was originally introduced by Dirac, because Dirac was mostly
interested in electrons in atoms where $v\ll c$, and in this case it is more
convenient to adopt a representation for which $\gamma^0$ rather than
$\gamma^5$ is diagonal.)

The representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group we have constructed here
is not unitary, because the generators $\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}$ are not all
represented by Hermitian matrices. In particular, in the representation
\eqref{eq:5.4.17} we have $\mathcal{J}^{ij}$ Hermitian, but $\mathcal{J}^{i0}$ is
anti-Hermitian. Such reality conditions can conveniently be written in a
manifestly Lorentz-invariant fashion by introducing the matrix
$\beta=i\gamma^0$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.13}, which in the representation \eqref{eq:5.4.17} takes
the form
\begin{equation}
\beta=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{1}\\\mathbf{1}&0\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{5.4.29}\label{eq:5.4.29}
\end{equation}
Inspection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.17} shows that
\begin{equation}
\beta\gamma^{\mu\dagger}\beta=-\gamma^\mu
\tag{5.4.30}\label{eq:5.4.30}
\end{equation}
and it follows then that
\begin{equation}
\beta\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma\dagger}\beta=\mathcal{J}^{\rho\sigma}.
\tag{5.4.31}\label{eq:5.4.31}
\end{equation}
Hence, though not unitary, the matrices $D(\Lambda)$ satisfy the
pseudounitarity relation
\begin{equation}
\beta D(\Lambda)^\dagger\beta=D(\Lambda)^{-1}.
\tag{5.4.32}\label{eq:5.4.32}
\end{equation}
Also, $\gamma^5$ is Hermitian and anticommutes with $\beta$ so
\begin{equation}
\beta(\gamma^5)^\dagger\beta=-\gamma^5
\tag{5.4.33}\label{eq:5.4.33}
\end{equation}
and it follows that
\begin{equation}
\beta(\gamma^5\gamma_\mu)^\dagger\beta=-\gamma^5\gamma_\mu.
\tag{5.4.34}\label{eq:5.4.34}
\end{equation}

The Dirac and related matrices also have important symmetry properties.
Inspection of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.4.17} and \eqref{eq:5.4.18} shows that $\gamma_\mu$ is symmetric
for $\mu=0,2$ and antisymmetric for $\mu=1,3$, so
\begin{equation}
\gamma_\mu^{\mathrm T}=-\mathcal{C}\gamma_\mu\mathcal{C}^{-1},
\tag{5.4.35}\label{eq:5.4.35}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm T$ denotes a transpose, and
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}\equiv\gamma_2\beta=-i
\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_2&0\\0&-\sigma_2\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{5.4.36}\label{eq:5.4.36}
\end{equation}
It follows immediately that
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm T}=-\mathcal{C}\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{C}^{-1},
\tag{5.4.37}\label{eq:5.4.37}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\gamma^5)^{\mathrm T}=+\mathcal{C}\gamma^5\mathcal{C}^{-1},
\tag{5.4.38}\label{eq:5.4.38}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\gamma^5\gamma_\mu)^{\mathrm T}
=+\mathcal{C}\gamma^5\gamma_\mu\mathcal{C}^{-1}.
\tag{5.4.39}\label{eq:5.4.39}
\end{equation}
These signs will prove significant when we consider the charge-conjugation
properties of various currents in the next section. Of course, we can combine
our results for adjoints and transposes to obtain the complex conjugates of the
Dirac and allied matrices:
\begin{equation}
\gamma_\mu^*=\beta\mathcal{C}\gamma_\mu\mathcal{C}^{-1}\beta,
\tag{5.4.40}\label{eq:5.4.40}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}^*=-\beta\mathcal{C}\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{C}^{-1}\beta,
\tag{5.4.41}\label{eq:5.4.41}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\gamma^5)^*=-\beta\mathcal{C}\gamma^5\mathcal{C}^{-1}\beta,
\tag{5.4.42}\label{eq:5.4.42}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\gamma^5\gamma_\mu)^*=-\beta\mathcal{C}\gamma^5\gamma_\mu\mathcal{C}^{-1}\beta.
\tag{5.4.43}\label{eq:5.4.43}
\end{equation}
