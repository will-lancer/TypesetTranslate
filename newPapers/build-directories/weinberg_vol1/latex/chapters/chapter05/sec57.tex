\subsection[General Causal Fields]{General Causal Fields\protect\footnotemark}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}

We now proceed to construct causal fields that transform according to the
general irreducible $(A,B)$ representations described in the previous section.
The index $\ell$ is replaced here with a pair of indices $a,b$, running over
the ranges \eqref{eq:5.6.12}, \eqref{eq:5.6.13}, so the fields are now written as
\begin{align}
\psi_{ab}(x)={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
\bigl[\kappa a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}u_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\notag\\
&\hspace{45mm}+\lambda a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}
v_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)\bigr].
\tag{5.7.1}\label{eq:5.7.1}
\end{align}
with $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ arbitrary constants. We are here leaving open the
possibility that this particle is its own antiparticle, in which case
$a^c_{\mathbf p,\sigma}=a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}$.

Our first task is to find the zero-momentum coefficient functions
$u_{ab}(0,\sigma)$ and $v_{ab}(0,\sigma)$. The fundamental conditions
\eqref{eq:5.1.25}--\eqref{eq:5.1.26} on $u(0,\sigma)$ and $v(0,\sigma)$ read here
\[
\sum_{\bar\sigma}u_{\bar a\bar b}(0,\bar\sigma)
J^{(j)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
=\sum_{a,b}\mathcal J_{\bar a\bar b,ab}u_{ab}(0,\sigma),
\]
\[
-\sum_{\bar\sigma}v_{\bar a\bar b}(0,\bar\sigma)
J^{(j)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
=\sum_{a,b}\mathcal J_{\bar a\bar b,ab}v_{ab}(0,\sigma),
\]
or using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.6.14}--\eqref{eq:5.6.15}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\bar\sigma}u_{\bar a\bar b}(0,\bar\sigma)
J^{(j)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
=\sum_a\mathbf J^{(A)}_{\bar a a}u_{a\bar b}(0,\sigma)
+\sum_b\mathbf J^{(B)}_{\bar b b}u_{\bar a b}(0,\sigma),
\tag{5.7.2}\label{eq:5.7.2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
-\sum_{\bar\sigma}v_{\bar a\bar b}(0,\bar\sigma)
J^{(j)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
=\sum_a\mathbf J^{(A)}_{\bar a a}v_{a\bar b}(0,\sigma)
+\sum_b\mathbf J^{(B)}_{\bar b b}v_{\bar a b}(0,\sigma).
\tag{5.7.3}\label{eq:5.7.3}
\end{equation}
But Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.2} is the defining condition for the Clebsch--Gordan
coefficients $C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)$! These coefficients are defined by the
requirement that if $\Psi_{ab}$ are states that under an infinitesimal rotation
transform as
\[
\delta\Psi_{\bar a\bar b}=i\sum_a\boldsymbol\theta\cdot
\mathbf J^{(A)}_{\bar a a}\Psi_{a\bar b}
+i\sum_b\boldsymbol\theta\cdot
\mathbf J^{(B)}_{\bar b b}\Psi_{\bar a b},
\]
then, under the same rotation, the state
\[
\Psi^j_\sigma\equiv\sum_{ab}C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)\Psi_{ab}
\]
transforms as
\[
\delta\Psi^j_\sigma=i\sum_{\bar\sigma}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot
\mathbf J^{(j)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}\Psi^j_{\bar\sigma}.
\]
Inspection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.2} shows that this requirement is satisfied by the
coefficients $u_{ab}(0,\sigma)$, and therefore, up to a possible
proportionality factor, $u_{ab}(0,\sigma)$ is just $C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)$. This
constant is conventionally chosen so that
\begin{equation}
u_{ab}(0,\sigma)=(2m)^{-1/2}C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab).
\tag{5.7.4}\label{eq:5.7.4}
\end{equation}
This result is unique because each irreducible $(A,B)$ representation of the
homogeneous Lorentz group contains a given spin $j$ representation of the
rotation group at most \emph{once}. Similarly, inspection of Eqs.
\eqref{eq:5.6.16}--\eqref{eq:5.6.17} shows that the complex conjugates of the angular momentum
matrices are
\begin{equation}
-J^{(j)*}_{\sigma\sigma'}=(-1)^{\sigma-\sigma'}J^{(j)}_{-\sigma,-\sigma'}.
\tag{5.7.5}\label{eq:5.7.5}
\end{equation}
Therefore if we write Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.3} in terms of
$(-1)^{j-\sigma}v_{ab}(\mathbf p,-\sigma)$, it takes the same form as Eq.
\eqref{eq:5.7.2}. With a suitable adjustment of a constant factor, the unique solution
for $v(0,\sigma)$ is
\begin{equation}
v_{ab}(0,\sigma)=(-1)^{j+\sigma}u_{ab}(0,-\sigma).
\tag{5.7.6}\label{eq:5.7.6}
\end{equation}

We must now perform a boost to calculate the coefficient functions for finite
momentum. For a fixed direction $\widehat{\mathbf p}\equiv
\mathbf p/|\mathbf p|$, we can write the boost \eqref{eq:2.5.24} as a function of a
parameter $\theta$ defined by
\begin{equation}
\cosh\theta=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}/m,
\qquad \sinh\theta=|\mathbf p|/m
\tag{5.7.7}\label{eq:5.7.7}
\end{equation}
and write $L^\mu{}_\nu(\theta)$ in place of $L^\mu{}_\nu(p)$, where
\begin{align}
L^i{}_k(\theta)&=\delta_{ik}+(\cosh\theta-1)\widehat p_i\widehat p_k,
\notag\\
L^i{}_0(\theta)=L^0{}_i(\theta)&=\widehat p_i\sinh\theta,
\tag{5.7.8}\label{eq:5.7.8}\\
L^0{}_0(\theta)&=\cosh\theta.
\notag
\end{align}
The advantage of this parameterization is that
\begin{equation}
L(\bar\theta)L(\theta)=L(\bar\theta+\theta).
\tag{5.7.9}\label{eq:5.7.9}
\end{equation}
For infinitesimal $\theta$, we have
$[L(\theta)]^\mu{}_\nu\to\delta^\mu{}_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu$, where
$\omega^i{}_0=\omega^0{}_i=\widehat p_i\theta$ and
$\omega^i{}_j=\omega^0{}_0=0$. Following the same reasoning that led from Eq.
\eqref{eq:2.2.24} to Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.26}, it follows then that
\begin{equation}
D(L(p))=\exp(-i\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal K}\theta).
\tag{5.7.10}\label{eq:5.7.10}
\end{equation}
This is for any representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group; for the
irreducible $(A,B)$ representations, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.6.7} and \eqref{eq:5.6.8} give
\begin{equation}
i\boldsymbol{\mathcal K}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal A}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal B}
\tag{5.7.11}\label{eq:5.7.11}
\end{equation}
and since $\boldsymbol{\mathcal A}$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal B}$ are commuting
matrices
\begin{equation}
D(L(p))=\exp(-\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal A}\theta)
\exp(+\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal B}\theta).
\tag{5.7.12}\label{eq:5.7.12}
\end{equation}
In more detail, using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.6.14} and \eqref{eq:5.6.15}
\begin{equation}
D(L(p))_{a'b',ab}=
\bigl(\exp(-\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(A)}\theta)\bigr)_{a'a}
\bigl(\exp(+\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(B)}\theta)\bigr)_{b'b}.
\tag{5.7.13}\label{eq:5.7.13}
\end{equation}
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.7.4} and \eqref{eq:5.7.6} then give the coefficient functions at finite
momentum as
\begin{align}
u_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)={}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2p^0}}
\sum_{a'b'}
\bigl(\exp(-\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(A)}\theta)\bigr)_{aa'}
\bigl(\exp(+\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(B)}\theta)\bigr)_{bb'}
\notag\\
&\cdot C_{AB}(j\sigma;a'b')
\tag{5.7.14}\label{eq:5.7.14}
\end{align}
and
\begin{equation}
v_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)=(-1)^{j+\sigma}u_{ab}(\mathbf p,-\sigma).
\tag{5.7.15}\label{eq:5.7.15}
\end{equation}
These results give the field explicitly for a given transformation type
$(A,B)$, so the field \eqref{eq:5.1.31} of this type is unique up to the choice of the
constant factors $\kappa$ and $\lambda$.

It is very easy in this formalism to construct Lorentz scalar interaction
densities. The $(A,B)$ representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group is just
the direct product of the $(A,0)$ and $(0,B)$ representations, so the general
Lorentz transformation rules \eqref{eq:5.1.6}, \eqref{eq:5.1.7} read here
\begin{equation}
U_0(\Lambda)\psi_{ab}(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda)=
\sum_{a'b'}D^{A0}_{aa'}(\Lambda^{-1})D^{0B}_{bb'}(\Lambda^{-1})
\psi_{a'b'}(\Lambda x).
\tag{5.7.16}\label{eq:5.7.16}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.6.14} and \eqref{eq:5.6.15} show that the matrix generators of the
$(A,0)$ and $(0,B)$ representations are just the spin matrices for spin $A$
and $B$, respectively. Thus we can construct scalars of the form
\begin{equation}
\sum_{a_1a_2\cdots a_n}\sum_{b_1b_2\cdots b_n}
g_{a_1a_2\cdots a_n;b_1b_2\cdots b_n}
\psi^{(1)}_{a_1b_1}(x)\psi^{(2)}_{a_2b_2}(x)\cdots\psi^{(n)}_{a_nb_n}(x)
\tag{5.7.17}\label{eq:5.7.17}
\end{equation}
by simply taking $g_{a_1a_2\cdots a_n;b_1b_2\cdots b_n}$ as the product of a
coefficient for coupling spins $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n$ to make a scalar and a
coefficient for coupling spins $B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_n$ to make a scalar. (Even
though we do not explicitly consider interactions involving derivatives, we
will in this way obtain the most general interaction involving $n$ fields,
because the derivative of a field of type $(A,B)$ can always be decomposed into
fields of other types without derivatives.) For instance, the most general
Lorentz scalar formed from a product of three fields of transformation types
$(A_1,B_1)$, $(A_2,B_2)$, and $(A_3,B_3)$ is
\begin{equation}
g\sum_{a_1a_2a_3}\sum_{b_1b_2b_3}
\begin{pmatrix}A_1&A_2&A_3\\a_1&a_2&a_3\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}B_1&B_2&B_3\\b_1&b_2&b_3\end{pmatrix}
\psi^{(1)}_{a_1b_1}\psi^{(2)}_{a_2b_2}\psi^{(3)}_{a_3b_3}.
\tag{5.7.18}\label{eq:5.7.18}
\end{equation}
with a single free parameter $g$. This is the most general three-field
interaction. (The brackets in \eqref{eq:5.7.17} denote the Wigner ``three-$j$''
symbols~\hyperref[ref:5.10]{[10]}
\[
\begin{pmatrix}j_1&j_2&j_3\\m_1&m_2&m_3\end{pmatrix}
\equiv\sum_{m'_3}C_{j_1j_2}(j_3m'_3;m_1m_2)C_{j_3j_3}(00;m'_3m_3)
\]
which describe the coupling of three spins to make a rotational scalar.)

For the $S$-matrix to be Lorentz-invariant it is not enough that the
interaction density $\mathcal H_I(x)$ be a scalar like \eqref{eq:5.7.18}; it is also
necessary that $\mathcal H_I(x)$ should commute with $\mathcal H_I(y)$ at
space-like separations $x-y$. To see how to satisfy this condition, consider
the commutator or anticommutator of two fields for the same particle species,
a field $\psi$ of type $(A,B)$, and the adjoint $\widetilde\psi^\dagger$ of a
field $\widetilde\psi$ of type $(\widetilde A,\widetilde B)$. We find
\begin{align}
[\psi_{ab}(x),\widetilde\psi^\dagger_{\tilde a\tilde b}(y)]_\mp
={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int d^3p\,(2p^0)^{-1}
\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl[\kappa\widetilde\kappa^*e^{ip\cdot(x-y)}
\mp\lambda\widetilde\lambda^*e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}\bigr],
\tag{5.7.19}\label{eq:5.7.19}
\end{align}
where $\pi(\mathbf p)$ is the spin sum
\begin{equation}
(2p^0)^{-1}\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)
=\sum_\sigma u_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\widetilde u^*_{\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
=\sum_\sigma v_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\widetilde v^*_{\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\tag{5.7.20}\label{eq:5.7.20}
\end{equation}
and as usual, the top and bottom signs are for bosons and fermions,
respectively. (We allow here for different coefficients $\widetilde\kappa$ and
$\widetilde\lambda$ in the $\widetilde\psi$ field.) In more detail
\begin{align}
\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)={}&
\sum_{a'b'}\sum_{\tilde a'\tilde b'}\sum_\sigma
C_{AB}(j\sigma;a'b')C_{\widetilde A\widetilde B}
(j\sigma;\tilde a'\tilde b')
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl(\exp(-\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(A)}\theta)\bigr)_{aa'}
\bigl(\exp(+\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(B)}\theta)\bigr)_{bb'}
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl(\exp(-\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(\widetilde A)}\theta)\bigr)^*_{\tilde a\tilde a'}
\bigl(\exp(+\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(\widetilde B)}\theta)\bigr)^*_{\tilde b\tilde b'}.
\tag{5.7.21}\label{eq:5.7.21}
\end{align}
The function $\pi(\mathbf p)$ has been calculated explicitly~\hyperref[ref:5.11]{[11]}. What
concerns us here is the fact that it turns out to be the mass-shell value of a
\emph{polynomial} function $P$ of $\mathbf p$ and $p^0$:
\begin{equation}
\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)=
P_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p,\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2})
\tag{5.7.22}\label{eq:5.7.22}
\end{equation}
and that $P$ is even or odd according to whether
$2A+2\widetilde B$ is an even or odd integer
\begin{equation}
P(-\mathbf p,-p^0)=(-1)^{2A+2\widetilde B}P(\mathbf p,p^0).
\tag{5.7.23}\label{eq:5.7.23}
\end{equation}
We shall check this here for just one particular direction of $\mathbf p$.
Taking $\mathbf p$ in the three-direction, \eqref{eq:5.7.21} gives
\[
\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)=\sum_\sigma
C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)C_{\widetilde A\widetilde B}
(j\sigma;\tilde a\tilde b)
\exp\bigl([-a+b-\tilde a+\tilde b]\theta\bigr).
\]
The Clebsch--Gordan coefficients vanish unless $\sigma=a+b$ and
$\sigma=\tilde a+\tilde b$, so we can replace
\[
-a+b-\tilde a+\tilde b=-2a+\sigma+2\tilde b-\sigma=2\tilde b-2a.
\]
We can write $\exp(\pm\theta)$ as $(p^0\pm p^3)/m$, so here
\[
\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)=\sum_\sigma
C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)C_{\widetilde A\widetilde B}
(j\sigma;\tilde a\tilde b)
\begin{cases}
[(p^0+p^3)/m]^{2\tilde b-2a},&\tilde b\geq a,\\
[(p^0-p^3)/m]^{2a-2\tilde b},&a\geq\tilde b.
\end{cases}
\]
where $p^0\equiv\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$. We see that $\pi(\mathbf p)$ can
indeed be written as the mass-shell value of a polynomial $P(\mathbf p,p^0)$.
Also, $2\tilde b-2a$ equals $2\widetilde B+2A$ minus an even integer, so the
polynomial satisfies the reflection condition \eqref{eq:5.7.23}.

Any polynomial in $\mathbf p$ and $\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$ can be written in a
form \emph{linear} in $\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$ (by expressing even powers of
$\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$ in terms of $\mathbf p$) so $\pi(\mathbf p)$ can be
written
\begin{equation}
\pi_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)=P_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p)
+2\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}\,Q_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(\mathbf p),
\tag{5.7.24}\label{eq:5.7.24}
\end{equation}
where $P$ and $Q$ are now polynomials in $\mathbf p$ alone, with
\begin{align}
P(-\mathbf p)&=(-1)^{2A+2\widetilde B}P(\mathbf p),
\tag{5.7.25}\label{eq:5.7.25}\\
Q(-\mathbf p)&=-(-1)^{2A+2\widetilde B}Q(\mathbf p).
\tag{5.7.26}\label{eq:5.7.26}
\end{align}
For $x-y$ space-like, we can adopt a Lorentz frame in which $x^0=y^0$, and
write Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.19} as
\begin{align*}
[\psi_{ab}(x),\widetilde\psi^\dagger_{\tilde a\tilde b}(y)]_\mp
={}&[\kappa\widetilde\kappa^*\mp
(-1)^{2A+2\widetilde B}\lambda\widetilde\lambda^*]
P_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(-i\boldsymbol\nabla)\Delta_+(\mathbf x-\mathbf y,0)
\\
&+[\kappa\widetilde\kappa^*\pm
(-1)^{2A+2\widetilde B}\lambda\widetilde\lambda^*]
Q_{ab,\tilde a\tilde b}(-i\boldsymbol\nabla)\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y).
\end{align*}
In order that this should vanish when $\mathbf x\neq\mathbf y$, we must have
\begin{equation}
\kappa\widetilde\kappa^*=\pm(-1)^{2A+2\widetilde B}
\lambda\widetilde\lambda^*.
\tag{5.7.27}\label{eq:5.7.27}
\end{equation}

Now let us consider the special case where $\psi$ and $\widetilde\psi$ are the
same, so in particular $A=\widetilde A$ and $B=\widetilde B$. (It is
unavoidable that such commutators or anticommutators will appear in
$[\mathcal H_I(x),\mathcal H_I(y)]$, because the hermiticity of the Hamiltonian
requires that if $\mathcal H_I(x)$ involves $\psi$, it also involves
$\psi^\dagger$.) In this case, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.7.27} gives
\[
|\kappa|^2=\pm(-1)^{2A+2B}|\lambda|^2.
\]
This is possible if and only if
\begin{equation}
\pm(-1)^{2A+2B}=+1
\tag{5.7.28}\label{eq:5.7.28}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
|\kappa|^2=|\lambda|^2.
\tag{5.7.29}\label{eq:5.7.29}
\end{equation}
Of course, $2A+2B$ differs from $2j$ by an \emph{even} integer, so Eq.
\eqref{eq:5.7.28} says that \emph{our particle is a boson or fermion according to
whether $2j$ is even or odd}. This is the general relation between spin and
statistics~\hyperref[ref:5.12]{[12]}, of which we have already seen special examples for particles
described by scalar, vector, or Dirac fields.

Now let's return to the general case, where the fields $\psi$ and
$\widetilde\psi$ may be different. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.27}, and dividing both sides
by $|\widetilde\kappa|^2=|\widetilde\lambda|^2$, we have
\[
\frac{\kappa}{\widetilde\kappa}=(-1)^{2B+2\widetilde B}
\frac{\lambda}{\widetilde\lambda}.
\]
It follows that, for any field,
\begin{equation}
\lambda=(-1)^{2B}c\,\kappa,
\tag{5.7.30}\label{eq:5.7.30}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the same factor for all fields of a given particle. Furthermore,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.29} shows that $c$ is just a phase, $|c|=1$. We can therefore
eliminate $c$ for all fields by a redefinition of the relative phase of the
operators $a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}$ and $a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,\sigma}$, so that
$c=1$, and hence $\lambda=(-1)^{2B}\kappa$. Also, the factor $\kappa$ for each
field type may be eliminated by a redefinition of the over-all scale of the
field. We emerge from all this with a formula for the $(A,B)$ field of a given
particle, that is unique up to overall scale
\begin{align}
\psi_{ab}(x)={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
\bigl[u_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}
\notag\\
&\hspace{33mm}+(-1)^{2B}u_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,-\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr].
\tag{5.7.31}\label{eq:5.7.31}
\end{align}

The different fields for a given particle do not really represent
possibilities that are physically distinct. For instance, the possible fields
for $j=0$ are those of type $(A,A)$ (because the triangle inequality
$|A-B|\leq j\leq A+B$ here requires $A=B$). Starting with a $(0,0)$ scalar
field $\phi$, we can easily construct such $(A,A)$ fields from the $2A$th
derivative
\begin{equation}
\{\partial_{\mu_1}\cdots\partial_{\mu_{2A}}\}\phi,
\tag{5.7.32}\label{eq:5.7.32}
\end{equation}
where $\{\}$ here denotes the traceless part; for instance
\[
\{\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\}\equiv
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^\mu\partial x^\nu}
-\frac14\eta_{\mu\nu}\Box.
\]
(Recall that a traceless symmetric tensor of rank $N$ transforms according to
the $(N/2,N/2)$ representation.) But Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.31} represents the \emph{unique}
causal $(A,B)$ field for a given particle of spin $j$, so the $(A,A)$ fields
\eqref{eq:5.7.31} for $j=0$ can be nothing but linear combinations of the $2A$th
derivatives \eqref{eq:5.7.32} of a scalar field.

More generally, \emph{any} field $(A,B)$ for a given particle of spin $j$ can
be expressed as a differential operator of rank $2B$ acting on the
field\footnote{The only possible flaw in this argument would be if some of the
$(A,B)$ fields obtained in this way actually vanished. But in this case, the
$(j,0)$ field $\phi_\sigma$ would satisfy a field equation
$\sum_{\bar\sigma}M_{\bar\sigma}(i\partial/\partial x)
\phi_{\bar\sigma}(x)=0$ and hence, for each $\sigma$,
$\sum_{\bar\sigma}M_{\bar\sigma}(ip)u_{\bar\sigma}(\mathbf p,\sigma)=0$.
For the
$(j,0)$ representation the Clebsch--Gordan coefficient
$C_{j0}(j\sigma;\bar\sigma0)$ is just the Kronecker symbol
$\delta_{\bar\sigma\sigma}$, so this would require
$\sum_{\bar\sigma}M_{\bar\sigma}(ip)D_{\bar\sigma\sigma}(L(p))=0$,
which is impossible unless all the $M_\sigma(ip)$ vanish, since $D(\Lambda)$
has an inverse $D(\Lambda^{-1})$. The $(j,0)$ fields $\phi_\sigma(x)$ thus
satisfy no field equation other than the Klein--Gordon equation
$(\Box-m^2)\phi_\sigma(x)=0$, and therefore none of the $(A,B)$ fields obtained
from \eqref{eq:5.7.33} can vanish.} $\phi_\sigma(x)$ of type $(j,0)$ (or a differential
operator of rank $2A$ acting on the field of type $(0,j)$). To see this,
consider the field
\begin{equation}
\{\partial_{\mu_1}\cdots\partial_{\mu_{2B}}\}\phi_\sigma.
\tag{5.7.33}\label{eq:5.7.33}
\end{equation}
This transforms as the direct product of the representations $(B,B)$ and
$(j,0)$, and hence by the usual rules of vector addition, it can be decomposed
into fields transforming according to all the irreducible representations
$(A,B)$ with $|j-B|\leq A\leq j+B$, or equivalently
$|A-B|\leq j\leq A+B$. Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.31} represents the unique field of type
$(A,B)$ for a given particle of spin $j$, it can be nothing but the $(A,B)$
field obtained from the derivatives \eqref{eq:5.7.33}.

Now let us consider the behavior of these fields under inversions, beginning
with space inversion. Using the results of Section 4.2, the space-inversion
properties of the particle annihilation and antiparticle creation operators
are
\begin{align}
P a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}P^{-1}&=\eta^*a_{-\mathbf p,\sigma},
\tag{5.7.34}\label{eq:5.7.34}\\
P a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,\sigma}P^{-1}&=\eta^c
a^{c\dagger}_{-\mathbf p,\sigma},
\tag{5.7.35}\label{eq:5.7.35}
\end{align}
where $\eta$ and $\eta^c$ are the intrinsic parities for the particle and
antiparticle, respectively. The general causal $(A,B)$ field \eqref{eq:5.7.31} thus
transforms under the parity operator $P$ into
\begin{align}
P\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)P^{-1}={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
\bigl[\eta^*a_{-\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}u^{AB}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\notag\\
&\hspace{22mm}+\eta^c(-1)^{2B}a^{c\dagger}_{-\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}
v^{AB}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)\bigr].
\tag{5.7.36}\label{eq:5.7.36}
\end{align}
We want to change the integration variable from $\mathbf p$ to $-\mathbf p$,
and for this purpose we will need to evaluate $u_{ab}(-\mathbf p,\sigma)$ and
$v_{ab}(-\mathbf p,\sigma)$. To do this, we need only glance back at \eqref{eq:5.7.14}
and \eqref{eq:5.7.15}, and use the symmetry property of the Clebsch--Gordan
coefficient~\hyperref[ref:5.14]{[14]}
\begin{equation}
C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)=(-1)^{A+B-j}C_{BA}(j\sigma;ba).
\tag{5.7.37}\label{eq:5.7.37}
\end{equation}
This gives
\begin{align}
u^{AB}_{ab}(-\mathbf p,\sigma)&=(-1)^{A+B-j}u^{BA}_{ba}(\mathbf p,\sigma),
\tag{5.7.38}\label{eq:5.7.38}\\
v^{AB}_{ab}(-\mathbf p,\sigma)&=(-1)^{A+B-j}v^{BA}_{ba}(\mathbf p,\sigma),
\tag{5.7.39}\label{eq:5.7.39}
\end{align}
so
\begin{align}
P\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)P^{-1}={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
(-1)^{A+B-j}
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl[\eta^*a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot\mathcal P x}
u^{BA}_{ba}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\notag\\
&\hspace{16mm}+\eta^c(-1)^{2B}a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,\sigma}
e^{-ip\cdot\mathcal P x}v^{BA}_{ba}(\mathbf p,\sigma)\bigr],
\tag{5.7.40}\label{eq:5.7.40}
\end{align}
where, as before, $\mathcal P x\equiv(-\mathbf x,x^0)$. This is the causal
field $\psi^{BA}_{ba}$ evaluated at $\mathcal P x$, except that the coefficients
of the annihilation and creation terms may not be the same as called for in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.31}. But these coefficients \emph{must} be the same up to an overall
constant factor as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.31} because, aside from scale, Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.31} is
the unique causal field of any type. Hence the ratio of the coefficients of
the two terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.40} must be the same as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.31} (but with
$B$ replaced with $A$ because this is supposed to be a $(B,A)$ field):
\begin{equation}
\eta^c(-1)^{2B}/\eta^*=(-1)^{2A}.
\tag{5.7.41}\label{eq:5.7.41}
\end{equation}
But $A-B$ differs from the spin $j$ by only an integer, so this gives
\begin{equation}
\eta^c=\eta^*(-1)^{2j}.
\tag{5.7.42}\label{eq:5.7.42}
\end{equation}
We saw special cases of this result in Sections 5.2, 5.3, and 5.5, where
$j=0$, $j=1$, and $j=\tfrac12$, respectively. We now see that the result is
general; \emph{the intrinsic parity $\eta^c\eta$ of a particle--antiparticle
pair is $+1$ for bosons, and $-1$ for fermions}. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.42} in Eq.
\eqref{eq:5.7.40}, our final result for space inversion is
\begin{equation}
P\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)P^{-1}=
\eta^*(-1)^{A+B-j}\psi^{BA}_{ba}(-\mathbf x,x^0).
\tag{5.7.43}\label{eq:5.7.43}
\end{equation}
Let's see how this applies to the Dirac field. For the top $(\tfrac12,0)$ and
bottom $(0,\tfrac12)$ components of the Dirac field, the sign
$(-1)^{A+B-j}$ is just $+1$, so the parity operator simply takes $\mathbf x$
into $-\mathbf x$; reverses the top and bottom components; and multiplies the
field with $\eta^*$. The reversal of the top and bottom components of the
Dirac field is accomplished by the matrix $\beta$ in \eqref{eq:5.5.41}.

Now let us consider charge-conjugation. Its effect on the particle annihilation
and antiparticle creation operators is
\begin{align}
C a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}C^{-1}&=\xi^*a^c_{\mathbf p,\sigma},
\tag{5.7.44}\label{eq:5.7.44}\\
C a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,\sigma}C^{-1}&=\xi^c a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\sigma},
\tag{5.7.45}\label{eq:5.7.45}
\end{align}
where $\xi$ and $\xi^c$ are the charge-conjugation parities of the particle
and antiparticle, respectively. Applying this transformation to the field
\eqref{eq:5.7.31}, we find
\begin{align}
C\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)C^{-1}={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
u^{AB}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl[\xi^*a^c_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}
+\xi^c(-1)^{2B}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,-\sigma}(-1)^{j-\sigma}
e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr].
\tag{5.7.46}\label{eq:5.7.46}
\end{align}
It is useful to compare this formula for the charge-conjugate of an $(A,B)$
field with the adjoint of the $(B,A)$ field for the same particle:
\begin{align}
\psi^{BA\dagger}_{ba}(x)={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
u^{BA*}_{ba}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl[(-1)^{2A}(-1)^{j-\sigma}a^c_{\mathbf p,-\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}
+a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr].
\tag{5.7.47}\label{eq:5.7.47}
\end{align}
To calculate the $u^*$, we use our previous result
\[
\mathbf J^{(j)*}=-\mathcal C_j\mathbf J^{(j)}\mathcal C_j^{-1},
\qquad (\mathcal C_j)_{\bar\sigma\sigma}\propto
(-1)^{j-\sigma}\delta_{\bar\sigma,-\sigma}.
\]
The Clebsch--Gordan coefficient in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.14} is real, so
\begin{align*}
u^{BA}_{ba}(\mathbf p,\sigma)^*={}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2p^0}}
\sum_{a'b'}
\bigl(\exp(-\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(A)}\theta)\bigr)_{-a,-a'}
\bigl(\exp(+\widehat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J^{(B)}\theta)\bigr)_{-b,-b'}
\\
&\cdot(-1)^{a'-a}(-1)^{b'-b}C_{BA}(j\sigma;b'a').
\end{align*}
We use the reflection property of the Clebsch--Gordan coefficients~\hyperref[ref:5.14]{[14]}
\begin{equation}
C_{BA}(j,-\sigma;-b',-a')=C_{AB}(j\sigma;a'b')
\tag{5.7.48}\label{eq:5.7.48}
\end{equation}
and the fact that those coefficients vanish unless $a'+b'=\sigma$, to write
\begin{equation}
u^{BA}_{-b,-a}(\mathbf p,-\sigma)^*=(-1)^{a+b-\sigma}
u^{AB}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma).
\tag{5.7.49}\label{eq:5.7.49}
\end{equation}
The field adjoint \eqref{eq:5.7.47} is then (replacing
$a\to-a$, $b\to-b$, $\sigma\to-\sigma$)
\begin{align*}
\psi^{BA\dagger}_{-b,-a}(x)={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
(-1)^{a+b-\sigma}u^{AB}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\\
&\cdot\bigl[(-1)^{2A}(-1)^{j+\sigma}a^c_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}
+a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,-\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr].
\end{align*}
Using the sign relation $(-1)^{-2A-j}=(-1)^{2B+j}$, this is
\begin{align}
(-1)^{-2A-a-b-j}\psi^{BA\dagger}_{-b,-a}(x)
={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,u^{AB}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl[a^c_{\mathbf p,\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}
+(-1)^{j-\sigma+2B}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,-\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr].
\tag{5.7.50}\label{eq:5.7.50}
\end{align}
In order that $C\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)C^{-1}$ should commute or anticommute with
all ordinary fields at space-like separations, it is necessary that it be
proportional to $\psi^{BA\dagger}_{-b,-a}(x)$, because this is the adjoint of
the unique causal field of transformation type $(B,A)$. Comparing Eq.
\eqref{eq:5.7.50} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.46}, we see that this is only possible if the
charge-conjugation parities are related by
\begin{equation}
\xi^*=\xi^c,
\tag{5.7.51}\label{eq:5.7.51}
\end{equation}
in which case
\begin{equation}
C\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)C^{-1}=
\xi^*(-1)^{-2A-a-b-j}\psi^{BA\dagger}_{-b,-a}(x).
\tag{5.7.52}\label{eq:5.7.52}
\end{equation}
We have already encountered the relation \eqref{eq:5.7.51} for spins $0$, $1$, and
$\tfrac12$ in Sections 5.2, 5.3, and 5.5, and noted some of its implications
for electron--positron and quark--antiquark states in Section 5.5.

In particular, for a particle that is its own antiparticle, Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.52} is
satisfied without any charge-conjugation operator on the left-hand side or
phase $\xi^*$ on the right:
\begin{equation}
\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)=(-1)^{-2A-a-b-j}\psi^{BA\dagger}_{-b,-a}(x).
\tag{5.7.53}\label{eq:5.7.53}
\end{equation}
We have already seen an example of this sort of reality condition for Majorana
spin $\tfrac12$ particles in Section 5.5.

Finally we come to time-reversal. Applied to particle annihilation and
antiparticle creation operators, this gives
\begin{align}
T a_{\mathbf p,\sigma}T^{-1}&=\zeta^*(-1)^{j-\sigma}
a_{-\mathbf p,-\sigma},
\tag{5.7.54}\label{eq:5.7.54}\\
T a^{c\dagger}_{\mathbf p,\sigma}T^{-1}&=\zeta^c(-1)^{j-\sigma}
a^{c\dagger}_{-\mathbf p,-\sigma}.
\tag{5.7.55}\label{eq:5.7.55}
\end{align}
The irreducible field \eqref{eq:5.7.31} thus has the transformation property
\begin{align}
T\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)T^{-1}={}&(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_\sigma\int d^3p\,
u^{AB*}_{ab}(\mathbf p,\sigma)(-1)^{j-\sigma}
\notag\\
&\cdot\bigl[\zeta^*a_{-\mathbf p,-\sigma}e^{-ip\cdot x}
+\zeta^c(-1)^{2B}a^{c\dagger}_{-\mathbf p,-\sigma}e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr].
\tag{5.7.56}\label{eq:5.7.56}
\end{align}
To calculate the complex conjugate of the coefficient function, we use Eq.
\eqref{eq:5.7.14} and the standard formula~\hyperref[ref:5.14]{[14]}
\begin{equation}
C_{AB}(j\sigma;a,b)=(-1)^{A+B-j}C_{AB}(j,-\sigma;-a,-b)
\tag{5.7.57}\label{eq:5.7.57}
\end{equation}
and find:
\begin{equation}
u^{AB*}_{ab}(-\mathbf p,-\sigma)=
(-1)^{a+b+\sigma+A+B-j}u^{AB}_{-a,-b}(\mathbf p,\sigma).
\tag{5.7.58}\label{eq:5.7.58}
\end{equation}
Changing the variables of integration and summation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.7.56} to
$-\mathbf p$ and $-\sigma$, we find that in order for an $(A,B)$ field to be
transformed by time-reversal into something proportional to another $(A,B)$
field, it is necessary that
\begin{equation}
\zeta^c=\zeta^*,
\tag{5.7.59}\label{eq:5.7.59}
\end{equation}
in which case
\begin{equation}
T\psi^{AB}_{ab}(x)T^{-1}=
(-1)^{a+b+A+B-2j}\zeta^*\psi^{AB}_{-a,-b}(\mathbf x,-x^0).
\tag{5.7.60}\label{eq:5.7.60}
\end{equation}

It should be mentioned that from time to time various difficulties have been
reported~\hyperref[ref:5.15]{[15]} in the field theory of particles with spin $j\geq 3/2$.
Generally, these are encountered in the study of the propagation of a higher
spin field in the presence of a c-number external field. Depending on the
details of the theory, the difficulties encountered include non-causality,
inconsistency, unphysical mass states, and violation of unitarity. I will not
go into details about these problems here, because it seems to me that they are
not relevant to the calculational scheme described in this chapter, for the
following reasons:

(1) The fields $\psi_{ab}(x)$ have been constructed here directly from the
creation and annihilation operators for physical particles, so no question of
inconsistency or unphysical mass states can arise. These are free fields, but
by incorporating them into an interaction Hamiltonian density in the
interaction picture, we can use perturbation theory to calculate $S$-matrix
elements that automatically satisfy the cluster decomposition principle. As
long as the interaction Hamiltonian is Hermitian, there can be no difficulty
with unitarity. Lorentz invariance is guaranteed in perturbation theory as long
as we add appropriate local but non-covariant terms in the Hamiltonian
density; though a rigorous proof is lacking, there is no reason to doubt that
this is always possible. Thus any difficulties with higher spin can only arise
when we try to go beyond perturbation theory.

(2) As discussed in Section 13.6, the solution of field equations in the
presence of a c-number background field (the context where all the problems
with higher spin have been found) does go beyond perturbation theory, in that
the results correspond to summing an infinite subset of terms in the
perturbation series. This partial summation is justified, even for weak
external fields, if the fields are sufficiently slowly varying, the smallness
of energy denominators making up for the weakness of the fields. But the
results obtained in this way depend on all the details of the interaction of
the high-spin particle with the external fields: not only the multipole moments
of the particle but also possible terms in the interaction that are non-linear
in the external fields. The problems reported~\hyperref[ref:5.15]{[15]} with higher spin have been
encountered only for higher-spin particles that have been arbitrarily assumed
to have only very simple interactions with external fields. No one has shown
that the problems persist for arbitrary interactions, and as we shall see in
Chapter 12, particles of higher spin are expected to have interactions of all
possible types allowed by symmetry principles.

(3) In fact, there are good reasons to believe that the problems with higher
spin disappear if the interaction with external fields is sufficiently
complicated. For one thing, there is no doubt about the existence of
higher-spin \emph{particles}, including various stable nuclei and hadronic
resonances. If there is any problem with higher spin, it can only be for
`point' particles, that is, those whose interactions with external fields are
particularly simple. It should be kept in mind that the requirement of
simplicity depends on the choice of which field we choose to represent the
higher-spin particle. Remember that any free field types for a given particle
can be expressed as a derivative operator acting on any other field type, so in
the interaction picture any interaction with external fields may be written in
terms of any field types we like, but interactions that are simple when
expressed in terms of a field of one type may look complicated when expressed
in terms of a field of another type. So the requirement of simplicity does not
seem to have any objective content.

(4) Also, both higher-dimensional `Kaluza--Klein' theories and string theories
provide examples of consistent theories of a charged massive particles of spin
two interacting with a electromagnetic background field~\hyperref[ref:5.16]{[16]}. (It was found
that the consistency of the theory depends on the assumption of realistic
external fields that satisfy the field equations, a point generally neglected
in earlier work.) Reformulating this work in the interaction picture, the spin
two particle is represented by a $(1,1)$ free field, but as mentioned above,
the interactions may be reexpressed in the interaction picture in terms of any
field type $(A,B)$ that contains the $j=2$ representation of the rotation
group.
