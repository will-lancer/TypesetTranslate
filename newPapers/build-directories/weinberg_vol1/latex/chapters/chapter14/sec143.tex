\subsection{The Lamb Shift in Light Atoms}

Let us now consider radiative corrections to the energy levels of a
non-relativistic electron in a general electrostatic field, such as an electron
in the Coulomb field of a light nucleus with $Z\alpha\ll1$. It is natural in
this limit to treat the Coulomb field as a weak perturbation, but we will see
that this would lead to an infrared divergence, related to that found in
Section 11.3. The infrared divergence is really fictitious, because the
four-momenta $\mathbf p,E_N$ and $\mathbf p',E_N$ are not on the electron mass
shell, but it does force us to proceed with some care.

Usually this problem is dealt with by dividing the integral over virtual
photon energies into a low-energy range, within which we can treat the
electrons non-relativistically but must include effects to all orders in the
external field, and a high-energy range, within which we have to include
relativistic effects but may include only effects of lowest order in the
external field. Instead, we shall here introduce a fictitious photon mass
$\mu$, chosen to be much larger than typical electron kinetic energies, but
much less than typical electron momenta. For a Coulomb field, this amounts to
the requirement that
\begin{equation}
(Z\alpha)^2m_e\ll\mu\ll Z\alpha m_e.
\tag{14.3.1}\label{eq:14.3.1}
\end{equation}
We write the photon propagator in the first two terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.24}
(including the formulas for the counterterms $Z_2-1$ and $Z_2\delta m$) in the
form
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}
=\left[\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]
+\left[\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}
-\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}\right].
\tag{14.3.2}\label{eq:14.3.2}
\end{equation}
The energy shift is correspondingly a sum of two terms, a `high-energy' and a
`low-energy' term: The high-energy term is calculated by using the first term
in the photon propagator \eqref{eq:14.3.2} in the first three terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.24}, and adding the result to the last two terms (the vacuum
polarization terms) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.24} which are not infrared divergent
anyway; the low-energy term is calculated using the second term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.2} in the first three terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.24}. One
advantage of this procedure is that we shall be able to use the results of the
relativistic calculations of Sections 11.3 and 11.4 directly, without the
rather tricky conversion from a photon mass to an infrared energy cutoff. Of
course, in the end we shall have to check that the dependence on the photon
mass $\mu$ in the high-energy and low-energy contributions to the energy shift
cancel, leaving the total energy shift $\mu$-independent.

\subsubsection*{A\quad High-Energy Term}

Because $\mu$ is taken much larger than the atomic binding energies we can
here keep only terms of lowest order in the external field. The one-loop
radiative correction to atomic energy levels in a general time-independent
external vector potential $\mathcal A^\mu(\mathbf x)$ is given in momentum
space by Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.28}, with the self-energy insertion
$\Sigma_{\mathcal A}(p',p)$ given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.2.24} and
\eqref{eq:14.2.23}. The terms of zeroth order in the external field simply
cancel: the $\delta m$ term cancels the first term with $\mathcal A=0$; the
$Z_3-1$ term cancels the third term with $\mathcal A=0$; and the $Z_2-1$ terms
vanish because $u(p)$ satisfies the Dirac equation. The term in
$\Sigma_{\mathcal A}(p',p)$ that is of first order in $\mathcal A^\mu$ may be
put in the form
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_{\mathcal A1}(p',p)=-ie\mathcal A_\mu(p'-p)\Gamma_1^\mu(p',p)
\tag{14.3.3}\label{eq:14.3.3}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
\Gamma_1^\mu(p',p)={}&\frac{ie^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int
\frac{\dd^4k}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}
\left[\frac{-\ii(\sl{p}'-\sl{k})+m_e}
{(p'-k)^2+m_e^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]\gamma^\mu
\left[\frac{-\ii(\sl{p}-\sl{k})+m_e}
{(p-k)^2+m_e^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]
\notag\\
&\quad+(Z_2-1)\gamma^\mu \notag\\
&-\frac{ie^2\gamma_\nu}{(2\pi)^4[(p-p')^2-\ii\epsilon]}
\int \dd^4l\,\operatorname{Tr}\left\{
\left[\frac{-\ii\sl{l}+m_e}{l^2+m_e^2}\right]\gamma^\mu
\left[\frac{-\ii\sl{l}-\ii\sl{p}'+\ii\sl{p}+m_e}
{(l+p'-p)^2+m_e^2}\right]\gamma^\nu\right\}
\notag\\
&-\frac{Z_3-1}{(p'-p)^2-\ii\epsilon}\notag\\
&\quad\cdot
\left[(p-p')^2\eta_{\mu\nu}-(p'-p)_\mu(p'-p)_\nu\right]\gamma^\nu.
\tag{14.3.4}\label{eq:14.3.4}
\end{align}
Comparison of the first two terms with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.1} and
\eqref{eq:11.3.8} and the second two terms with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.9},
\eqref{eq:11.2.3}, and \eqref{eq:11.2.15} reveals that
$\Gamma_1^\mu(p',p)$ is the complete one-loop vertex function, including
vacuum polarization and all counterterms, whose mass-shell matrix elements we
have already calculated in Section 11.3. Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.2.26} and
\eqref{eq:14.2.25}, this contribution to the energy shift
\eqref{eq:14.2.28} is given by
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{high energy}}
={}&ie\int \dd^3p'\int \dd^3p\,
\bigl(\bar u_N(\mathbf p')\Gamma_1^\mu(\mathbf p',E_N,
\mathbf p,E_N)u_N(\mathbf p)\bigr)
\mathcal A_\mu(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p).
\tag{14.3.5}\label{eq:14.3.5}
\end{align}
(This could have been guessed at, by simply replacing the $\gamma^\mu$ in the
interaction of the electron with the external field with $\Gamma_1^\mu$.) As
discussed in Section 14.1, because $Z\alpha\ll1$ we can approximate the Dirac
wave function $u_N$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.5} as
\begin{equation}
[u_N(\mathbf p)]_\alpha=\sum_\sigma u_\alpha(\mathbf p,\sigma)
[f_N(\mathbf p)]_\sigma,
\tag{14.3.6}\label{eq:14.3.6}
\end{equation}
where $f_N$ is the non-relativistic two-component wave function of an electron
in the external Coulomb field, and $u(\mathbf p,\sigma)$ is the four-component
normalized solution of the momentum space Dirac equation
\begin{equation}
[\ii\gamma_\mu p^\mu+m_e]u(\mathbf p,\sigma)=0
\tag{14.3.7}\label{eq:14.3.7}
\end{equation}
for spin $z$-component $\sigma$. Since $u_N(\mathbf p)$ approximately
satisfies the free-particle Dirac equation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.6.15} gives the
general form of the matrix element of $\Gamma_1^\mu$ as
\begin{align}
\bar u_N(\mathbf p')[\gamma^\mu+\Gamma_1^\mu(p',p)]u_N(\mathbf p)
={}&\bar u_N(\mathbf p')
\left[\gamma^\mu F_1(q^2)+\frac{\ii}{2}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]q_\nu
F_2(q^2)\right]u_N(\mathbf p),
\tag{14.3.8}\label{eq:14.3.8}
\end{align}
where $q\equiv p'-p$. The wave functions $u_N(\mathbf p)$ fall off very rapidly
for $|\mathbf p|\gg Z\alpha m_e$, so we only need $F_1(q^2)$ and $F_2(q^2)$ in
the limit $|q^2|\ll m_e^2$. In this limit, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.31},
\eqref{eq:10.6.18}, and \eqref{eq:11.3.16} give
\begin{equation}
F_1(q^2)\simeq1+\frac{e^2}{24\pi^2}\left(\frac{q^2}{m_e^2}\right)
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu^2}{m_e^2}\right)+\frac25+\frac34\right],
\tag{14.3.9}\label{eq:14.3.9}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F_2(q^2)\simeq\frac{e^2}{16m_e\pi^2}.
\tag{14.3.10}\label{eq:14.3.10}
\end{equation}
Let us first consider the contribution of the $F_1$ term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.8}, which makes by far the largest contribution to the
energy shift, and raises the most interesting problems in its calculation.
For a pure electrostatic field with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal A}=0$,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.5}, \eqref{eq:14.3.8}, and \eqref{eq:14.3.9} give
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{F_1}={}&-\frac{e^2}{24\pi^2m_e^2}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu^2}{m_e^2}\right)+\frac25+\frac34\right]
\int \dd^3p'\int \dd^3p\,\bar u_N(\mathbf p')
\notag\\
&\quad\cdot
\bigl(-ie\mathcal A^0(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)\bigr)
\gamma^0(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)^2u_N(\mathbf p).
\tag{14.3.11}\label{eq:14.3.11}
\end{align}
To calculate this contribution, we may use the leading term in the
non-relativistic matrix element \eqref{eq:14.1.41}, and find
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{F_1}={}&-\frac{e^2}{24\pi^2m_e^2}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu^2}{m_e^2}\right)+\frac25+\frac34\right]
\int \dd^3p'\int \dd^3p\,f_N^\dagger(\mathbf p')
e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)
[\mathbf p'-\mathbf p]^2f_N(\mathbf p)
\tag{14.3.12}\label{eq:14.3.12}
\end{align}
or in position space
\begin{equation}
[\delta E_N]_{F_1}=\frac{e^2}{24\pi^2m_e^2}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu^2}{m_e^2}\right)+\frac25+\frac34\right]
\int \dd^3x\,f_N^\dagger(\mathbf x)
[e\nabla^2\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)]f_N(\mathbf x).
\tag{14.3.13}\label{eq:14.3.13}
\end{equation}
In particular, for the Coulomb potential \eqref{eq:14.1.2}, we have
$e\nabla^2\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)=-Ze^2\delta^3(\mathbf x)$, and the label
$N$ consists of a principal quantum number $n$ and angular-momentum quantum
numbers $j,m,\ell$, while \eqref{eq:11.2.41} gives
$[f_{njm\ell}(0)]_\sigma=2(Z\alpha m_e/n)^{3/2}
\delta_{\ell,0}\delta_{\sigma,m}/\sqrt{4\pi}$. The energy shift
\eqref{eq:14.3.11} is then
\begin{equation}
[\delta E_{nj\ell}]_{F_1}=-\frac{2Z^4\alpha^5m_e}{3\pi n^3}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu^2}{m_e^2}\right)+\frac25+\frac34\right]
\delta_{\ell,0}.
\tag{14.3.14}\label{eq:14.3.14}
\end{equation}
(The lack of dependence of $\delta E$ on the total angular momentum
$z$-component $m$ is guaranteed by rotational invariance.) The term $2/5$ in
the brackets in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.12} and \eqref{eq:14.3.13} arises from
vacuum polarization, and yields just the energy shift calculated somewhat
heuristically in Section 11.2.

Before going on to calculate the magnetic and low-energy contributions to the
energy shift, it is worth noting that the result we have obtained so far
yields a fair order-of-magnitude estimate of the Lamb shift without further
work. We can anticipate that the low-energy terms will contain a term
proportional to $\ln(\mu/B)$, with a coefficient such as to cancel the
$\mu$-dependence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.12}. The constant $B$ here is an energy
which must be included to make the argument of the logarithm dimensionless;
since it is the binding of the electron in the atom that will eventually
provide our infrared cutoff, we may guess that $B$ is a typical atomic binding
energy, of order $B\simeq(Z\alpha)^2m_e$. The total energy shift in a state
$N$ with principal quantum number $n$ and orbital angular momentum $\ell$ is
therefore of the form
\begin{equation}
\delta E_N=-\frac{2Z^4\alpha^5m_e}{3\pi n^3}
\left[\ln(Z^4\alpha^4)\delta_{\ell,0}+O(1)\right].
\tag{14.3.15}\label{eq:14.3.15}
\end{equation}
For the $2s$ state of hydrogen, the logarithmic term alone gives
\[
\delta E_{2s}=-\frac{\alpha^5m_e}{12\pi}\ln(\alpha^4)
=5.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{eV}=1300\,\mathrm{MHz}\cdot2\pi\hbar.
\]
As we shall see, the `$O(1)$' terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.15} will lower the
total energy shift by about $25\%$.

Now let us consider the contribution of the $F_2$ term in the matrix element
of $\Gamma_1^\mu$, which as we saw in Section 10.6 may be interpreted as a
radiative correction to the magnetic moment of the electron. Using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.10}, \eqref{eq:14.3.8}, and \eqref{eq:14.3.6} in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.5}, we find that this term gives an energy shift
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{F_2}={}&-\frac{e^2}{32\pi^2m_e}\int \dd^3p'\int \dd^3p\,
\bigl(\bar u_N(\mathbf p')[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]u_N(\mathbf p)\bigr)
e\mathcal A_\mu(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)(p'-p)_\nu.
\tag{14.3.16}\label{eq:14.3.16}
\end{align}
or in position space
\begin{equation}
[\delta E_N]_{F_2}=\frac{ie^2}{64\pi^2m_e}\int \dd^3x\,
\bigl(\bar u_N(\mathbf x)[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]u_N(\mathbf x)\bigr)
e\mathcal F_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf x),
\tag{14.3.17}\label{eq:14.3.17}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathcal F_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf x)
=\partial_\mu\mathcal A_\nu(\mathbf x)-\partial_\nu\mathcal A_\mu(\mathbf x).
\tag{14.3.18}\label{eq:14.3.18}
\end{equation}
For a pure electrostatic field with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal A}= \mathbf{0}$, this is
\begin{equation}
[\delta E_N]_{F_2}=-\frac{ie^2}{32\pi^2m_e}\int \dd^3x\,
\bigl(\bar u_N(\mathbf x)[\gamma^0,\boldsymbol\gamma]u_N(\mathbf x)\bigr)
\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla[e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)].
\tag{14.3.19}\label{eq:14.3.19}
\end{equation}
In the non-relativistic limit $Z\alpha\ll1$, we can use the approximate result
\eqref{eq:14.1.43}, which here reads
\begin{align}
\bigl(\bar u_N[\gamma^0,\boldsymbol\gamma]u_N\bigr)
&\simeq\frac{\ii}{m_e}\left[(\boldsymbol\nabla f_N^\dagger)
\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\,\boldsymbol\sigma f_N
+f_N^\dagger\boldsymbol\sigma\,\boldsymbol\sigma
\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla f_N\right]\notag\\
&=\frac{\ii}{m_e}\left[\boldsymbol\nabla(f_N^\dagger f_N)
-\ii(\boldsymbol\nabla f_N^\dagger\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\sigma)f_N
-if_N^\dagger(\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\nabla f_N)\right].
\tag{14.3.20}\label{eq:14.3.20}
\end{align}
Using this in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.8} and integrating by parts, this part of the
energy shift is
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{F_2}=-\frac{e^2}{32\pi^2m_e^2}\int \dd^3x\,
\Bigl[&-|f_N(\mathbf x)|^2\nabla^2(e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x))\notag\\
&+2if_N^\dagger(\mathbf x)\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot
\bigl(\boldsymbol\nabla(e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x))
\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\nabla f_N(\mathbf x)\bigr)\Bigr].
\tag{14.3.21}\label{eq:14.3.21}
\end{align}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.12} and \eqref{eq:14.3.21} gives the total
high-energy contribution to the energy shift in an arbitrary electrostatic
potential $\mathcal A^0$:
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{high energy}}={}&
\frac{e^2}{24\pi^2m_e^2}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu^2}{m_e^2}\right)+\frac25\right]
\int \dd^3x\,f_N^\dagger(\mathbf x)
[e\nabla^2\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)]f_N(\mathbf x)\notag\\
&+\frac{ie^2}{16\pi^2m_e^2}\int \dd^3x\,
f_N^\dagger(\mathbf x)\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot
\bigl(\boldsymbol\nabla[e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)]
\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\nabla f_N(\mathbf x)\bigr).
\tag{14.3.22}\label{eq:14.3.22}
\end{align}

\subsubsection*{B\quad Low-Energy Term}

The low-energy contribution to the energy shift is obtained from the first
three terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.24}, making the replacement in the photon
propagator
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}\longrightarrow
\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}-\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}.
\tag{14.3.23}\label{eq:14.3.23}
\end{equation}
This substitution will eventually serve to cut off the integral over
components of the photon four-momentum $k$ at values of order $\mu$, but it is
not possible to see this until we carefully take mass renormalization into
account, so we shall defer making any non-relativistic approximations until
then. Also, we are now including photon momenta as small or smaller than the
binding energies of the atomic states, so we must treat the electrostatic
forces responsible for this binding to all orders.

Instead of working with the momentum space formula \eqref{eq:14.2.24}, it will
be convenient to return to the configuration space formula
\eqref{eq:14.2.18}. This gives the low-energy contribution to the electron
self-energy function as
\begin{align}
[\Sigma_{\mathcal A}^*(x,y)]_{\text{low energy}}
={}&ie^2\gamma^\mu S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)\gamma_\mu D(x-y;\mu)
+\delta m_e(\mu)\delta^4(x-y)\notag\\
&-\ii(Z_2(\mu)-1)\{\gamma^\mu[\partial_\mu+ie\mathcal A_\mu]+m_e\}
\delta^4(x-y),
\tag{14.3.24}\label{eq:14.3.24}
\end{align}
where $D(x-y;\mu)$ is the modified photon propagator
\begin{equation}
D(x-y;\mu)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^4}\int \dd^4k\,e^{-\ii k\cdot(x-y)}
\left[\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}-\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}\right],
\tag{14.3.25}\label{eq:14.3.25}
\end{equation}
and the counterterms $Z_2(\mu)-1$ and $\delta m(\mu)$ are calculated using this
modified propagator. Converting from time to energy variables, the low-energy
contribution to the function \eqref{eq:14.2.13} is then
\begin{align}
[\Sigma_{\mathcal A}^*(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)]_{\text{low energy}}
={}&\frac{ie^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int \dd^4k\,\gamma^\mu
\notag\\
&\quad\cdot S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E-k^0)\gamma_\mu
\left[\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}-\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]
e^{\ii\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}\notag\\
&-(Z_2(\mu)-1)(\boldsymbol\gamma\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla
+\ii\gamma^0E+ie\gamma^\nu\mathcal A_\nu+m_e)
\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)\notag\\
&\quad+\delta m_e(\mu)\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y).
\tag{14.3.26}\label{eq:14.3.26}
\end{align}
The low-energy contribution to the energy shift is then given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.17} as
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}
={}&-\int \dd^3x\int \dd^3y\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)
[\Sigma_{\mathcal A}^*(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E_N)]_{\text{low energy}}
u_N(\mathbf y)\notag\\
={}&-\frac{ie^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int \dd^4k\int \dd^3x\int \dd^3y\,
\bar u_N(\mathbf x)\gamma^\mu
S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E_N-k^0)\gamma_\mu u_N(\mathbf y)
\notag\\
&\hspace{15mm}\cdot
\left[\frac{1}{k^2-\ii\epsilon}-\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]
e^{\ii\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}\notag\\
&\quad-\delta m_e(\mu)\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)u_N(\mathbf x).
\tag{14.3.27}\label{eq:14.3.27}
\end{align}
Note that the terms proportional to $Z_2(\mu)-1$ have dropped out because the
Dirac wave function $u_N(\mathbf x)$ satisfies the Dirac equation
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.1.10}. For the electron propagator in the presence of the
Coulomb field, we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.15}:
\[
S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)
=\sum_M\frac{u_M(\mathbf x)\bar u_M(\mathbf y)}{E_M-E-\ii\epsilon}
-\sum_M\frac{v_M(\mathbf x)\bar v_M(\mathbf y)}{E_M+E-\ii\epsilon},
\]
with the sums in the first and second terms running over all one-electron and
one-positron states, respectively. The $k^0$ integrals can be done most easily
by closing the contour of integration with a large semi-circle in the lower
half-plane in the first term and in the upper half-plane in the second term:
\begin{align*}
&\int \dd k^0\left(\frac{1}{k^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}\right)
\left(\frac{1}{E_M\mp E_N\pm k^0-\ii\epsilon}\right)\\
&\qquad={}
\frac{\ii\pi}{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}}
\left(\frac{1}{E_M\mp E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon}\right),
\end{align*}
and likewise if $\mu$ is replaced with zero. The energy shift
\eqref{eq:14.3.27} now becomes
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}
={}&-\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\sum_M
\Biggl[\Gamma_{MN}^\rho(\mathbf k)^*\Gamma_{\rho MN}(\mathbf k)
\left(\frac{1}{|\mathbf k|(E_M-E_N+|\mathbf k|-\ii\epsilon)}
\right.\notag\\[-2mm]
&\left.\hspace{41mm}
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}
(E_M-E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon)}\right)\notag\\
&-\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^\rho(\mathbf k)^*
\widetilde\Gamma_{\rho MN}(\mathbf k)
\left(\frac{1}{|\mathbf k|(E_M+E_N+|\mathbf k|-\ii\epsilon)}
\right.\notag\\[-2mm]
&\left.\hspace{41mm}
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}
(E_M+E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon)}\right)\Biggr]
\notag\\
&-\delta m_e(\mu)\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)u_N(\mathbf x),
\tag{14.3.28}\label{eq:14.3.28}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{MN}^\rho(\mathbf k)
\equiv\int \dd^3y\,e^{-\ii\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf y}
\bar u_M(\mathbf y)\gamma^\rho u_N(\mathbf y),
\tag{14.3.29}\label{eq:14.3.29}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^\rho(\mathbf k)
\equiv\int \dd^3y\,e^{-\ii\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf y}
\bar v_M(\mathbf y)\gamma^\rho u_N(\mathbf y).
\tag{14.3.30}\label{eq:14.3.30}
\end{equation}
(Of course, the `sum' over $M$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.28} receives
contributions only from electron states in the first term and positron states
in the second term.) Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.28} could have been derived more
directly from old-fashioned perturbation theory; the energy denominators
$E_M-E_N+\omega$ and $E_M+E_N+\omega$ are the result of subtracting the energy
$E_N$ of the initial state from the energy of an intermediate state consisting
of either an electron of energy $E_M$ and a photon of energy $\omega$, or else
a positron of energy $E_M$, a photon of energy $\omega$, and both the final and
the initial electron. (See Figure~\ref{fig:14.2}.)

Before making any approximations to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.28}, it will be
convenient to express the time components of the matrix elements
$\Gamma_{MN}^\rho$ and $\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^\rho$ in terms of the
corresponding space components, using relations\footnote{To derive
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.31}, note that
\begin{align*}
k_i\Gamma_{MN}^i(\mathbf k)
&=-\ii\int \dd^3x\,e^{-\ii\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}
\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot(\bar u_M(\mathbf x)\boldsymbol\gamma u_N(\mathbf x))\\
&=\ii\int \dd^3x\,e^{-\ii\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}
\partial_0\left[\bar u_M(\mathbf x)\gamma^0u_N(\mathbf x)
e^{-\ii(E_N-E_M)x^0}\right]_{x^0=0}
=(E_N-E_M)\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k).
\end{align*}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.32} is derived in the same way.} derived from the
conservation of the electric current:
\begin{equation}
k_i\Gamma_{MN}^i(\mathbf k)=(E_N-E_M)\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k),
\tag{14.3.31}\label{eq:14.3.31}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
k_i\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^i(\mathbf k)
=(E_N+E_M)\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k).
\tag{14.3.32}\label{eq:14.3.32}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, by using the completeness relation \eqref{eq:14.1.8} it is
straightforward to show that
\begin{equation}
\sum_M\left[|\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k)|^2
+|\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k)|^2\right]=1
\tag{14.3.33}\label{eq:14.3.33}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{align}
\sum_M\Bigl[|\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k)|^2(E_M-E_N)
-|\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k)|^2(E_M+E_N)\Bigr]
={}&\sum_M\Bigl[-\Gamma_{MN}^{0*}(\mathbf k)
\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)\notag\\
&-\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^{0*}(\mathbf k)
\mathbf k\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol\Gamma}_{MN}(\mathbf k)\Bigr]
\notag\\
={}&-\ii\mathbf k\cdot\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)
\boldsymbol\gamma u_N(\mathbf x)=0,
\tag{14.3.34}\label{eq:14.3.34}
\end{align}
the last step following from the parity condition \eqref{eq:14.1.23}. In this
way, Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.28} can be rewritten
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}={}&
-\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\sum_M\Biggl[
\frac{|\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/\mathbf k^2}
{|\mathbf k|(E_M-E_N+|\mathbf k|-\ii\epsilon)}\notag\\
&\hspace{12mm}-
\frac{|\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)}
{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}
(E_M-E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon)}\Biggr]\notag\\
&+\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\sum_M\Biggl[
\frac{|\widetilde{\boldsymbol\Gamma}_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol\Gamma}_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/\mathbf k^2}
{|\mathbf k|(E_M+E_N+|\mathbf k|)}\notag\\
&\hspace{12mm}-
\frac{|\widetilde{\boldsymbol\Gamma}_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol\Gamma}_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)}
{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}(E_M+E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2})}
\Biggr]\notag\\
&-\frac{e^2}{(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\sum_M
|\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k)|^2
\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf k^2}-\frac{1}{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}\right)
\notag\\
&+\frac12\alpha\mu-\delta m_e(\mu)
\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)u_N(\mathbf x).
\tag{14.3.35}\label{eq:14.3.35}
\end{align}
In the next-to-last term, we have used the elementary integral
\[
\int \dd^3k\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf k^2}
-\frac{1}{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}\right)=2\mu\pi^2.
\]

So far, this has been an exact rewriting of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.28}. We must
now invoke several approximations. First, consider mass renormalization. We
have already calculated $\delta m_e(\mu)$ to order $\alpha$ in Section 11.4;
it is
\begin{equation}
\delta m_e(\mu)=\frac{2m_e\pi^2e^2}{(2\pi)^4}
\int_0^1\dd x\,(1+x)\ln\left(
\frac{m_e^2x^2+\mu^2(1-x)}{m_e^2x^2}\right).
\tag{14.3.36}\label{eq:14.3.36}
\end{equation}
Although in Section 11.4 we regarded $\mu$ as a regulator mass, to be taken
much larger than $m_e$, we can just as well use Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.36} to
provide a value for $\delta m_e(\mu)$ in the case that interests us here,
$\mu\ll m_e$. In this limit Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.36} gives
\begin{equation}
\delta m_e(\mu)\longrightarrow\frac{\alpha\mu}{2}
\left[1-\frac{3\mu}{2\pi m_e}+\cdots\right].
\tag{14.3.37}\label{eq:14.3.37}
\end{equation}
We also recall that for $Z\alpha\ll1$, $u_N(\mathbf x)$ is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.1.37} as
\begin{equation}
u_N(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\begin{bmatrix}
(1-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf v/2+\cdots)f_N(\mathbf x)\\
(1+\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf v/2+\cdots)f_N(\mathbf x)
\end{bmatrix},
\tag{14.3.38}\label{eq:14.3.38}
\end{equation}
where dots indicate terms of higher order in $Z\alpha$; $\mathbf v$ is the
non-relativistic velocity operator $-\ii\boldsymbol\nabla/m_e$; and
$f_N(\mathbf x)$ is a two-component spinor solution of the non-relativistic
Schrödinger equation, normalized according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.1.38} so that
\begin{equation}
\int|f_N(\mathbf x)|^2=1-\frac14(\mathbf v^2)_{NN}+\cdots.
\tag{14.3.39}\label{eq:14.3.39}
\end{equation}
This gives the coefficient of $\delta m_e(\mu)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35} as
\begin{equation}
\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)u_N(\mathbf x)
=1-\frac12(\mathbf v^2)_{NN}+\cdots.
\tag{14.3.40}\label{eq:14.3.40}
\end{equation}
We note immediately that the leading term in
$-\delta m_e(\mu)\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_Nu_N$ cancels the term $\alpha\mu/2$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35}. Indeed, we could have anticipated this cancellation,
because the term $\alpha\mu/2$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35} survives in the limit
$Z\alpha\to0$, and the definition of $m_e(\mu)$ as the renormalized electron
mass implies that there must be no energy shift in this limit. By the same
argument we can anticipate that the term of order $\alpha\mu^2/m_e$ in
$\delta m_e(\mu)$ (which is larger than of order $\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e$, and
therefore cannot simply be neglected) cancels the second and third terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35}, which are also of this order.\footnote{The cancellation
can be shown as follows. We anticipate that the second and third terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35} are small enough so that they can be evaluated using
the extreme nonrelativistic approximation $\beta u_N(\mathbf x)=u_N(\mathbf
x)$ for the Dirac equation satisfied by $u_N(\mathbf x)$. On the other hand,
although the Coulomb force may be neglected in the positron wave functions
$v_M(\mathbf x)$, the sum over $M$ in the third term receives important
contributions from relativistic positrons, so we use the approximation
$v_{\mathbf p,\sigma}(\mathbf x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}v(\mathbf p,\sigma)
e^{\ii\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}$, where $v(\mathbf p,\sigma)$ is the positron
spinor introduced in Section 5.5, normalized so that
$\bar v(\mathbf p,\sigma')v(\mathbf p,\sigma)=\delta_{\sigma'\sigma}$.
Thus the sums over $M$ in the second and third terms of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35} are approximately given by
\begin{align*}
\frac12\left[\sum_M\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^{i*}(\mathbf k)
\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^{j}(\mathbf k)+(\ii\leftrightarrow j)\right]
&\simeq\delta_{ij}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m_e^2}+m_e}
{2\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m_e^2}}\right),\\
\sum_M|\widetilde\Gamma_{MN}^0(\mathbf k)|^2
&\simeq\left(\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m_e^2}-m_e}
{2\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m_e^2}}\right).
\end{align*}
To leading order in $\mu/m_e$, the second and third terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35} are then, respectively,
\begin{align*}
-\frac{e^2}{4m_e(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k
\left[\frac{2}{k}-\frac{3-k^2/(k^2+\mu^2)}{\sqrt{k^2+\mu^2}}\right]
\left(\frac{\sqrt{k^2+m_e^2}+m_e}{2\sqrt{k^2+m_e^2}}\right)
&\simeq\frac{\alpha\mu^2}{4\pi m_e},\\
-\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k
\left(\frac1{k^2}-\frac1{k^2+\mu^2}\right)
\left(\frac{\sqrt{k^2+m_e^2}-m_e}{2\sqrt{k^2+m_e^2}}\right)
&\simeq-\frac{\alpha\mu^2}{\pi m_e}.
\end{align*}
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.32} rules out the possibility that a relativistic
correction to the latter expression might not be suppressed by the factor
$k^2/m_e^2$ that appears in this expression for $|\mathbf k|^2\ll m_e^2$.)
These two terms are cancelled by the term $+3\mu^2/4\pi m_e$ in
$-\delta m_e(\mu)\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_N(\mathbf x)u_N(\mathbf x)$. Finally, we
note that relativistic corrections to the above estimates for the sums over
positron states would involve additional factors of $v^2/c^2\simeq(Z\alpha)^2$,
yielding contributions of order
$\alpha(Z\alpha)^2\mu^2/m_e\ll\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e$, which justifies the
non-relativistic approximations used here.} On the other hand, the product of
the $\alpha\mu^2/m_e$ term in $\delta m_e$ with the second term in the matrix
element \eqref{eq:14.3.40} is of order
$(Z\alpha)^2\alpha\mu^2/m_e\ll\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e$, and may therefore be
neglected. To order $\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e$, the only remaining effect of mass
renormalization is to leave us with the product of the leading term in
$\delta m_e(\mu)$ with the term of order $(Z\alpha)^2$ in
$\int \dd^3x\,\bar u_Nu_N$:
\[
-\left[\frac{e^2\mu}{8\pi}\right]
\left[-\frac12(\mathbf v^2)_{NN}\right]
=\frac{e^2\mu}{16\pi}(\mathbf v^2)_{NN}.
\]
(This is the effect of mass renormalization on the electron kinetic energy,
mentioned in Section 1.3.) It turns out that this is just the negative of what
the first term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35} would be if we neglected the
difference between energy levels. To see this, note that the integral in this
term is effectively cut off at $|\mathbf k|\sim\mu\ll Z\alpha m_e$, so we can
evaluate the matrix element $\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)$ in the limit
$\mathbf k\to0$. To lowest order in $Z\alpha$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.1.42} gives
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(0)=(\mathbf v)_{MN}
\tag{14.3.41}\label{eq:14.3.41}
\end{equation}
and using the completeness of the solutions $f_N$ of the non-relativistic
Schrödinger equation, we then have
\begin{equation}
\sum_M\Gamma_{MN}^{i*}(\mathbf k)\Gamma_{MN}^j(\mathbf k)
\simeq(v^iv^j)_{NN},
\tag{14.3.42}\label{eq:14.3.42}
\end{equation}
so to this order
\begin{align*}
&-\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\sum_M\\
&\qquad\cdot\Biggl[
\frac{|\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/\mathbf k^2}
{\mathbf k^2}
-\frac{|\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)}
{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}\Biggr]\\
&\qquad\simeq-\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}(\mathbf v^2)_{NN}
\int \dd^3k\left[\frac{2}{3\mathbf k^2}
-\frac{1-\mathbf k^2/3(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)}{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}\right]\\
&\qquad=-\frac{e^2\mu}{16\pi}(\mathbf v^2)_{NN}.
\end{align*}
Thus after mass renormalization we are left with just the first term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.35}, less the same with energy differences $E_N-E_M$
dropped:
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}={}&
\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3k\sum_M(E_M-E_N)
\Biggl[\frac{|\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/\mathbf k^2}
{\mathbf k^2(E_M-E_N+|\mathbf k|-\ii\epsilon)}\notag\\
&\hspace{14mm}-
\frac{|\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2
-|\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma_{MN}(\mathbf k)|^2/(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)}
{(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)(E_M-E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon)}
\Biggr].
\tag{14.3.43}\label{eq:14.3.43}
\end{align}
Again using Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.41}, this is
\begin{align}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}={}&
\frac{e^2}{2(2\pi)^3}\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2
\int \dd^3k\Biggl[
\frac{2}{3\mathbf k^2(E_M-E_N+|\mathbf k|-\ii\epsilon)}\notag\\
&\hspace{22mm}-
\frac{1-\mathbf k^2/3(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)}
{(\mathbf k^2+\mu^2)(E_M-E_N+\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon)}
\Biggr].
\tag{14.3.44}\label{eq:14.3.44}
\end{align}
Even though typical values of the electron momentum are much larger than
typical atomic energy differences, this is not true of typical values of
$|\mathbf k|$ in this integral, because the integral would be infrared
divergent if we did not keep the $E_M-E_N$ terms in the denominators. The
integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.44} may be evaluated in the limit
$\mu\gg|E_M-E_N|\sim(Z\alpha)^2m_e$ by dividing the range of integration of
$|\mathbf k|$ into two segments, from zero to $\lambda$ and from $\lambda$ to
infinity, with $\lambda$ chosen so that $|E_M-E_N|\ll\lambda\ll\mu$ but
otherwise arbitrary. In this way we find
\begin{align*}
\int_0^\infty k^2\dd k\Biggl[&
\frac{2}{3k^2(E_M-E_N+k)-\ii\epsilon}
-\frac{1-k^2/3(k^2+\mu^2)}
{(k^2+\mu^2)(E_M-E_N+\sqrt{k^2+\mu^2}-\ii\epsilon)}\Biggr]\\
&\simeq\frac23\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu}{2|E_M-E_N|}\right)
+\frac56+\ii\pi\theta(E_N-E_M)\right].
\end{align*}
The imaginary term here reflects the possibility of decay of the atom in
state $N$ to states $M$ of lower energy. This term contributes to the decay
rate, given by the imaginary part of the energy shift. We are interested here
in the real part of the energy shift, and so will drop this imaginary term in
what follows. Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.44} now gives
\begin{equation}
[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}
=\frac{e^2}{6\pi^2}\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mu}{2|E_N-E_M|}\right)+\frac56\right].
\tag{14.3.45}\label{eq:14.3.45}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{C\quad Total Energy Shift}

We need to make a connection between the sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.45} and
the matrix element in the high-energy term \eqref{eq:14.3.22}. For this
purpose, let's first see what value the sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.45} would
have if we could ignore the logarithm. We note that
$(E_M-E_N)v_{NM}=[v,H]_{NM}$, so
\begin{align*}
\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2
&=\frac12\sum_M\left([v^i,H]_{NM}v^i_{MN}
+v^i_{NM}[H,v^i]_{MN}\right)\\
&=-\frac{1}{2m_e^2}\bigl([p^i,[p^i,H]]\bigr)_{NN}.
\end{align*}
The only term in the non-relativistic Hamiltonian $H$ that does not commute
with the momentum operator $\mathbf p$ is the potential term
$-e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)$, so this gives
\begin{equation}
\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2
=-\frac{e}{2m_e^2}\bigl(\nabla^2\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)\bigr)_{NN}.
\tag{14.3.46}\label{eq:14.3.46}
\end{equation}
Inspection of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.45} and \eqref{eq:14.3.22} now shows that
the term proportional to $\ln\mu$ in the high-energy term is cancelled by the
term proportional to $\ln\mu$ in the low-energy term:
\begin{align}
\delta E_N={}&[\delta E_N]_{\text{high energy}}
+[\delta E_N]_{\text{low energy}}\notag\\
={}&\frac{e^2}{6\pi^2}\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2
\left[\ln\left(\frac{m_e}{2|E_N-E_M|}\right)+\frac56-\frac15\right]
\notag\\
&-\frac{e^2}{16\pi^2m_e^2}
\bigl(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot
(\boldsymbol\nabla(e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x))\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf p)
\bigr)_{NN}.
\tag{14.3.47}\label{eq:14.3.47}
\end{align}
So far, this has been for a general electrostatic field
$\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)$. Let us now specialize to a pure Coulomb field,
with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x)=Ze/|\mathbf x|.
\tag{14.3.48}\label{eq:14.3.48}
\end{equation}
In this case, Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.46} reads
\begin{equation}
\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2
=\frac{Ze^2}{2m_e^2}(\delta^3(\mathbf x))_{NN}
=\frac{Ze^2}{2m_e^2}\bigl(f_N^\dagger(0)f_N(0)\bigr).
\tag{14.3.49}\label{eq:14.3.49}
\end{equation}
This is non-vanishing only for $\ell=0$. Also, the matrix element in the last
term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.47} has the value
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot
(\boldsymbol\nabla(e\mathcal A^0(\mathbf x))\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf p)
\bigr)_{NN}
=-Ze^2\left(\frac{1}{r^3}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf L\right)_{NN},
\tag{14.3.50}\label{eq:14.3.50}
\end{equation}
which is non-vanishing only for $\ell\ne0$. It is therefore useful at this
point to divide our consideration between the two cases, $\ell=0$ and
$\ell\ne0$.

\paragraph{i.\quad $\ell=0$}
It is convenient here to define a mean excitation energy $\Delta E_N$:
\begin{align}
\sum_M|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2(E_M-E_N)\ln|E_N-E_M|
={}&\ln\Delta E_N\sum_M|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2(E_M-E_N)\notag\\
={}&\frac{Ze^2}{2m_e^2}\ln\Delta E_N
\bigl(f_N^\dagger(0)f_N(0)\bigr).
\tag{14.3.51}\label{eq:14.3.51}
\end{align}
For $s$-wave hydrogenic states, the label $N$ consists of a principal quantum
number $n$ and spin $z$-component $m$, and
$[f_{nm}(0)]_\sigma=2(Z\alpha m_e/n)^{3/2}
\delta_{\sigma,m}/\sqrt{4\pi}$, so
\begin{equation}
\bigl(f_N^\dagger(0)f_N(0)\bigr)
=\frac1\pi\left(\frac{Z\alpha m_e}{n}\right)^3.
\tag{14.3.52}\label{eq:14.3.52}
\end{equation}
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.51} and \eqref{eq:14.3.52} in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.47} gives the energy shift in these states as
\begin{equation}
[\delta E]_{n,\ell=0}=\frac{4\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e}{3\pi n^3}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{m_e}{2\Delta E_{n,\ell=0}}\right)+\frac{19}{30}\right].
\tag{14.3.53}\label{eq:14.3.53}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{ii.\quad $\ell\ne0$}
For non-vanishing orbital angular momentum the sum \eqref{eq:14.3.49}
vanishes, so the definition \eqref{eq:14.3.51} is inappropriate. Instead, it
is conventional here to define a mean excitation energy $\Delta E_N$ by
\begin{equation}
\sum_M(E_M-E_N)|\mathbf v_{MN}|^2\ln|E_N-E_M|
=\frac{2(Z\alpha)^4m_e}{n^3}\ln\left(
\frac{2\Delta E_N}{Z^2\alpha^2m_e}\right).
\tag{14.3.54}\label{eq:14.3.54}
\end{equation}
(Because Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.49} vanishes, it makes no difference what units
are used to measure $E_N-E_M$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.54}.) Also, in a state of
total angular momentum $j$ and orbital angular momentum $\ell$, the scalar
product $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf L$ has the familiar value
$j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-3/4$, and for principal quantum number $n$ the operator
$1/r^3$ has the expectation value
\begin{equation}
\int \dd^3r\,|f_N|^2/r^3
=\frac{2Z^3\alpha^3m_e^3}{n^3\ell(\ell+1)(2\ell+1)}.
\tag{14.3.55}\label{eq:14.3.55}
\end{equation}
Putting this all together in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.47}, we have for $\ell\ne0$:
\begin{align}
[\delta E]_{nj\ell}={}&-\frac{4\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e}{3\pi n^3}
\ln\left(\frac{2\Delta E_{nj\ell}}{Z^2\alpha^2m_e}\right)\notag\\
&+\frac{\alpha(Z\alpha)^4m_e}{2\pi n^3}
\left[\frac{j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-\tfrac34}
{\ell(\ell+1)(2\ell+1)}\right].
\tag{14.3.56}\label{eq:14.3.56}
\end{align}
It only remains to use these results to give numerical value for the energy
shifts. The mean excitation energies here must be calculated numerically;
using non-relativistic hydrogen wave functions, they have the
values~\hyperlink{ref:14.5}{[5]}:
\[
\Delta E_{1s}=19.769266917(6)\,\mathrm{Ry},
\]
\[
\Delta E_{2s}=16.63934203(1)\,\mathrm{Ry},
\]
\[
\Delta E_{2p}=0.9704293186(3)\,\mathrm{Ry},
\]
where $1\,\mathrm{Ry}\equiv m_e\alpha^2/2=13.6057\,\mathrm{eV}$.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.53} then gives
\begin{align}
[\delta E]_{1s}
&=\frac{4\alpha^5m_e}{3\pi}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{m_e}{2\Delta E_{1s}}\right)+\frac{19}{30}\right]
=3.3612\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV}\notag\\
&=2\pi\hbar\cdot8127.4\,\mathrm{MHz},
\tag{14.3.57}\label{eq:14.3.57}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
[\delta E]_{2s}
&=\frac{\alpha^5m_e}{6\pi}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{m_e}{2\Delta E_{2s}}\right)+\frac{19}{30}\right]
=4.2982\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV}\notag\\
&=2\pi\hbar\cdot1039.31\,\mathrm{MHz},
\tag{14.3.58}\label{eq:14.3.58}
\end{align}
while Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.56} gives
\begin{align}
[\delta E]_{2p_{1/2}}
&=\frac{\alpha^5m_e}{6\pi}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\alpha^2m_e}{2\Delta E_{2p}}\right)-\frac18\right]
=-5.3267\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm{eV}\notag\\
&=2\pi\hbar\cdot(-12.88)\,\mathrm{MHz}.
\tag{14.3.59}\label{eq:14.3.59}
\end{align}
The classic Lamb shift is the energy difference between the $2s$ and
$2p_{1/2}$ states of the hydrogen atom, states that would be degenerate in the
absence of radiative corrections. Our calculation has given
\[
[\delta E]_{2s}-[\delta E]_{2p_{1/2}}
=4.35152\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV}
=2\pi\hbar\cdot1052.19\,\mathrm{MHz}.
\]
This is numerically close (though analytically not identical) to the old
result of Kroll and Lamb~\hyperlink{ref:14.6}{[6]} and French and
Weisskopf~\hyperlink{ref:14.7}{[7]}, which they obtained using the techniques
of old-fashioned perturbation theory. Earlier in this section we made a crude
estimate of $1300\,\mathrm{MHz}$ by considering only the high-energy
contribution to the $2s$ energy shift, with an infrared cutoff guessed to be
of order $\alpha^2m_e=2\,\mathrm{Ry}$. We can now see that this was an
overestimate, arising mostly from the fact that the true value of the
effective infrared cutoff $\Delta E_{2s}=16.64\,\mathrm{Ry}$ is considerably
larger than we had guessed. On the other hand, as described in Section 1.3, in
1947 Hans Bethe~\hyperlink{ref:14.8}{[8]} was able to make a rather good
estimate of the Lamb shift, $1040\,\mathrm{MHz}$, by considering only the
low-energy contribution to the $2s$ energy shift, with an ultraviolet cutoff
guessed to be $m_e$. (Bethe made the first estimate of the excitation energy,
$\Delta E_{2s}\simeq17.8\,\mathrm{Ry}$.)

The calculation of the Lamb shift described here has been improved by the
inclusion of higher-order radiative corrections and nuclear size and recoil
effects. At present the greatest uncertainty is due to a doubt about the
correct value of the rms charge radius $r_p$ of the proton. For
$r_p=0.862\cdot10^{-13}\,\mathrm{cm}$ or
$r_p=0.805\cdot10^{-13}\,\mathrm{cm}$, one
calculation~\hyperlink{ref:14.9}{[9]} gives a Lamb shift of either
$1057.87\,\mathrm{MHz}$ or $1057.85\,\mathrm{MHz}$, while
another~\hyperlink{ref:14.10}{[10]} gives either $1057.883\,\mathrm{MHz}$ or
$1057.865\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Given the uncertainty in proton radius, the
agreement is excellent with the present experimental
value~\hyperlink{ref:14.11}{[11]}, $1057.845(9)\,\mathrm{MHz}$. The accuracy of
this experimental value is limited chiefly by the
$\sim100\,\mathrm{MHz}$ natural linewidth of the $2p$ state in hydrogen, so
further improvements here will be very difficult.

In the last few years there has been an important improvement in measurements
of the energy shift of the $1s$ ground state itself, by direct comparison of
the frequency of the $1s$--$2s$ resonance with four times the frequencies of
the $2s$--$4s$ and $2s$--$4d$ two-photon resonances. These $s$ and $d$ states
are much narrower than the $2p$ state, so these frequency differences can be
measured more accurately than the classic Lamb shift. For a brief while it
seemed that there was a discrepancy here between theory and experiment.
Calculations~[\hyperlink{ref:14.12}{12},\hyperlink{ref:14.13}{13}] showed
that for a proton radius $r_p=0.862(11)\cdot10^{-13}\,\mathrm{cm}$ or
$0.805(11)\cdot10^{-13}\,\mathrm{cm}$, the inclusion of proton size and other
corrections increases the theoretical $1s$ energy shift from the above result
of $8127.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$ to $8173.12(6)\,\mathrm{MHz}$ or
$8172.94(9)\,\mathrm{MHz}$, respectively. This result for a proton radius
$r_p=0.862(11)\cdot10^{-13}\,\mathrm{cm}$, which is believed to be more
reliable, was not quite in agreement with the measured
value~\hyperlink{ref:14.13}{[13]} of $8172.86(5)\,\mathrm{MHz}$. But a later
calculation~\hyperlink{ref:14.14}{[14]} that adopts this proton radius and
includes terms of order $\alpha^2(Z\alpha)^5$ yields energy shifts for the
$1s$, $2s$, and $4s$ states in agreement with experiment. So apparently
quantum electrodynamics wins again.
