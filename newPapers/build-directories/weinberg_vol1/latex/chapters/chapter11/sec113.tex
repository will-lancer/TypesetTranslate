\subsection{Anomalous Magnetic Moments and Charge Radii}

For our next example, we shall calculate the shift in the magnetic moment
and the charge radius of an electron or muon due to lowest-order radiative
corrections. The one-loop graphs and renormalization corrections for the
photon--lepton vertex are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:11.4}. Of these graphs, those
involving insertions in incoming or outgoing lepton lines vanish because the
lepton is on the mass shell, as discussed in Section 10.3. The graph involving
an insertion in the external photon line is the vacuum polarization effect,
discussed in the previous section. This leaves one one-loop graph (the last in
Figure~\ref{fig:11.4}) that needs to be calculated here:
\begin{align}
\Gamma^\mu_{\text{1 loop}}(p',p)={}&\int d^4k
 \left[e\gamma^\rho(2\pi)^4\right]
 \left[{ -i\over(2\pi)^4}
 { -i(\sl{p}'-\sl{k})+m\over(p'-k)^2+m^2-i\epsilon}\right]
 \left[\gamma^\mu\right]\notag\\
&\quad\cdot
 \left[{ -i\over(2\pi)^4}
 { -i(\sl{p}-\sl{k})+m\over(p-k)^2+m^2-i\epsilon}\right]
 \left[e\gamma_\rho(2\pi)^4\right]
 \left[{ -i\over(2\pi)^4}{1\over k^2-i\epsilon}\right],
\tag{11.3.1}\label{eq:11.3.1}
\end{align}
where $p'$ and $p$ are the final and initial lepton four-momenta,
respectively. (The contribution of the vertex connecting the external photon
line and the internal lepton line is taken as $\gamma^\mu$, because a factor
$e(2\pi)^4$ was extracted in defining $\Gamma^\mu$.)

This integral has an obvious ultraviolet divergence, roughly like
$\int d^4k/(k^2)^2$. Unlike the case of the vacuum polarization, here we do
not need a fancy regularization procedure like dimensional regularization to
maintain the structure required by gauge invariance, because the photon is a
neutral particle and so the integral may be rendered finite by suitable
modifications of the photon propagator (for instance by including a factor
$M^2/(k^2+M^2)$ with a large cutoff mass $M$), without having to introduce
modifications elsewhere to maintain gauge invariance. In any case, as we
shall see, the anomalous magnetic moment and charge radii can be calculated
without encountering any ultraviolet divergences at all. In what follows we
shall leave the integrals for the vertex function in their infinite form,
with it being understood that if necessary any divergent integrals can be
expressed in terms of a cutoff mass $M$.

We start by combining denominators, using a repeated version of the Feynman
trick described in the Appendix to this chapter
\begin{equation}
{1\over ABC}=2\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\,
 [Ay+B(x-y)+C(1-x)]^{-3}.
\tag{11.3.2}\label{eq:11.3.2}
\end{equation}
Applied to the denominators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.1}, this gives
\begin{align}
&{1\over(p'-k)^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
 {1\over(p-k)^2+m^2-i\epsilon}{1\over k^2-i\epsilon}
\notag\\
&=2\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\,
 \Bigl[((p'-k)^2+m^2-i\epsilon)y
 +((p-k)^2+m^2-i\epsilon)(x-y)\notag\\
&\hspace{52mm}+(k^2-i\epsilon)(1-x)\Bigr]^{-3}
\notag\\
&=2\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\,
 \Bigl[(k-p'y-p(x-y))^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)-i\epsilon\Bigr]^{-3},
\tag{11.3.3}\label{eq:11.3.3}
\end{align}
where $q\equiv p-p'$ is the momentum transferred to the photon. Shifting the
variable of integration
\[
k\longrightarrow k+p'y+p(x-y)
\]
the integral \eqref{eq:11.3.1} becomes
\begin{align}
\Gamma^\mu_{\text{1 loop}}(p',p)={}&{2ie^2\over(2\pi)^4}
 \int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\int
 {d^4k\over[k^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)-i\epsilon]^3}\notag\\
&\quad\cdot\gamma^\rho
 \left[-i\bigl(\sl{p}'(1-y)-\sl{k}-\sl{p}(x-y)\bigr)+m\right]\gamma^\mu
\notag\\
&\quad\cdot
 \left[-i\bigl(\sl{p}(1-x+y)-\sl{k}-\sl{p}'y\bigr)+m\right]\gamma_\rho.
\tag{11.3.4}\label{eq:11.3.4}
\end{align}

Our next step is a Wick rotation. As explained in the previous section, the
$-i\epsilon$ in the denominator dictates that when we rotate the $k^0$
contour of integration to the imaginary axis we must rotate counterclockwise,
so that the integral over $k^0$ from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ is replaced with
an integral over imaginary values from $-i\infty$ to $+i\infty$, or
equivalently over real values of $k^4\equiv-ik^0$ from $-\infty$ to
$+\infty$. We also exploit the rotational symmetry of the denominator in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.4}; we drop terms in the denominator of odd order in $k$, replace
$k^\mu k^\nu$ with $\eta^{\mu\nu}\kappa^2/4$, and replace the volume element
$d^4k=i\,dk^1dk^2dk^3dk^4$ with $2i\pi^2\kappa^3d\kappa$, where $\kappa$
is the Euclidean length of the four-vector $k$. Putting this all together,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.4} now becomes
\begin{align}
\Gamma^\mu_{\text{1 loop}}(p',p)={}&{-4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}
 \int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\int_0^\infty\kappa^3d\kappa
 \Biggl\{-{\kappa^2\over4}
 \gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\mu\gamma_\sigma\gamma_\rho
\notag\\
&+\gamma^\rho
 \left[-i\bigl(\sl{p}'(1-y)-\sl{p}(x-y)\bigr)+m\right]\gamma^\mu
 \left[-i\bigl(\sl{p}(1-x+y)-\sl{p}'y\bigr)+m\right]\gamma_\rho
 \Biggr\}\notag\\
&\quad\cdot[\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^{-3}.
\tag{11.3.5}\label{eq:11.3.5}
\end{align}

We are interested here only in the matrix element
$\bar u'\Gamma^\mu u$ of the vertex function between Dirac spinors that
satisfy the relations
\[
\bar u'[i\sl{p}'+m]=0,\qquad [i\sl{p}+m]u=0.
\]
We can therefore simplify this expression by using the anticommutation
relations of the Dirac matrices to move all factors $\sl{p}'$ to the right and
all factors $\sl{p}$ to the left, replacing them when they arrive on the right
or left with $im$. After a straightforward but tedious calculation,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.5} then becomes
\begin{align}
\bar u'\Gamma^\mu_{\text{one loop}}(p',p)u={}&
 {-4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy
 \int_0^\infty\kappa^3d\kappa\,
 \bar u'\Bigl\{\gamma^\mu\bigl[-\kappa^2+2m^2(x^2-4x+2)
\notag\\
&\qquad+2q^2(y(x-y)+1-x)\bigr]\notag\\
&\qquad+4im p'{}^\mu(y-x+xy)+4im p^\mu(x^2-xy-y)\Bigr\}u
\notag\\
&\quad\cdot[\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^{-3}.
\tag{11.3.6}\label{eq:11.3.6}
\end{align}
We next exploit the symmetry of the final factor under the reflection
$y\longrightarrow x-y$. Under this reflection, the functions $y-x+xy$ and
$x^2-xy-y$ that multiply $p'{}^\mu$ and $p^\mu$ are interchanged, so both
may be replaced with their average:
\[
\tfrac12(y-x+xy)+\tfrac12(x^2-xy-y)=-\tfrac12x(1-x).
\]
This gives finally
\begin{align}
\bar u'\Gamma^\mu_{\text{one loop}}(p',p)u={}&
 {-4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy
 \int_0^\infty\kappa^3d\kappa\notag\\
&\cdot\bar u'\Bigl\{\gamma^\mu\bigl[-\kappa^2+2m^2(x^2-4x+2)
 +2q^2(y(x-y)+1-x)\bigr]\notag\\
&\qquad-2im(p'{}^\mu+p^\mu)x(1-x)\Bigr\}u
 [\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^{-3}.
\tag{11.3.7}\label{eq:11.3.7}
\end{align}
Note that $p^\mu$ and $p'{}^\mu$ now enter only in the combination
$p^\mu+p'{}^\mu$, as required by current conservation.

There are other diagrams that need to be taken into account. Of course,
there is the zeroth-order term $\gamma^\mu$ in $\Gamma^\mu$. The term
proportional to $Z_2-1$ in the correction term \eqref{eq:11.1.9} yields a term in
$\Gamma^\mu$
\begin{equation}
\Gamma^\mu_{\mathcal L_2}=(Z_2-1)\gamma^\mu.
\tag{11.3.8}\label{eq:11.3.8}
\end{equation}
Also, the effect of insertions of corrections to the external photon
propagator is a term:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma^\mu_{\text{vac pol}}(p',p)=
 {1\over(p'-p)^2-i\epsilon}\Pi^{\mu\nu}(p'-p)\gamma_\nu.
\tag{11.3.9}\label{eq:11.3.9}
\end{equation}
The form of each of these terms is in agreement with the general result
(10.6.10) (with $H(q^2)=0$)
\begin{equation}
\bar u'\Gamma^\mu(p',p)u
=\bar u'\left[\gamma^\mu F(q^2)-{i\over2m}(p+p')^\mu G(q^2)\right]u.
\tag{11.3.10}\label{eq:11.3.10}
\end{equation}
To order $e^2$, the form factors are
\begin{align}
F(q^2)={}&Z_2+\pi(q^2)+{4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}
 \int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\int_0^\infty\kappa^3d\kappa\notag\\
&\cdot{\kappa^2-2m^2(x^2-4x+2)-2q^2(y(x-y)+1-x)
 \over[\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^3},
\tag{11.3.11}\label{eq:11.3.11}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
G(q^2)={-4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}
 \int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\int_0^\infty
 {4m^2x(1-x)\kappa^3d\kappa
 \over[\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^3},
\tag{11.3.12}\label{eq:11.3.12}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(q^2)$ is the vacuum polarization function \eqref{eq:11.2.22}.

The integral for the form factor $G(q^2)$ is finite as it stands:
\begin{equation}
G(q^2)=-{e^2m^2\over4\pi^2}\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\,
 {x(1-x)\over m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)}.
\tag{11.3.13}\label{eq:11.3.13}
\end{equation}

This makes it easy to calculate the anomalous magnetic moment. As noted in
Section 10.6, it is only the $\gamma^\mu$ term that contributes to the magnetic
moment, so the effect of radiative corrections is to multiply the Dirac value
$e/2m$ of the magnetic moment by a factor $F(0)$. But the definition of $e$
as the true lepton charge requires that
\begin{equation}
F(0)+G(0)=1,
\tag{11.3.14}\label{eq:11.3.14}
\end{equation}
so the magnetic moment may be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\mu={e\over2m}\bigl(1-G(0)\bigr).
\tag{11.3.15}\label{eq:11.3.15}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.13}, we find
\begin{equation}
-G(0)={e^2\over8\pi^2}=0.001161.
\tag{11.3.16}\label{eq:11.3.16}
\end{equation}
This is the famous $\alpha/2\pi$ correction first calculated by Schwinger~\hyperref[ref:11.5]{[5]}.

Of course, this is only the first term in the radiative corrections to the
magnetic moment. Even in just the next order, fourth order in $e$, there are
so many terms that the calculations become quite complicated. However,
because of the large muon--electron mass ratio, there is one fourth-order term
in the magnetic moment of the muon that is somewhat larger than any of the
others. It arises from the insertion of an electron loop in the virtual photon
line of the second-order diagram, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:11.5}. The effect of this
electron loop is to change the photon propagator $1/k^2$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.1} to
$(1+\pi_e(k^2))/k^2$, where $\pi_e(k^2)$ is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.22}, but with
the mass $m$ taken as the electron mass:
\[
\pi_e(k^2)={e^2\over2\pi^2}\int_0^1x(1-x)
 \ln\left(1+{k^2x(1-x)\over m_e^2}\right)dx.
\]
Inspection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.12} shows that in calculating the muon magnetic
moment the effective cutoff on the virtual photon momentum $k$ is $m_\mu$.
The ratio $m_\mu/m_e$ is so large that for $k^2$ of order $m_\mu^2$ we may
approximate
\begin{equation}
\pi_e(k^2)\simeq{e^2\over2\pi^2}\int_0^1x(1-x)
 \ln(m_\mu^2/m_e^2)
 ={e^2\over12\pi^2}\ln(m_\mu^2/m_e^2),
\tag{11.3.17}\label{eq:11.3.17}
\end{equation}
with the neglected terms having coefficients of order unity in place of
$\ln(m_\mu^2/m_e^2)$. Since this is a constant, the change in $-G(0)$
produced by adding an electron loop in the virtual photon line is simply
given by multiplying our previous result \eqref{eq:11.3.16} for $-G(0)$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.17}, so that now
\begin{equation}
\mu_\mu={e\over2m_\mu}\left({e^2\over8\pi^2}
 +{e^4\over96\pi^4}\left[\ln{m_\mu^2\over m_e^2}+O(1)\right]\right).
\tag{11.3.18}\label{eq:11.3.18}
\end{equation}
(As we shall see in Volume II, this argument is a primitive version of the
method of the renormalization group.) The result \eqref{eq:11.3.18} may be compared
with the full fourth-order result~\hyperref[ref:11.6]{[6]}
\begin{align}
\mu_\mu={e\over2m_\mu}\Biggl\{{e^2\over8\pi^2}
 +{e^4\over96\pi^4}\Biggl[&\ln{m_\mu^2\over m_e^2}-{25\over6}
 +{197\over24}+{\pi^2\over2}+{9\zeta(3)\over2}
\notag\\[-2pt]
&-3\pi^2\ln2+O\!\left(\left({m_e\over m_\mu}\right)^2\right)\Biggr]
 \Biggr\}.
\tag{11.3.19}\label{eq:11.3.19}
\end{align}
It turns out that the $O(1)$ terms multiplying $e^4/96\pi^4$ add up to
$-6.137$, which is not very much smaller than
$\ln(m_\mu^2/m_e^2)=10.663$, so the approximation \eqref{eq:11.3.18} gives the
fourth-order terms only to a factor of order 2. The correct fourth-order
result \eqref{eq:11.3.19} gives $\mu_\mu=1.00116546e/2m_\mu$, in comparison with the
second-order result $\mu_\mu=1.001161e/2m_\mu$ and the current experimental
value~\hyperref[ref:11.7]{[7]}, $\mu_\mu=1.001165923(8)e/2m_\mu$.

Now let us turn to the other form factor. The integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.11} for
$F(q^2)$ has an ultraviolet divergence. However, in order to satisfy the
charge-non-renormalization condition \eqref{eq:11.3.14}, it is necessary that $Z_2$
take the value
\begin{align}
Z_2={}&1+{e^2\over8\pi^2}-{4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}
 \int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy\int_0^\infty\kappa^3d\kappa
 {\kappa^2-2m^2(x^2-4x+2)\over[\kappa^2+m^2x^2]^3}.
\tag{11.3.20}\label{eq:11.3.20}
\end{align}
(Recall that $\pi(0)=0$.) This is itself ultraviolet divergent, with an
infinite part
\begin{equation}
(Z_2-1)_\infty=-{e^2\over8\pi^2}\int^\infty{d\kappa\over\kappa}.
\tag{11.3.21}\label{eq:11.3.21}
\end{equation}
Inserting Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.20} back into Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.11} gives
\begin{align}
F(q^2)={}&1+{e^2\over8\pi^2}+\pi(q^2)
 +{4\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy
 \int_0^\infty\kappa^3d\kappa\notag\\
&\cdot\Biggl\{{\kappa^2-2m^2(x^2-4x+2)
 -2q^2[y(x-y)+1-x]\over[\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^3}
\notag\\
&\hspace{28mm}-{\kappa^2-2m^2(x^2-4x+2)
 \over[\kappa^2+m^2x^2]^3}\Biggr\}.
\tag{11.3.22}\label{eq:11.3.22}
\end{align}
The integral over $\kappa$ is now convergent:
\begin{align}
F(q^2)={}&1+{e^2\over8\pi^2}+\pi(q^2)
 +{2\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy
 \Biggl\{{-m^2[x^2-4x+2]-q^2[y(x-y)+1-x]
 \over m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)}\notag\\
&\hspace{18mm}+{x^2-4x+2\over x^2}
 -\ln\left[{m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)\over m^2x^2}\right]\Biggr\}.
\tag{11.3.23}\label{eq:11.3.23}
\end{align}
However, we see that the integral over $x$ and $y$ now diverges
logarithmically at $x=0$ and $y=0$, because there are two powers of $x$
and/or $y$ in the denominators, and just two differentials $dx\,dy$ in the
numerator. This divergence can be traced to the vanishing of the denominator
$[\kappa^2+m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)]^3$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.11} at $x=0$, $y=0$, and
$\kappa=0$. Because this infinity comes from the region of small rather than
large $\kappa$, it is termed an \emph{infrared divergence} rather than an
ultraviolet divergence.

We shall give a comprehensive treatment of the infrared divergences in
Chapter 13. It will be shown there that infrared divergences in the
cross-section for processes like electron--electron scattering, such as those
that are introduced by the infrared divergence in the electron form factor
$F(q^2)$, are cancelled when we include the emission of low-energy photons as
well as elastic scattering. Also, as we shall see in Chapter 14, when we
calculate radiative corrections to atomic energy levels the infrared
divergence in $F(q^2)$ is cut off because the bound electron is not exactly on
the free-particle mass shell. For the present we shall continue our
calculation by simply introducing a fictitious photon mass $\mu$ to cut off
the infrared divergence in $F(q^2)$, leaving it for Chapter 14 to see how to
use this result.

With a photon mass $\mu$, the denominator $k^2-i\epsilon$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.1}
would be replaced with $k^2+\mu^2-i\epsilon$. The effect would than be to add
a term $\mu^2(1-x)$ to the cubed quantity in the denominators of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.3.3}--\eqref{eq:11.3.7}, \eqref{eq:11.3.11}, \eqref{eq:11.3.20}, and \eqref{eq:11.3.22}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.23}
then is replaced with
\begin{align}
F(q^2)={}&1+{e^2\over8\pi^2}+\pi(q^2)
 +{2\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy
 \Biggl\{{-m^2[x^2-4x+2]-q^2[y(x-y)+1-x]
 \over m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)+\mu^2(1-x)}\notag\\
&\hspace{14mm}+{m^2[x^2-4x+2]\over m^2x^2+\mu^2(1-x)}
 -\ln\left[{m^2x^2+q^2y(x-y)+\mu^2(1-x)
 \over m^2x^2+\mu^2(1-x)}\right]\Biggr\}.
\tag{11.3.24}\label{eq:11.3.24}
\end{align}
This integral is now completely convergent. It can be expressed in terms of
Spence functions, but the result is not particularly illuminating. For our
purposes in Chapter 14, it will be sufficient to calculate the behavior of
$F(q^2)$ for small $q^2$. We already know from the Ward identity that
$F(0)=1-G(0)=1+e^2/8\pi^2$, so let us consider the first derivative $F'(q^2)$
at $q^2=0$. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.24}, this is
\begin{align}
F'(0)={}&\pi'(0)+{2\pi^2e^2\over(2\pi)^4}
 \int_0^1dx\int_0^xdy
 \Biggl\{{2y(x-y)+1-x\over m^2x^2+\mu^2(1-x)}\notag\\
&\hspace{25mm}+{m^2[x^2-4x+2]y(x-y)
 \over[m^2x^2+\mu^2(1-x)]^2}\Biggr\}.
\tag{11.3.25}\label{eq:11.3.25}
\end{align}
The vacuum polarization contribution is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.22} as
\begin{equation}
\pi'(0)={e^2\over60\pi^2m^2}.
\tag{11.3.26}\label{eq:11.3.26}
\end{equation}
Dropping all terms proportional to powers of $\mu/m$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.25}, we
then have\footnote{The $y$-integral is trivial. The $x$-integral is most
easily calculated in the limit $\mu\ll m$ by dividing the range of integration
into two parts, one from 0 to $s$, where $\mu/m\ll s\ll1$, and the second from
$s$ to 1.}
\begin{equation}
F'(0)={e^2\over24\pi^2m^2}
 \left[\ln\left({\mu^2\over m^2}\right)+{2\over5}+{1\over4}\right]
\tag{11.3.27}\label{eq:11.3.27}
\end{equation}
with the term $2/5$ the contribution of vacuum polarization. On the other
hand, Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.3.13} shows that $G(q^2)$ has a finite derivative at $q^2=0$,
\begin{equation}
G'(0)={e^2\over48\pi^2m^2}.
\tag{11.3.28}\label{eq:11.3.28}
\end{equation}

These results are most conveniently expressed in terms of the charge form
factor $F_1(q^2)$, defined by the alternative representation (10.6.15) of the
vertex function
\begin{align}
&\bar u(p',\sigma')\Gamma^\mu(p',p)u(p,\sigma)\notag\\
&\quad=\bar u(p',\sigma')\left[\gamma^\mu F_1(q^2)
 +\tfrac12i[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu](p'-p)_\nu F_2(q^2)\right]u(p,\sigma).
\tag{11.3.29}\label{eq:11.3.29}
\end{align}
According to Eqs. (10.6.17) and (10.6.18),
\begin{equation}
F_1(q^2)=F(q^2)+G(q^2).
\tag{11.3.30}\label{eq:11.3.30}
\end{equation}
For $|q^2|\ll m^2$, this form factor is approximately
\begin{equation}
F_1(q^2)\simeq1+{e^2\over24\pi^2}{q^2\over m^2}
 \left[\ln\left({\mu^2\over m^2}\right)+{2\over5}+{3\over4}\right].
\tag{11.3.31}\label{eq:11.3.31}
\end{equation}
This may be expressed in terms of a \emph{charge radius} $a$, defined by the
limiting behavior of the charge form factor for $q^2\longrightarrow0$:
\begin{equation}
F_1(q^2)\longrightarrow1-q^2a^2/6.
\tag{11.3.32}\label{eq:11.3.32}
\end{equation}
(This definition is motivated by the fact that the average of
$\exp(i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)$ over a spherical shell of radius $a$ goes as
$1-\mathbf q^2a^2/6$ for $\mathbf q^2a^2\ll1$.) We see that the charge
radius of the electron is given by
\begin{equation}
a^2=-{e^2\over4\pi^2m^2}
 \left[\ln\left({\mu^2\over m^2}\right)+{2\over5}+{3\over4}\right].
\tag{11.3.33}\label{eq:11.3.33}
