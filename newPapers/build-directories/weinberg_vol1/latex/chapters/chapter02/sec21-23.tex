The point of view of this book is that quantum field theory is the way
it is because (with certain qualifications) this is the only way to reconcile
quantum mechanics with special relativity. Therefore our first task is
to study how symmetries like Lorentz invariance appear in a quantum
setting.

\subsection{Quantum Mechanics}

First, some good news: quantum field theory is based on the same
quantum mechanics that was invented by Schr\"odinger, Heisenberg, Pauli,
Born, and others in 1925--26, and has been used ever since in atomic,
molecular, nuclear, and condensed matter physics. The reader is assumed
to be already familiar with quantum mechanics; this section provides
only the briefest of summaries of quantum mechanics, in the generalized
version of Dirac~\hyperref[ref:2.1]{[1]}.

\noindent
(i) Physical states are represented by rays in Hilbert space. A Hilbert
space is a kind of complex vector space; that is, if $\ket{\Phi}$ and
$\ket{\Psi}$ are vectors in the space (often called `state-vectors') then
so is $\xi\ket{\Phi}+\eta\ket{\Psi}$, for arbitrary complex numbers
$\xi,\eta$. It has a norm\footnote{We shall often use the Dirac bra-ket
notation: instead of $\braket{\Psi_1}{\Psi_2}$, we may write
$\braket{1}{2}$.}: for any pair of
vectors there is a complex
number $\braket{\Phi}{\Psi}$, such that
\begin{align}
  \braket{\Phi}{\Psi} &= \braket{\Psi}{\Phi}^{*},
  \tag{2.1.1}\label{eq:2.1.1}\\
  \bigl\langle \Phi \bigm| \xi_1\Psi_1+\xi_2\Psi_2\bigr\rangle
    &= \xi_1\braket{\Phi}{\Psi_1}+\xi_2\braket{\Phi}{\Psi_2},
  \tag{2.1.2}\label{eq:2.1.2}\\
  \bigl\langle \eta_1\Phi_1+\eta_2\Phi_2 \bigm| \Psi\bigr\rangle
    &= \eta_1^{*}\braket{\Phi_1}{\Psi}
      +\eta_2^{*}\braket{\Phi_2}{\Psi}.
  \tag{2.1.3}\label{eq:2.1.3}
\end{align}
The norm $\braket{\Psi}{\Psi}$ also satisfies a positivity condition:
$\braket{\Psi}{\Psi}\geq 0$, and vanishes if and only if
$\ket{\Psi}=0$. (There are also certain technical assumptions that allow
us to take limits of vectors within Hilbert space.) A ray is a set of
normalized vectors (i.e., $\braket{\Psi}{\Psi}=1$) with $\ket{\Psi}$ and
$\ket{\Psi'}$ belonging to the same ray if
$\ket{\Psi'}=\xi\ket{\Psi}$, where $\xi$ is an arbitrary complex number
with $|\xi|=1$.

\medskip
\noindent
(ii) Observables are represented by Hermitian operators. These are
mappings $\ket{\Psi}\to A\ket{\Psi}$ of Hilbert space into itself,
linear in the sense that
\begin{equation}
  A\bigl(\xi\ket{\Psi}+\eta\ket{\Phi}\bigr)
    =\xi A\ket{\Psi}+\eta A\ket{\Phi}
  \tag{2.1.4}\label{eq:2.1.4}
\end{equation}
and satisfying the reality condition $A^{\dagger}=A$, where for any
linear operator $A$ the adjoint $A^{\dagger}$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Phi}A^{\dagger}\ket{\Psi}
    \equiv \braket{A\Phi}{\Psi}
    =\bigl(\bra{\Psi}A\ket{\Phi}\bigr)^{*}.
  \tag{2.1.5}\label{eq:2.1.5}
\end{equation}
(There are also technical assumptions about the continuity of
$A\ket{\Psi}$ as a function of $\ket{\Psi}$.) A state represented by a ray
$\mathcal{R}$ has a definite value $\alpha$ for the observable represented
by an operator $A$ if vectors $\ket{\Psi}$ belonging to this ray are
eigenvectors of $A$ with eigenvalue $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
  A\ket{\Psi}=\alpha\ket{\Psi}
  \qquad\text{for $\ket{\Psi}$ in $\mathcal{R}$.}
  \tag{2.1.6}\label{eq:2.1.6}
\end{equation}
An elementary theorem tells us that for $A$ Hermitian, $\alpha$ is real,
and eigenvectors with different $\alpha$s are orthogonal.

\medskip
\noindent
(iii) If a system is in a state represented by a ray $\mathcal{R}$, and an
experiment is done to test whether it is in any one of the different states
represented by mutually orthogonal rays $\mathcal{R}_1,\mathcal{R}_2,\ldots$
(for instance, by measuring one or more observables) then the probability
of finding it in the state represented by $\mathcal{R}_n$ is
\begin{equation}
  P(\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}_n)
    =\bigl|\braket{\Psi}{\Psi_n}\bigr|^2,
  \tag{2.1.7}\label{eq:2.1.7}
\end{equation}
where $\ket{\Psi}$ and $\ket{\Psi_n}$ are any vectors belonging to rays
$\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}_n$, respectively. (A pair of rays is said
to be orthogonal if the state-vectors from the two rays have vanishing
scalar products.) Another elementary theorem gives a total probability
unity:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_n P(\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}_n)=1
  \tag{2.1.8}\label{eq:2.1.8}
\end{equation}
if the state-vectors $\ket{\Psi_n}$ form a complete set.

\subsection{Symmetries}

A symmetry transformation is a change in our point of view that does not
change the results of possible experiments. If an observer $O$ sees a system
in a state represented by a ray $\mathcal{R}$ or $\mathcal{R}_1$ or
$\mathcal{R}_2,\ldots$, then an equivalent observer $O'$ who looks at the
\emph{same} system will observe it in a different state, represented by a ray
$\mathcal{R}'$ or $\mathcal{R}'_1$ or $\mathcal{R}'_2,\ldots$, respectively,
but the two observers must find the same probabilities
\begin{equation}
  P(\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}_n)
    =P(\mathcal{R}'\to\mathcal{R}'_n).
  \tag{2.2.1}\label{eq:2.2.1}
\end{equation}
(This is only a necessary condition for a ray transformation to be a
symmetry; further conditions are discussed in the next chapter.) An
important theorem proved by Wigner~\hyperref[ref:2.2]{[2]} in the early 1930s
tells us that for any such transformation $\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}'$ of
rays we may define an operator $U$ on Hilbert space, such that if
$\ket{\Psi}$ is in ray $\mathcal{R}$ then $U\ket{\Psi}$ is in the ray
$\mathcal{R}'$, with $U$ either \emph{unitary} and \emph{linear}
\begin{align}
  \braket{U\Phi}{U\Psi} &= \braket{\Phi}{\Psi},
  \tag{2.2.2}\label{eq:2.2.2}\\
  U\bigl(\xi\ket{\Phi}+\eta\ket{\Psi}\bigr)
    &=\xi U\ket{\Phi}+\eta U\ket{\Psi}
  \tag{2.2.3}\label{eq:2.2.3}
\end{align}
or else \emph{antiunitary} and \emph{antilinear}
\begin{align}
  \braket{U\Phi}{U\Psi} &= \braket{\Phi}{\Psi}^{*},
  \tag{2.2.4}\label{eq:2.2.4}\\
  U\bigl(\xi\ket{\Phi}+\eta\ket{\Psi}\bigr)
    &=\xi^{*}U\ket{\Phi}+\eta^{*}U\ket{\Psi}.
  \tag{2.2.5}\label{eq:2.2.5}
\end{align}
Wigner's proof omits some steps. A more complete proof is given at the
end of this chapter in Appendix A.

As already mentioned, the adjoint of a linear operator $L$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Phi}L^{\dagger}\ket{\Psi}
    \equiv \braket{L\Phi}{\Psi}.
  \tag{2.2.6}\label{eq:2.2.6}
\end{equation}
This condition cannot be satisfied for an antilinear operator, because in
this case the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.6} would be linear in
$\ket{\Phi}$, while the left-hand side is antilinear in $\ket{\Phi}$.
Instead, the adjoint of an antilinear operator $A$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Phi}A^{\dagger}\ket{\Psi}
    \equiv \braket{A\Phi}{\Psi}^{*}
    =\bra{\Psi}A\ket{\Phi}.
  \tag{2.2.7}\label{eq:2.2.7}
\end{equation}
With this definition, the conditions for unitarity or antiunitarity both
take the form
\begin{equation}
  U^{\dagger}=U^{-1}.
  \tag{2.2.8}\label{eq:2.2.8}
\end{equation}

There is always a trivial symmetry transformation
$\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}$, represented by the identity operator
$U=\mathbf{1}$. This operator is, of course, unitary and linear. Continuity
then demands that any symmetry (like a rotation or translation or Lorentz
transformation) that can be made trivial by a continuous change of some
parameters (like angles or distances or velocities) must be represented
by a linear unitary operator $U$ rather than one that is antilinear and
antiunitary. (Symmetries represented by antiunitary antilinear operators
are less prominent in physics; they all involve a reversal in the direction
of time's flow. See Section 2.6.)

In particular, a symmetry transformation that is infinitesimally close
to being trivial can be represented by a linear unitary operator that is
infinitesimally close to the identity:
\begin{equation}
  U=\mathbf{1}+\ii\epsilon t
  \tag{2.2.9}\label{eq:2.2.9}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon$ a real infinitesimal. For this to be unitary and linear,
$t$ must be Hermitian and linear, so it is a candidate for an observable.
Indeed, most (and perhaps all) of the observables of physics, such as
angular momentum or momentum, arise in this way from symmetry
transformations.

The set of symmetry transformations has certain properties that define
it as a \emph{group}. If $T_1$ is a transformation that takes rays
$\mathcal{R}_n$ into $\mathcal{R}'_n$, and $T_2$ is another transformation
that takes $\mathcal{R}'_n$ into $\mathcal{R}''_n$, then the result of
performing both transformations is another symmetry transformation,
which we write $T_2T_1$, that takes $\mathcal{R}_n$ into
$\mathcal{R}''_n$. Also, a symmetry transformation $T$ which takes rays
$\mathcal{R}_n$ into $\mathcal{R}'_n$ has an inverse, written $T^{-1}$,
which takes $\mathcal{R}'_n$ into $\mathcal{R}_n$, and there is an identity
transformation, $T=\mathbf{1}$, which leaves rays unchanged.

The unitary or antiunitary operators $U(T)$ corresponding to these
symmetry transformations have properties that mirror this group structure,
but with a complication due to the fact that, unlike the symmetry
transformations themselves, the operators $U(T)$ act on vectors in the
Hilbert space, rather than on rays. If $T_1$ takes $\mathcal{R}_n$ into
$\mathcal{R}'_n$, then acting on a vector $\ket{\Psi_n}$ in the ray
$\mathcal{R}_n$, $U(T_1)$ must yield a vector $U(T_1)\ket{\Psi_n}$ in the
ray $\mathcal{R}'_n$, and if $T_2$ takes this ray into $\mathcal{R}''_n$,
then acting on $U(T_1)\ket{\Psi_n}$ it must yield a vector
$U(T_2)U(T_1)\ket{\Psi_n}$ in the ray $\mathcal{R}''_n$. But
$U(T_2T_1)\ket{\Psi_n}$ is also in this ray, so these vectors can differ
only by a phase $\phi_n(T_2,T_1)$
\begin{equation}
  U(T_2)U(T_1)\ket{\Psi_n}
    =e^{\ii\phi_n(T_2,T_1)}U(T_2T_1)\ket{\Psi_n}.
  \tag{2.2.10}\label{eq:2.2.10}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, with one significant exception, the linearity (or
antilinearity) of $U(T)$ tells us that these phases are independent of the
state $\ket{\Psi_n}$. Here is the proof. Consider any two different vectors
$\ket{\Psi_A},\ket{\Psi_B}$, which are not proportional to each other.
Then, applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.10} to the state
$\ket{\Psi_{AB}}=\ket{\Psi_A}+\ket{\Psi_B}$, we have
\begin{align}
  e^{\ii\phi_{AB}}U(T_2T_1)
    \bigl(\ket{\Psi_A}+\ket{\Psi_B}\bigr)
  &=U(T_2)U(T_1)\bigl(\ket{\Psi_A}+\ket{\Psi_B}\bigr)\notag\\
  &=U(T_2)U(T_1)\ket{\Psi_A}
    +U(T_2)U(T_1)\ket{\Psi_B}\notag\\
  &=e^{\ii\phi_A}U(T_2T_1)\ket{\Psi_A}
    +e^{\ii\phi_B}U(T_2T_1)\ket{\Psi_B}.
  \tag{2.2.11}\label{eq:2.2.11}
\end{align}
Any unitary or antiunitary operator has an inverse (its adjoint) which
is also unitary or antiunitary. Multiplying \eqref{eq:2.2.11} on the left with
$U^{-1}(T_2T_1)$, we have then
\begin{equation}
  e^{\pm \ii\phi_{AB}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi_A}+\ket{\Psi_B}\bigr)
    =e^{\pm \ii\phi_A}\ket{\Psi_A}
      +e^{\pm \ii\phi_B}\ket{\Psi_B},
  \tag{2.2.12}\label{eq:2.2.12}
\end{equation}
the upper and lower signs referring to $U(T_2T_1)$ unitary or antiunitary,
respectively. Since $\ket{\Psi_A}$ and $\ket{\Psi_B}$ are linearly
independent, this is only possible if
\begin{equation}
  e^{\ii\phi_{AB}}=e^{\ii\phi_A}=e^{\ii\phi_B}.
  \tag{2.2.13}\label{eq:2.2.13}
\end{equation}
So as promised, the phase in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.10} is independent of the
state-vector $\ket{\Psi_n}$, and therefore this can be written as an operator
relation
\begin{equation}
  U(T_2)U(T_1)=e^{\ii\phi(T_2,T_1)}U(T_2T_1).
  \tag{2.2.14}\label{eq:2.2.14}
\end{equation}
For $\phi=0$, this would say that the $U(T)$ furnish a representation of
the group of symmetry transformations. For general phases $\phi(T_2,T_1)$,
we have what is called a projective representation, or a representation
`up to a phase'. The structure of the Lie group cannot by itself tell us
whether physical state-vectors furnish an ordinary or a projective
representation, but as we shall see, it can tell us whether the group has
any intrinsically projective representations at all.

The exception to the argument that led to Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.14} is that it may
not be possible to prepare the system in a state represented by
$\ket{\Psi_A}+\ket{\Psi_B}$. For instance, it is widely believed to be
impossible to prepare a system in a superposition of two states whose total
angular momenta are integers and half-integers, respectively. In such
cases, we say that there is a `superselection rule' between different
classes of states~\hyperref[ref:2.3]{[3]}, and the phases $\phi(T_2,T_1)$ may
depend on which of these classes of states the operators $U(T_2)U(T_1)$
and $U(T_2T_1)$ act upon. We will have more to say about these phases and
projective representations in Section 2.7. As we shall see there, any
symmetry group with projective representations can always be enlarged
(without otherwise changing its physical implications) in such a way that
its representations can all be defined as non-projective, with $\phi=0$.
Until Section 2.7, we will just assume that this has been done, and take
$\phi=0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.14}.

There is a kind of group, known as a \emph{connected Lie group}, of
special importance in physics. These are groups of transformations
$T(\theta)$ that are described by a finite set of real continuous
parameters, say $\theta^a$, with each element of the group connected to
the identity by a path within the group. The group multiplication law then
takes the form
\begin{equation}
  T(\bar\theta)T(\theta)=T\bigl(f(\bar\theta,\theta)\bigr)
  \tag{2.2.15}\label{eq:2.2.15}
\end{equation}
with $f^a(\bar\theta,\theta)$ a function of the $\bar\theta$s and $\theta$s.
Taking $\theta^a=0$ as the coordinates of the identity, we must have
\begin{equation}
  f^a(\theta,0)=f^a(0,\theta)=\theta^a.
  \tag{2.2.16}\label{eq:2.2.16}
\end{equation}
As already mentioned, the transformations of such continuous groups must
be represented on the physical Hilbert space by unitary (rather than
antiunitary) operators $U(T(\theta))$. For a Lie group, these operators can
be represented in at least a finite neighborhood of the identity by a
power series
\begin{equation}
  U\bigl(T(\theta)\bigr)
    =\mathbf{1}+\ii\theta^a t_a+\frac{1}{2}\theta^b\theta^c t_{bc}+\cdots,
  \tag{2.2.17}\label{eq:2.2.17}
\end{equation}
where $t_a$, $t_{bc}=t_{cb}$, etc. are Hermitian operators independent of
the $\theta$s. Suppose that the $U(T(\theta))$ form an ordinary (i.e., not
projective) representation of this group of transformations, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  U\bigl(T(\bar\theta)\bigr)U\bigl(T(\theta)\bigr)
    =U\bigl(T(f(\bar\theta,\theta))\bigr).
  \tag{2.2.18}\label{eq:2.2.18}
\end{equation}
Let us see what this condition looks like when expanded in powers of
$\theta^a$ and $\bar\theta^a$. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.16}, the expansion of
$f^a(\bar\theta,\theta)$ to second order must take the form
\begin{equation}
  f^a(\bar\theta,\theta)
    =\theta^a+\bar\theta^a+f^a{}_{bc}\bar\theta^b\theta^c+\cdots
  \tag{2.2.19}\label{eq:2.2.19}
\end{equation}
with real coefficients $f^a{}_{bc}$. (The presence of any terms of order
$\theta^2$ or $\bar\theta^2$ would violate Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.16}.) Then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.18} reads
\begin{align}
  &\left[\mathbf{1}+\ii\bar\theta^a t_a
    +\frac{1}{2}\bar\theta^b\bar\theta^c t_{bc}+\cdots\right]
  \left[\mathbf{1}+\ii\theta^a t_a
    +\frac{1}{2}\theta^b\theta^c t_{bc}+\cdots\right]
  \notag\\
  &\quad=\mathbf{1}
    +\ii\bigl(\theta^a+\bar\theta^a
      +f^a{}_{bc}\bar\theta^b\theta^c+\cdots\bigr)t_a
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\quad
    +\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\theta^b+\bar\theta^b+\cdots\bigr)
      \bigl(\theta^c+\bar\theta^c+\cdots\bigr)t_{bc}+\cdots.
  \tag{2.2.20}\label{eq:2.2.20}
\end{align}
The terms of order $1$, $\theta$, $\bar\theta$, $\theta^2$, and
$\bar\theta^2$ automatically match on both sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.20}, but
from the $\bar\theta\theta$ terms we obtain a non-trivial condition
\begin{equation}
  t_{bc}=-t_b t_c-\ii f^a{}_{bc}t_a.
  \tag{2.2.21}\label{eq:2.2.21}
\end{equation}
This shows that if we are given the structure of the group, i.e., the
function $f(\theta,\bar\theta)$, and hence its quadratic coefficient
$f^a{}_{bc}$, we can calculate the second-order terms in $U(T(\theta))$
from the generators $t_a$ appearing in the first-order terms. However,
there is a consistency condition: the operator $t_{bc}$ must be
\emph{symmetric} in $b$ and $c$ (because it is the second derivative of
$U(T(\theta))$ with respect to $\theta^b$ and $\theta^c$) so Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.21}
requires that
\begin{equation}
  [t_b,t_c]=\ii C^a{}_{bc}t_a,
  \tag{2.2.22}\label{eq:2.2.22}
\end{equation}
where $C^a{}_{bc}$ are a set of real constants known as \emph{structure
constants}
\begin{equation}
  C^a{}_{bc}\equiv-f^a{}_{bc}+f^a{}_{cb}.
  \tag{2.2.23}\label{eq:2.2.23}
\end{equation}
Such a set of commutation relations is known as a \emph{Lie algebra}. In
Section 2.7 we will prove in effect that the commutation relation \eqref{eq:2.2.22}
is the single condition needed to ensure that this process can be
continued: the complete power series for $U(T(\theta))$ may be calculated
from an infinite sequence of relations like Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.21}, provided we know
the first-order terms, the generators $t_a$. This does not necessarily mean
that the operators $U(T(\theta))$ are uniquely determined for all
$\theta^a$ if we know the $t_a$, but it does mean that the $U(T(\theta))$
are uniquely determined in at least a finite neighborhood of the
coordinates $\theta^a=0$ of the identity, in such a way that Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.15}
is satisfied if $\theta$, $\bar\theta$, and $f(\theta,\bar\theta)$ are in
this neighborhood. The extension to all $\theta^a$ is discussed in
Section 2.7.

There is a special case of some importance, that we will encounter again
and again. Suppose that the function $f(\theta,\bar\theta)$ (perhaps just
for some subset of the coordinates $\theta^a$) is simply additive
\begin{equation}
  f^a(\theta,\bar\theta)=\theta^a+\bar\theta^a.
  \tag{2.2.24}\label{eq:2.2.24}
\end{equation}
This is the case for instance for translations in spacetime, or for
rotations about any one fixed axis (though \emph{not} for both together).
Then the coefficients $f^a{}_{bc}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.2.19} vanish, and so do the
structure constants \eqref{eq:2.2.23}. The generators then all commute
\begin{equation}
  [t_b,t_c]=0.
  \tag{2.2.25}\label{eq:2.2.25}
\end{equation}
Such a group is called \emph{Abelian}. In this case, it is easy to
calculate $U(T(\theta))$ for all $\theta^a$. From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.2.18} and
\eqref{eq:2.2.24}, we have for any integer $N$
\begin{equation*}
  U\bigl(T(\theta)\bigr)
    =\left[U\left(T\left(\frac{\theta}{N}\right)\right)\right]^N.
\end{equation*}
Letting $N\to\infty$, and keeping only the first-order term in
$U(T(\theta/N))$, we have then
\begin{equation*}
  U\bigl(T(\theta)\bigr)
    =\lim_{N\to\infty}
      \left[\mathbf{1}+\frac{\ii}{N}\theta^a t_a\right]^N
\end{equation*}
and hence
\begin{equation}
  U\bigl(T(\theta)\bigr)=\exp(\ii t_a\theta^a).
  \tag{2.2.26}\label{eq:2.2.26}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Quantum Lorentz Transformations}

Einstein's principle of relativity states the equivalence of certain
`inertial' frames of reference. It is distinguished from the Galilean
principle of relativity, obeyed by Newtonian mechanics, by the
transformation connecting coordinate systems in different inertial frames.
If $x^\mu$ are the coordinates in one inertial frame (with $x^0=t$ a time
coordinate and $x^1,x^2,x^3$ Cartesian space coordinates, the speed of
light being set equal to unity) then in any other inertial frame, the
coordinates $x'{}^\mu$ must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x'{}^\mu \dd x'{}^\nu
    =\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x^\mu \dd x^\nu
  \tag{2.3.1}\label{eq:2.3.1}
\end{equation}
or equivalently
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\mu\nu}
    \frac{\partial x'{}^\mu}{\partial x^\rho}
    \frac{\partial x'{}^\nu}{\partial x^\sigma}
    =\eta_{\rho\sigma}.
  \tag{2.3.2}\label{eq:2.3.2}
\end{equation}
Here $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ is the diagonal matrix, with elements
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{00}=-1,
  \qquad \eta_{11}=\eta_{22}=\eta_{33}=+1,
  \tag{2.3.3}\label{eq:2.3.3}
\end{equation}
and the summation convention is in force: we sum over any index like
$\mu$ and $\nu$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.2}, which appears twice in the same term, once
upstairs and once downstairs. These transformations have the special
property that the speed of light is the same (in our units, equal to unity)
in all inertial frames;\footnote{There is a larger class of coordinate
transformations, known as \emph{conformal transformations}, for which
$\eta_{\mu\nu}\dd x'{}^\mu \dd x'{}^\nu$ is proportional though generally not
equal to $\eta_{\mu\nu}\dd x^\mu \dd x^\nu$, and which therefore also leave the
speed of light invariant. Conformal invariance in two dimensions has
proved enormously important in string theory and statistical mechanics,
but the physical relevance of these conformal transformations in four
spacetime dimensions is not yet clear.}
a light wave travelling at unit
speed satisfies $|\dd\mathbf{x}/\dd t|=1$, or in other words
$\eta_{\mu\nu}\dd x^\mu \dd x^\nu=\dd\mathbf{x}^2-\dd t^2=0$, from which it follows
that also $\eta_{\mu\nu}\dd x'{}^\mu \dd x'{}^\nu=0$, and hence
$|\dd\mathbf{x}'/\dd t'|=1$.

Any coordinate transformation $x^\mu\to x'{}^\mu$ that satisfies
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.2} is \emph{linear}~\hyperref[ref:2.3a]{[3a]}
\begin{equation}
  x'{}^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}x^\nu+a^\mu
  \tag{2.3.4}\label{eq:2.3.4}
\end{equation}
with $a^\mu$ arbitrary constants, and $\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ a constant
matrix satisfying the conditions
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\sigma}
    =\eta_{\rho\sigma}.
  \tag{2.3.5}\label{eq:2.3.5}
\end{equation}
For some purposes, it is useful to write the Lorentz transformation
condition in a different way. The matrix $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ has an inverse,
written $\eta^{\mu\nu}$, which happens to have the same components: it is
diagonal, with $\eta^{00}=-1$,
$\eta^{11}=\eta^{22}=\eta^{33}=+1$. Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.5} with
$\eta^{\sigma\tau}\Lambda^\kappa{}_{\tau}$ and inserting parentheses
judiciously, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}
  \bigl(\Lambda^\nu{}_{\sigma}\Lambda^\kappa{}_{\tau}
    \eta^{\sigma\tau}\bigr)
  =\Lambda^\kappa{}_{\rho}
  =\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta^{\nu\kappa}\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}.
\end{equation*}
Multiplying with the inverse of the matrix
$\eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}$ then gives
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda^\nu{}_{\sigma}\Lambda^\kappa{}_{\tau}
    \eta^{\sigma\tau}=\eta^{\nu\kappa}.
  \tag{2.3.6}\label{eq:2.3.6}
\end{equation}

These transformations form a group. If we first perform a Lorentz
transformation \eqref{eq:2.3.4}, and then a second Lorentz transformation
$x'{}^\mu\to x''{}^\mu$, with
\begin{equation*}
  x''{}^\mu=\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}x'{}^\rho+\bar a^\mu
  =\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}
    \bigl(\Lambda^\rho{}_{\nu}x^\nu+a^\rho\bigr)+\bar a^\mu
\end{equation*}
then the effect is the same as the Lorentz transformation
$x^\mu\to x''{}^\mu$, with
\begin{equation}
  x''{}^\mu
    =\bigl(\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}\Lambda^\rho{}_{\nu}\bigr)x^\nu
      +\bigl(\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}a^\rho+\bar a^\mu\bigr).
  \tag{2.3.7}\label{eq:2.3.7}
\end{equation}
(Note that if $\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ and $\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ both
satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.5}, then so does
$\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}\Lambda^\rho{}_{\nu}$, so this is a Lorentz
transformation. The bar is used here just to distinguish one Lorentz
transformation from the other.) The transformations $T(\Lambda,a)$
induced on physical states therefore satisfy the composition rule
\begin{equation}
  T(\bar\Lambda,\bar a)T(\Lambda,a)
    =T(\bar\Lambda\Lambda,\bar\Lambda a+\bar a).
  \tag{2.3.8}\label{eq:2.3.8}
\end{equation}
Taking the determinant of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.5} gives
\begin{equation}
  (\operatorname{Det}\Lambda)^2=1
  \tag{2.3.9}\label{eq:2.3.9}
\end{equation}
so $\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ has an inverse,
$(\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_{\mu}$, which we see from Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.5} takes the form
\begin{equation}
  (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_{\mu}
    =\Lambda_{\mu}{}^\nu
    \equiv\eta_{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}\Lambda^\rho{}_{\sigma}.
  \tag{2.3.10}\label{eq:2.3.10}
\end{equation}
The inverse of the transformation $T(\Lambda,a)$ is seen from Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.8}
to be $T(\Lambda^{-1},-\Lambda^{-1}a)$, and, of course, the identity
transformation is $T(\mathbf{1},0)$.

In accordance with the discussion in the previous section, the
transformations $T(\Lambda,a)$ induce a unitary linear transformation on
vectors in the physical Hilbert space
\begin{equation*}
  \ket{\Psi}\longrightarrow U(\Lambda,a)\ket{\Psi}.
\end{equation*}
The operators $U$ satisfy a composition rule
\begin{equation}
  U(\bar\Lambda,\bar a)U(\Lambda,a)
    =U(\bar\Lambda\Lambda,\bar\Lambda a+\bar a).
  \tag{2.3.11}\label{eq:2.3.11}
\end{equation}
(As already mentioned, to avoid the appearance of a phase factor on the
right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.11}, it is, in general, necessary to enlarge
the Lorentz group. The appropriate enlargement is described in
Section 2.7.)

The whole group of transformations $T(\Lambda,a)$ is properly known as
the \emph{Poincar\'e group}. It has
a number of important subgroups. First, those transformations with
$a^\mu=0$ obviously form a subgroup, with
\begin{equation}
  T(\bar\Lambda,0)T(\Lambda,0)=T(\bar\Lambda\Lambda,0),
  \tag{2.3.12}\label{eq:2.3.12}
\end{equation}
known as the \emph{homogeneous Lorentz group}. Also, we note from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.9} that either $\operatorname{Det}\Lambda=+1$ or
$\operatorname{Det}\Lambda=-1$; those transformations with
$\operatorname{Det}\Lambda=+1$ obviously form a subgroup of either the
homogeneous Lorentz group or the Poincar\'e group. Further, from the
$00$-components of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.3.5} and \eqref{eq:2.3.6}, we have
\begin{equation}
  (\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2
    =1+\Lambda^i{}_{0}\Lambda^i{}_{0}
    =1+\Lambda^0{}_{i}\Lambda^0{}_{i},
  \tag{2.3.13}\label{eq:2.3.13}
\end{equation}
with $i$ summed over the values 1, 2, and 3. We see that either
$\Lambda^0{}_{0}\geq+1$ or $\Lambda^0{}_{0}\leq-1$. Those transformations
with $\Lambda^0{}_{0}\geq+1$ form a subgroup. Note that if
$\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ and $\bar\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ are two such
$\Lambda$s, then
\begin{equation*}
  (\bar\Lambda\Lambda)^0{}_{0}
    =\bar\Lambda^0{}_{0}\Lambda^0{}_{0}
      +\bar\Lambda^0{}_{1}\Lambda^1{}_{0}
      +\bar\Lambda^0{}_{2}\Lambda^2{}_{0}
      +\bar\Lambda^0{}_{3}\Lambda^3{}_{0}.
\end{equation*}
But Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.13} shows that the three-vector
$(\Lambda^1{}_{0},\allowbreak\Lambda^2{}_{0},\allowbreak\Lambda^3{}_{0})$
has length
$\sqrt{(\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2-1}$, and similarly the three-vector\hfill\break
$(\bar\Lambda^0{}_{1},\allowbreak\bar\Lambda^0{}_{2},\allowbreak
\bar\Lambda^0{}_{3})$ has length
$\sqrt{(\bar\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2-1}$, so the scalar product of these
two three-vectors is bounded by
\begin{equation}
  \left|
    \bar\Lambda^0{}_{1}\Lambda^1{}_{0}
      +\bar\Lambda^0{}_{2}\Lambda^2{}_{0}
      +\bar\Lambda^0{}_{3}\Lambda^3{}_{0}
  \right|
  \leq
  \sqrt{(\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2-1}
  \sqrt{(\bar\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2-1},
  \tag{2.3.14}\label{eq:2.3.14}
\end{equation}
and so
\begin{equation*}
  (\bar\Lambda\Lambda)^0{}_{0}
  \geq \bar\Lambda^0{}_{0}\Lambda^0{}_{0}
    -\sqrt{(\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2-1}
     \sqrt{(\bar\Lambda^0{}_{0})^2-1}
  \geq 1.
\end{equation*}
The subgroup of Lorentz transformations with
$\operatorname{Det}\Lambda=+1$ and $\Lambda^0{}_{0}\geq+1$ is known as
the \emph{proper orthochronous Lorentz group}. Since it is not possible by
a continuous change of parameters to jump from
$\operatorname{Det}\Lambda=+1$ to $\operatorname{Det}\Lambda=-1$, or
from $\Lambda^0{}_{0}\geq+1$ to $\Lambda^0{}_{0}\leq-1$, any Lorentz
transformation that can be obtained from the identity by a continuous
change of parameters must have $\operatorname{Det}\Lambda$ and
$\Lambda^0{}_{0}$ of the same sign as for the identity, and hence must
belong to the proper orthochronous Lorentz group.

Any Lorentz transformation is either proper and orthochronous, or may be
written as the product of an element of the proper orthochronous Lorentz
group with one of the discrete transformations $\mathcal{P}$ or
$\mathcal{T}$ or $\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$, where $\mathcal{P}$ is the space
inversion, whose non-zero elements are
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{P}^0{}_{0}=1,
  \qquad
  \mathcal{P}^1{}_{1}=\mathcal{P}^2{}_{2}=\mathcal{P}^3{}_{3}=-1,
  \tag{2.3.15}\label{eq:2.3.15}
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal{T}$ is the time-reversal matrix, whose non-zero elements are
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}^0{}_{0}=-1,
  \qquad
  \mathcal{T}^1{}_{1}=\mathcal{T}^2{}_{2}=\mathcal{T}^3{}_{3}=1.
  \tag{2.3.16}\label{eq:2.3.16}
\end{equation}
Thus the study of the whole Lorentz group reduces to the study of its
proper orthochronous subgroup, plus space inversion and time-reversal. We
will consider space inversion and time-reversal separately in Section 2.6.
Until then, we will deal only with the homogeneous or inhomogeneous proper
orthochronous Lorentz group.
