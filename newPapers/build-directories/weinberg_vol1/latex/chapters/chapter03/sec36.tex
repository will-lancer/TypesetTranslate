\subsection{Implications of Unitarity}

The unitarity of the $S$-matrix imposes an interesting and useful condition relating the amplitude
$M_{\alpha\alpha}$ for forward scattering in an arbitrary multi-particle state $\alpha$ to the
total rate for all reactions in that state. Recall that in the general case, where the state
$\beta$ may or may not be the same as the state $\alpha$, the $S$-matrix may be written as in
\eqref{eq:3.3.2}:
\[
 S_{\beta\alpha}=\delta(\beta-\alpha)-2\pi i\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)M_{\beta\alpha}.
\]
The unitarity condition then gives
\begin{align*}
 \delta(\gamma-\alpha)&=\int d\beta\,S^*_{\beta\gamma}S_{\beta\alpha}\\
 &=\delta(\gamma-\alpha)-2\pi i\delta^4(p_\gamma-p_\alpha)M_{\gamma\alpha}\\
 &\quad+2\pi i\delta^4(p_\gamma-p_\alpha)M^*_{\alpha\gamma}
 +4\pi^2\int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\gamma)
 \delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)M^*_{\beta\gamma}M_{\beta\alpha}.
\end{align*}
Cancelling the term $\delta(\gamma-\alpha)$ and a factor
$2\pi\delta^4(p_\gamma-p_\alpha)$, we find that for $p_\gamma=p_\alpha$
\begin{equation}
 0=-iM_{\gamma\alpha}+iM^*_{\alpha\gamma}
 +2\pi\int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)M^*_{\beta\gamma}M_{\beta\alpha}.
 \tag{3.6.1}\label{eq:3.6.1}
\end{equation}
This is most useful in the special case $\alpha=\gamma$, where it reads
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Im}M_{\alpha\alpha}
 =-\pi\int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)|M_{\beta\alpha}|^2.
 \tag{3.6.2}\label{eq:3.6.2}
\end{equation}
Using Eq. \eqref{eq:3.4.11}, this can be expressed as a formula for the total rate for all reactions
produced by an initial state $\alpha$ in a volume $V$
\begin{align}
 \Gamma_\alpha&\equiv\int d\beta\,\frac{d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)}{d\beta}\notag\\
 &=(2\pi)^{3N_\alpha-2}V^{1-N_\alpha}
 \int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)|M_{\beta\alpha}|^2\notag\\
 &=-\frac{1}{\pi}(2\pi)^{3N_\alpha-2}V^{1-N_\alpha}
 \operatorname{Im}M_{\alpha\alpha}.
 \tag{3.6.3}\label{eq:3.6.3}
\end{align}
In particular, where $\alpha$ is a two-particle state, this can be written
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Im}M_{\alpha\alpha}=-u_\alpha\sigma_\alpha/16\pi^3,
 \tag{3.6.4}\label{eq:3.6.4}
\end{equation}
where $u_\alpha$ is the relative velocity \eqref{eq:3.4.17} in state $\alpha$, and $\sigma_\alpha$ is the
\emph{total} cross-section in this state, given by \eqref{eq:3.4.15} as
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_\alpha\equiv\int d\beta\,d\sigma(\alpha\to\beta)/d\beta
 =(2\pi)^4u_\alpha^{-1}\int d\beta\,|M_{\beta\alpha}|^2
 \delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha).
 \tag{3.6.5}\label{eq:3.6.5}
\end{equation}
This is usually expressed in a slightly different way, in terms of a \emph{scattering amplitude}
$f(\alpha\to\beta)$. Eq. \eqref{eq:3.4.30} shows that the differential cross-section for \emph{two-body}
scattering in the center-of-mass frame is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\sigma(\alpha\to\beta)}{d\Omega}
 =\frac{(2\pi)^4k'E'_1E'_2E_1E_2}{kE^2}|M_{\beta\alpha}|^2,
 \tag{3.6.6}\label{eq:3.6.6}
\end{equation}
where $k'$ and $k$ are the magnitudes of the momenta in the initial and final states. We therefore
define the scattering amplitude as\footnote{The phase of $f$ is conventional, and is motivated by
the wave mechanical interpretation~\hyperref[ref:3.21]{[21]} of $f$ as the coefficient of the outgoing wave in the
solution of the time-independent Schrödinger equation. The normalization of $f$ used here is
slightly unconventional for inelastic scattering; usually $f$ is defined so that a ratio of final
and initial velocities appears in the formula for the differential cross-section.}
\begin{equation}
 f(\alpha\to\beta)\equiv-\frac{4\pi^2}{E}
 \sqrt{\frac{k'E'_1E'_2E_1E_2}{k}}\,M_{\beta\alpha},
 \tag{3.6.7}\label{eq:3.6.7}
\end{equation}
so that the differential cross-section is simply
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\sigma(\alpha\to\beta)}{d\Omega}=|f(\alpha\to\beta)|^2.
 \tag{3.6.8}\label{eq:3.6.8}
\end{equation}
In particular, for \emph{elastic} two-body scattering, we have
\begin{equation}
 f(\alpha\to\beta)\equiv-\frac{4\pi^2E_1E_2}{E}M_{\beta\alpha}.
 \tag{3.6.9}\label{eq:3.6.9}
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:3.4.18} for the relative velocity $u_\alpha$, the unitarity prediction \eqref{eq:3.6.3} now reads
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Im}f(\alpha\to\alpha)=\frac{k}{4\pi}\sigma_\alpha.
 \tag{3.6.10}\label{eq:3.6.10}
\end{equation}
This form of the unitarity condition \eqref{eq:3.6.3} is known as the \emph{optical theorem}~\hyperref[ref:3.22]{[22]}.

There is a pretty consequence of the optical theorem that tells much about the pattern of
scattering at high energy. The scattering amplitude $f$ may be expected to be a smooth function
of angle, so there must be some solid angle $\Delta\Omega$ within which $|f|^2$ has nearly the
same value (say, within a factor 2) as in the forward direction. The total cross-section is then
bounded by
\[
 \sigma_\alpha\geq\int|f|^2d\Omega
 \geq\frac{1}{2}|f(\alpha\to\alpha)|^2\Delta\Omega
 \geq\frac{1}{2}|\operatorname{Im}f(\alpha\to\alpha)|^2\Delta\Omega.
\]
Using Eq. \eqref{eq:3.6.10} then yields an upper bound on $\Delta\Omega$
\begin{equation}
 \Delta\Omega\leq\frac{32\pi^2}{k^2\sigma_\alpha}.
 \tag{3.6.11}\label{eq:3.6.11}
\end{equation}
As we shall see in the next section, total cross-sections are usually expected to approach
constants or grow slowly at high energy, so Eq. \eqref{eq:3.6.11} shows that the solid angle around the
forward direction within which the differential cross-section is roughly constant shrinks at
least as fast as $1/k^2$ for $k\to\infty$. This increasingly narrow peak in the forward direction
at high energies is known as a \emph{diffraction peak}.

Returning now to the general case of reactions involving arbitrary numbers of particles, we can
use Eq. \eqref{eq:3.6.2} together with CPT invariance to say something about the relations for total
interaction rates of particles and antiparticles. Because CPT is antiunitary, its conservation
does not in general imply any simple relation between a process $\alpha\to\beta$ and the process
with particles replaced with their antiparticles. Instead, it provides a relation between a
process and the \emph{inverse} process involving antiparticles: we can use the same arguments that
allowed us to deduce \eqref{eq:3.3.46} from time-reversal invariance to show that CPT invariance requires
the $S$-matrix to satisfy the condition
\begin{equation}
 S_{\beta,\alpha}=S_{\mathcal{CPT}\alpha,\mathcal{CPT}\beta},
 \tag{3.6.12}\label{eq:3.6.12}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{CPT}$ indicates that we must reverse all spin $z$-components, change all particles
into their corresponding antiparticles, and multiply the matrix element by various phase factors
for the particles in the initial state and by their complex conjugates for the particles in the
final state. Since CPT invariance also requires particles to have the same masses as their
corresponding antiparticles, the same relation holds for the coefficient of
$\delta^4(p_\alpha-p_\beta)$ in $S_{\beta\alpha}$:
\begin{equation}
 M_{\beta,\alpha}=M_{\mathcal{CPT}\alpha,\mathcal{CPT}\beta}.
 \tag{3.6.13}\label{eq:3.6.13}
\end{equation}
In particular, when the initial and final states are the same the phase factors all cancel, and
Eq. \eqref{eq:3.6.13} says that
\begin{align}
 &M_{p_1\sigma_1n_1;\,p_2\sigma_2n_2;\,\ldots,,
 p_1\sigma_1n_1;\,p_2\sigma_2n_2;\,\ldots}\notag\\
 &\qquad=M_{p_1,-\sigma_1,n_1^c;\,p_2,-\sigma_2,n_2^c;\,\ldots,,
 p_1,-\sigma_1,n_1^c;\,p_2,-\sigma_2,n_2^c;\,\ldots},
 \tag{3.6.14}\label{eq:3.6.14}
\end{align}
where a superscript $c$ on $n$ indicates the antiparticle of $n$. The generalized optical theorem
\eqref{eq:3.6.2} then tells us that \emph{the total reaction rate from an initial state consisting of some
set of particles is the same as for an initial state consisting of the corresponding antiparticles
with spins reversed}:
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma_{p_1\sigma_1n_1;\,p_2\sigma_2n_2;\,\ldots}
 =\Gamma_{p_1,-\sigma_1,n_1^c;\,p_2,-\sigma_2,n_2^c;\,\ldots}.
 \tag{3.6.15}\label{eq:3.6.15}
\end{equation}
In particular, applying this to one-particle states, we see that the decay rate of any particle
equals the decay rate of the antiparticle with reversed spin. Rotational invariance does not allow
particle decay rates to depend on the spin $z$-component of the decaying particle, so a special
case of the general result \eqref{eq:3.6.15} is that unstable particles and their corresponding antiparticles
have precisely the same lifetimes.

The same argument that led from the unitarity condition $S^\dagger S=\mathbf{1}$ to our result
\eqref{eq:3.6.2} also allows us to use the other unitarity relation $SS^\dagger=\mathbf{1}$ to derive the
result
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Im}M_{\alpha\alpha}
 =-\pi\int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)|M_{\alpha\beta}|^2.
 \tag{3.6.16}\label{eq:3.6.16}
\end{equation}
Putting this together with Eq. \eqref{eq:3.6.2} then yields the reciprocity relation
\begin{equation}
 \int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)|M_{\beta\alpha}|^2
 =\int d\beta\,\delta^4(p_\beta-p_\alpha)|M_{\alpha\beta}|^2
 \tag{3.6.17}\label{eq:3.6.17}
\end{equation}
or in other words
\begin{equation}
 \int d\beta\,\frac{d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)}{d\beta}
 =\int d\beta\,\frac{d\Gamma(\beta\to\alpha)}{d\alpha}.
 \tag{3.6.18}\label{eq:3.6.18}
\end{equation}
This result can be used in deriving some of the most important results of kinetic theory~\hyperref[ref:3.23]{[23]}.
If $P_\alpha d\alpha$ is the probability of finding the system in a volume $d\alpha$ of the space
of multi-particle states $\ket{\alpha}$, then the rate of decrease in $P_\alpha$ due to transitions
to all other states is $P_\alpha\int d\beta\,d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)/d\beta$, while the rate of
increase of $P_\alpha$ due to transitions from all other states is
$\int d\beta\,P_\beta\,d\Gamma(\beta\to\alpha)/d\alpha$; the rate of change of $P_\alpha$ is then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dP_\alpha}{dt}=\int d\beta\,P_\beta\frac{d\Gamma(\beta\to\alpha)}{d\alpha}
 -P_\alpha\int d\beta\,\frac{d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)}{d\beta}.
 \tag{3.6.19}\label{eq:3.6.19}
\end{equation}
It follows immediately that $\int P_\alpha d\alpha$ is time-independent. (Just interchange the
labelling of the integration variables in the integral in the second term in Eq. \eqref{eq:3.6.19}.)
On the other hand, the rate of change of the entropy $-\int d\alpha\,P_\alpha\ln P_\alpha$ is
\[
 -\frac{d}{dt}\int d\alpha\,P_\alpha\ln P_\alpha
 =-\int d\alpha\int d\beta\,(\ln P_\alpha+1)
 \left[P_\beta\frac{d\Gamma(\beta\to\alpha)}{d\alpha}
 -P_\alpha\frac{d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)}{d\beta}\right].
\]
Interchanging the labelling of the integration variables in the second term, this may be written
\[
 -\frac{d}{dt}\int d\alpha\,P_\alpha\ln P_\alpha
 =\int d\alpha\int d\beta\,P_\beta\ln\left(\frac{P_\beta}{P_\alpha}\right)
 \frac{d\Gamma(\beta\to\alpha)}{d\alpha}.
\]

$P_\beta\ln(P_\beta/P_\alpha)$
