$K^+\to\pi^++\pi^++\pi^-$.

\subsection{Perturbation Theory}

The technique that has historically been most useful in calculating the $S$-matrix is
perturbation theory, an expansion in powers of the interaction term $H_{\mathrm{int}}$ in the
Hamiltonian $H=H_0+H_{\mathrm{int}}$. Eqs. \eqref{eq:3.2.7} and \eqref{eq:3.1.18} give the $S$-matrix as
\[
 S_{\beta\alpha}=\delta(\beta-\alpha)-2\pi i\delta(E_\beta-E_\alpha)T^+_{\beta\alpha},
 \qquad T^+_{\beta\alpha}=\langle\beta|H_{\mathrm{int}}|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{in}},
\]
where $\ket{\alpha}_{\mathrm{in}}$ satisfies the Lippmann--Schwinger equation \eqref{eq:3.1.17}:
\[
 \ket{\alpha}_{\mathrm{in}}=\ket{\alpha}
 +\int d\gamma\,\frac{T^+_{\gamma\alpha}\ket{\gamma}}{E_\alpha-E_\gamma+i\epsilon}.
\]
Operating on this equation with $H_{\mathrm{int}}$ and taking the scalar product with
$\ket{\beta}$ yields an integral equation for $T^+$
\begin{equation}
 T^+_{\beta\alpha}=(H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\beta\alpha}
 +\int d\gamma\,\frac{(H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\beta\gamma}T^+_{\gamma\alpha}}
 {E_\alpha-E_\gamma+i\epsilon},
 \tag{3.5.1}\label{eq:3.5.1}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 (H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\beta\alpha}\equiv
 \langle\beta|H_{\mathrm{int}}|\alpha\rangle.
 \tag{3.5.2}\label{eq:3.5.2}
\end{equation}
The perturbation series for $T^+_{\beta\alpha}$ is obtained by iteration from Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.1}
\begin{align}
 T^+_{\beta\alpha}={}&(H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\beta\alpha}
 +\int d\gamma\,\frac{(H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\beta\gamma}
 (H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\gamma\alpha}}{E_\alpha-E_\gamma+i\epsilon}\notag\\
 &+\int d\gamma\,d\gamma'\,
 \frac{(H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\beta\gamma}(H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\gamma\gamma'}
 (H_{\mathrm{int}})_{\gamma'\alpha}}
 {(E_\alpha-E_\gamma+i\epsilon)(E_\alpha-E_{\gamma'}+i\epsilon)}+\cdots.
 \tag{3.5.3}\label{eq:3.5.3}
\end{align}
The method of calculation based on Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.3}, which dominated calculations of the $S$-matrix
in the 1930s, is today known as \emph{old-fashioned perturbation theory}. Its obvious drawback
is that the energy denominators obscure the underlying Lorentz invariance of the $S$-matrix.
It still has some uses, however, in clarifying the way that singularities of the $S$-matrix arise
from various intermediate states. For the most part in this book, we will rely on a rewritten
version of Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.3}, known as \emph{time-dependent perturbation theory}, which has the virtue
of making Lorentz invariance much more transparent, while somewhat obscuring the contribution
of individual intermediate states.

The easiest way to derive the time-ordered perturbation expansion is to use Eq. \eqref{eq:3.2.5}, which
gives the $S$-operator as
\[
 S=U(\infty,-\infty),
\]
where
\[
 U(\tau,\tau_0)\equiv
 \exp(iH_0\tau)\exp\bigl(-iH(\tau-\tau_0)\bigr)\exp(-iH_0\tau_0).
\]
Differentiating this formula for $U(\tau,\tau_0)$ with respect to $\tau$ gives the differential
equation
\begin{equation}
 i\frac{d}{d\tau}U(\tau,\tau_0)=H_I(\tau)U(\tau,\tau_0),
 \tag{3.5.4}\label{eq:3.5.4}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 H_I(t)\equiv\exp(iH_0t)H_{\mathrm{int}}\exp(-iH_0t).
 \tag{3.5.5}\label{eq:3.5.5}
\end{equation}
(Operators with this sort of time-dependence are said to be defined in the \emph{interaction
picture}, to distinguish their time-dependence from the time-dependence
\[
 O_H(t)=\exp(iHt)O_H\exp(-iHt)
\]
required in the Heisenberg picture of quantum mechanics.)
Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.4} as well as the initial condition $U(\tau_0,\tau_0)=\mathbf{1}$ is obviously satisfied
by the solution of the integral equation
\begin{equation}
 U(\tau,\tau_0)=\mathbf{1}-i\int_{\tau_0}^{\tau}dt\,H_I(t)U(t,\tau_0).
 \tag{3.5.6}\label{eq:3.5.6}
\end{equation}
By iteration of this integral equation, we obtain an expansion for $U(\tau,\tau_0)$ in powers
of $H_{\mathrm{int}}$
\begin{align}
 U(\tau,\tau_0)={}&\mathbf{1}-i\int_{\tau_0}^{\tau}dt_1\,H_I(t_1)
 +(-i)^2\int_{\tau_0}^{\tau}dt_1\int_{\tau_0}^{t_1}dt_2\,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)\notag\\
 &+(-i)^3\int_{\tau_0}^{\tau}dt_1\int_{\tau_0}^{t_1}dt_2
 \int_{\tau_0}^{t_2}dt_3\,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)H_I(t_3)+\cdots.
 \tag{3.5.7}\label{eq:3.5.7}
\end{align}
Setting $\tau=\infty$ and $\tau_0=-\infty$ then gives the perturbation expansion for the
$S$-operator:
\begin{align}
 S={}&\mathbf{1}-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt_1\,H_I(t_1)
 +(-i)^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt_1\int_{-\infty}^{t_1}dt_2\,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)\notag\\
 &+(-i)^3\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt_1\int_{-\infty}^{t_1}dt_2
 \int_{-\infty}^{t_2}dt_3\,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)H_I(t_3)+\cdots.
 \tag{3.5.8}\label{eq:3.5.8}
\end{align}
This can also be derived directly from the old-fashioned perturbation expansion \eqref{eq:3.5.3}, by
using the Fourier representation of the energy factors in Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.3}:
\begin{equation}
 (E_\alpha-E_\gamma+i\epsilon)^{-1}
 =-i\int_0^\infty d\tau\,\exp\bigl(i(E_\alpha-E_\gamma)\tau\bigr)
 \tag{3.5.9}\label{eq:3.5.9}
\end{equation}
with the understanding that such integrals are to be evaluated by inserting a convergence
factor $e^{-\epsilon\tau}$ in the integrand, with $\epsilon\to0+$.

There is a way of rewriting Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.8} that proves very useful in carrying out manifestly
Lorentz-invariant calculations. Define the \emph{time-ordered product} of any time-dependent
operators as the product with factors arranged so that the one with the latest time-argument
is placed leftmost, the next-latest next to the leftmost, and so on. For instance,
\[
 T\{H_I(t)\}=H_I(t),
\]
\[
 T\{H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)\}=\theta(t_1-t_2)H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)
 +\theta(t_2-t_1)H_I(t_2)H_I(t_1),
\]
and so on, where $\theta(\tau)$ is the step function, equal to $+1$ for $\tau>0$ and to zero
for $\tau<0$. The time-ordered product of $n$ $H_I$s is a sum over all $n!$ permutations of
the $H_I$s, each of which gives the same integral over all $t_1\cdots t_n$, so Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.8}
may be written
\begin{equation}
 S=\mathbf{1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^n}{n!}
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt_1dt_2\cdots dt_n\,
 T\{H_I(t_1)\cdots H_I(t_n)\}.
 \tag{3.5.10}\label{eq:3.5.10}
\end{equation}
This is sometimes known as the Dyson series~\hyperref[ref:3.20]{[20]}. This series can be summed if the $H_I(t)$
at different times all commute; the sum is then
\[
 S=\exp\left(-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,H_I(t)\right).
\]
Of course, this is not usually the case; in general \eqref{eq:3.5.10} does not even converge, and is at
best an asymptotic expansion in whatever coupling constant factors appear in $H_{\mathrm{int}}$.
However Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.10} is sometimes written in the general case as
\[
 S=T\exp\left(-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,H_I(t)\right)
\]
with $T$ indicating here that the expression is to be evaluated by time-ordering each term in
the series expansion for the exponential.

We can now readily find one large class of theories for which the $S$-matrix is manifestly
Lorentz-invariant. Since the elements of the $S$-matrix are the matrix elements of the
$S$-operator between free-particle states $\ket{\alpha}$, $\ket{\beta}$, etc., what we want is
that the $S$-operator should commute with the operator $U_0(\Lambda,a)$ that produces Lorentz
transformations on these free-particle states. Equivalently, the $S$-operator must commute with
the generators of $U_0(\Lambda,a)$: $H_0$, $\mathbf{P}_0$, $\mathbf{J}_0$, and $\mathbf{K}_0$.
To satisfy this requirement, let's try the hypothesis that $H_I(t)$ is an integral over
three-space
\begin{equation}
 H_I(t)=\int d^3x\,\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},t)
 \tag{3.5.11}\label{eq:3.5.11}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ a scalar in the sense that
\begin{equation}
 U_0(\Lambda,a)\mathcal{H}_I(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,a)
 =\mathcal{H}_I(\Lambda x+a).
 \tag{3.5.12}\label{eq:3.5.12}
\end{equation}
(By equating the coefficients of $a^0$ for infinitesimal transformations it can be checked that
$\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ has a time-dependence consistent with Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.5}.) Then $S$ may be written
as a sum of four-dimensional integrals
\begin{equation}
 S=\mathbf{1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^n}{n!}
 \int d^4x_1\cdots d^4x_n\,
 T\{\mathcal{H}_I(x_1)\cdots\mathcal{H}_I(x_n)\}.
 \tag{3.5.13}\label{eq:3.5.13}
\end{equation}
Everything is now manifestly Lorentz invariant, except for the time-ordering of the operator
product.

Now, the time-ordering of two spacetime points $x_1,x_2$ is Lorentz-invariant unless $x_1-x_2$
is space-like, i.e., unless $(x_1-x_2)^2>0$, so the time-ordering in Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.13} introduces
no special Lorentz frame if (though not only if) the $\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ all commute at space-like
or light-like separations:
\begin{equation}
 [\mathcal{H}_I(x),\mathcal{H}_I(x')]=0\qquad\text{for}\qquad(x-x')^2\geq0.
 \tag{3.5.14}\label{eq:3.5.14}
\end{equation}
We can use the results of Section 3.3 to give a formal non-perturbative proof that an interaction
\eqref{eq:3.5.11} satisfying Eqs. \eqref{eq:3.5.12} and \eqref{eq:3.5.14} does lead to an $S$-matrix with the correct
Lorentz transformation properties. For an infinitesimal boost, Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.12} gives
\begin{equation}
 i[\mathbf{K}_0,\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},t)]
 =t\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},t)
 +\mathbf{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},t),
 \tag{3.5.15}\label{eq:3.5.15}
\end{equation}
so integrating over $\mathbf{x}$ and setting $t=0$,
\begin{equation}
 [\mathbf{K}_0,H_{\mathrm{int}}]
 =\left[\mathbf{K}_0,\int d^3x\,\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},0)\right]
 =[H_0,\mathbf{W}],
 \tag{3.5.16}\label{eq:3.5.16}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{W}\equiv\int d^3x\,\mathbf{x}\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},0).
 \tag{3.5.17}\label{eq:3.5.17}
\end{equation}
If (as is usually the case) the matrix elements of $\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},0)$ between
eigenstates of $H_0$ are smooth functions of the energy eigenvalues, then the same is true of
$H_{\mathrm{int}}$, as is necessary for the validity of scattering theory, and also true of
$\mathbf{W}$, which is necessary in the proof of Lorentz invariance. The other condition for
Lorentz invariance, the commutation relation \eqref{eq:3.3.21}, is also valid if and only if
\begin{equation}
 0=[\mathbf{W},H_{\mathrm{int}}]
 =\int d^3x\int d^3y\,\mathbf{x}
 [\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},0),\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{y},0)].
 \tag{3.5.18}\label{eq:3.5.18}
\end{equation}
This condition would follow from the `causality' condition \eqref{eq:3.5.14}, but provides a somewhat
less restrictive sufficient condition for Lorentz invariance of the $S$-matrix.

Theories of this class are not the only ones that are Lorentz invariant, but the most general
Lorentz invariant theories are not very different. In particular, there is always a commutation
condition something like \eqref{eq:3.5.14} that needs to be satisfied. This condition has no counterpart
for non-relativistic systems, for which time-ordering is always Galilean-invariant.
\emph{It is this condition that makes the combination of Lorentz invariance and quantum mechanics
so restrictive.}
\footnote{We write the condition on $x$ and $x'$ here as $(x-x')^2\geq0$ instead of
$(x-x')^2>0$, because as we shall see in Chapter 6, Lorentz invariance can be disturbed by
troublesome singularities at $x=x'$.}

The methods described so far in this section are useful when the interaction operator
$H_{\mathrm{int}}$ is sufficiently small. There is also a modified version of this approximation,
known as the \emph{distorted-wave Born approximation}, that is useful when the interaction
contains two terms
\begin{equation}
 H_{\mathrm{int}}=H_s+H_w
 \tag{3.5.19}\label{eq:3.5.19}
\end{equation}
with $H_w$ weak but $H_s$ strong. We can define $\ket{\alpha;s}_{\mathrm{in/out}}$ as what the
`in' and `out' states would be if $H_s$ were the whole interaction
\begin{equation}
 \ket{\alpha;s}_{\mathrm{in/out}}=\ket{\alpha}
 +(E_\alpha-H_0\pm i\epsilon)^{-1}H_s\ket{\alpha;s}_{\mathrm{in/out}}.
 \tag{3.5.20}\label{eq:3.5.20}
\end{equation}
We can then write \eqref{eq:3.1.16} as
\begin{align*}
 T^+_{\beta\alpha}
 &=\langle\beta|H_{\mathrm{int}}|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{in}}\\
 &=\left({}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|-{}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_s(E_\beta-H_0+i\epsilon)^{-1}\right)
 (H_s+H_w)\ket{\alpha}_{\mathrm{in}}\\
 &={}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_w|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{in}}\\
 &\quad+{}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|
 [H_s-H_s(E_\beta-H_0+i\epsilon)^{-1}(H_s+H_w)]
 |\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{in}},
\end{align*}
and so
\begin{equation}
 T^+_{\beta\alpha}={}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_w|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{in}}
 +{}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_s|\alpha\rangle.
 \tag{3.5.21}\label{eq:3.5.21}
\end{equation}
The second term on the right-hand side is just what $T^+_{s,\beta\alpha}$ would be in the
presence of strong interactions alone
\begin{equation}
 T^+_{s,\beta\alpha}=\langle\beta|H_s|\alpha;s\rangle_{\mathrm{in}}
 ={}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_s|\alpha\rangle.
 \tag{3.5.22}\label{eq:3.5.22}
\end{equation}
(For a proof of Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.22}, just drop $H_w$ everywhere in the derivation of Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.21}.)
Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.21} is most useful when this second term vanishes: that is, when the process
$\alpha\to\beta$ cannot be produced by the strong interactions alone. (For example, in nuclear
beta decay we need a weak nuclear force to turn neutrons into protons, even though we cannot
ignore the presence of the strong nuclear force acting in the initial and final nuclear states.)
For such processes, the matrix element \eqref{eq:3.5.22} vanishes, so Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.21} reads
\begin{equation}
 T^+_{\beta\alpha}={}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_w|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{in}}.
 \tag{3.5.23}\label{eq:3.5.23}
\end{equation}
So far, this is all exact. However, this way of rewriting the $T$-matrix becomes worthwhile when
$H_w$ is so weak that we may neglect its effect on the state $\ket{\alpha}_{\mathrm{in}}$ in
Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.23}, and hence replace $\ket{\alpha}_{\mathrm{in}}$ with the state
$\ket{\alpha;s}_{\mathrm{in}}$, which takes account only of the strong interaction $H_s$. In this
approximation, Eq. \eqref{eq:3.5.23} becomes
\begin{equation}
 T^+_{\beta\alpha}\simeq
 {}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\langle\beta;s|H_w|\alpha;s\rangle_{\mathrm{in}}.
 \tag{3.5.24}\label{eq:3.5.24}
\end{equation}
This is valid to first order in $H_w$, but to all orders in $H_s$. This approximation is ubiquitous
in physics; for instance, the $S$-matrix element for nuclear
