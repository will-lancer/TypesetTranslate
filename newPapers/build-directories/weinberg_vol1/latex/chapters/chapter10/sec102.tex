\subsection{Polology}

One of the most important uses of the non-perturbative methods described
in this chapter is to clarify the pole structure of Feynman amplitudes as

\input{figures/chapter10/fig101.tex}

functions of the momenta carried by external lines. Often the $S$-matrix
for a physical process can be well approximated by the contribution of a
single pole. Also, an understanding of this pole structure will help us later
in dealing with radiative corrections to particle propagators.

Consider the momentum-space amplitude
\begin{align}
  &\int d^4x_1\cdots d^4x_n\,
  e^{-iq_1\cdot x_1}\cdots e^{-iq_n\cdot x_n}
  \left\langle T\{A_1(x_1)\cdots A_n(x_n)\}\right\rangle_0
  \notag\\
  &\hspace{5cm}\equiv G(q_1\cdots q_n).
  \tag{10.2.1}\label{eq:10.2.1}
\end{align}
The $A$s are Heisenberg-picture operators of arbitrary Lorentz type, and
$\langle\cdots\rangle_0$ denotes the expectation value in the true vacuum
$\InKet{\Omega}=\OutKet{\Omega}\equiv\ket{\Omega}$.
As discussed in Section 6.4, if $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ are ordinary fields appearing in
the Lagrangian, then \eqref{eq:10.2.1} is a sum of the terms calculated using the
ordinary Feynman rules, for all graphs with external lines corresponding
to the fields $A_1,\ldots,A_n$, carrying off-shell four-momenta $q_1\cdots q_n$ into the
graph. However, we will not be limited to this case; the $A_i$ may be
arbitrary local functions of fields and field derivatives.

We are interested in poles of $G$ at certain values of the invariant squares
of the total four-momenta carried by various subsets of the external lines.
To be definite, let's consider $G$ as a function of $q^2$, where
\begin{equation}
  q\equiv q_1+\cdots+q_r=-q_{r+1}-\cdots-q_n
  \tag{10.2.2}\label{eq:10.2.2}
\end{equation}
with $1\leq r\leq n-1$. We will show that $G$ has a pole at $q^2=-m^2$, where $m$ is
the mass of any one-particle state that has non-vanishing matrix elements
with the states $A_1^\dagger\cdots A_r^\dagger\ket{\Omega}$ and
$A_{r+1}\cdots A_n\ket{\Omega}$, and that the residue at this
pole is given by
\begin{align}
  G\longrightarrow{}&
  \frac{-2i\sqrt{\mathbf q^2+m^2}}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
  (2\pi)^4\delta^4(q_1+\cdots+q_n)
  \notag\\
  &\quad\cdot\sum_\sigma
  M_{0;\mathbf q,\sigma}(q_2\cdots q_r)
  M_{\mathbf q,\sigma;0}(q_{r+2}\cdots q_n),
  \tag{10.2.3}\label{eq:10.2.3}
\end{align}
where the $M$s are defined by\footnote{Recall that in the absence of time-varying
external fields, there is no distinction between `in' and `out' one-particle
states, so that $\InKet{\mathbf p,\sigma}
=\OutKet{\mathbf p,\sigma}
\equiv\ket{\mathbf p,\sigma}$.}
\begin{align}
  &\int d^4x_1\cdots d^4x_r\,
  e^{-iq_1\cdot x_1}\cdots e^{-iq_r\cdot x_r}
  \bra{\Omega}T\{A_1(x_1)\cdots A_r(x_r)\}\ket{\mathbf p,\sigma}
  \notag\\
  &\qquad=(2\pi)^4\delta^4(q_1+\cdots+q_r-p)
  M_{0;\mathbf p,\sigma}(q_2\cdots q_r),
  \tag{10.2.4}\label{eq:10.2.4}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  &\int d^4x_{r+1}\cdots d^4x_n\,
  e^{-iq_{r+1}\cdot x_{r+1}}\cdots e^{-iq_n\cdot x_n}
  \bra{\mathbf p,\sigma}T\{A_{r+1}(x_{r+1})\cdots A_n(x_n)\}\ket{\Omega}
  \notag\\
  &\qquad=(2\pi)^4\delta^4(q_{r+1}+\cdots+q_n+p)
  M_{\mathbf p,\sigma;0}(q_{r+2}\cdots q_n)
  \tag{10.2.5}\label{eq:10.2.5}
\end{align}
(with $p^0=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$), and the sum is over all spin (or other) states of the
particle of mass $m$.

Before proceeding to the proof, it will help to clarify the significance of
\eqref{eq:10.2.3} if we write it in the somewhat long-winded form
\begin{align}
  G(q_1\cdots q_n)\longrightarrow{}&\int d^4k
  \Bigl[(2\pi)^4\delta^4(q_1+\cdots+q_r-k)(2\pi)^{3/2}
  \bigl(2\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m^2}\bigr)^{1/2}
  M_{0;\mathbf k,\sigma}(q_2\cdots q_r)\Bigr]
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\left[\frac{-i}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{1}{k^2+m^2-i\epsilon}\right]
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\Bigl[(2\pi)^4\delta^4(k+q_{r+1}+\cdots+q_n)(2\pi)^{3/2}
  \bigl(2\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m^2}\bigr)^{1/2}
  M_{\mathbf k,\sigma;0}(q_{r+2}\cdots q_n)\Bigr].
  \tag{10.2.6}\label{eq:10.2.6}
\end{align}
This is just what we should expect from a Feynman diagram with a single
internal line for a particle of mass $m$ connecting the first $r$ and the last
$n-r$ external lines.\footnote{See Figure~\ref{fig:10.2}. The factors
$(2\pi)^{3/2}[2(\mathbf k^2+m^2)]^{1/2}$ just serve to remove kinematic factors
associated with the mass $m$ external line in $M_{0;\mathbf k,\sigma}$ and
$M_{\mathbf k,\sigma;0}$. Also, the sum over $\sigma$ of the product of
coefficient-function factors from these two matrix elements yields the numerator of
the propagator associated with the internal line in Figure~\ref{fig:10.2}.} However, it is
not necessary that the particle of mass $m$ correspond to a field that appears
in the Lagrangian of the theory. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.2.3} and \eqref{eq:10.2.6} apply even if this
particle is a bound state of the so-called elementary particles whose fields
do appear in the Lagrangian. In this case, the pole arises not from single
Feynman diagrams, like Figure~\ref{fig:10.2}, but rather from infinite sums of diagrams,
such as the one shown in Figure~\ref{fig:10.3}. This is the first place where the methods
of this chapter take us beyond results that could be derived as properties of
each order of perturbation theory.

Now to the proof. Among the $n!$ possible orderings of the times $x_1^0\cdots x_n^0$
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.1}, there are $n!/r!(n-r)!$ for which the first $r$ of the $x_i^0$ are
all larger than the last $n-r$. Isolating the contribution of this part of the
volume of integration in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.1}, we have
\begin{align}
  G(q_1\cdots q_n)={}&\int d^4x_1\cdots d^4x_n\,
  e^{-iq_1\cdot x_1}\cdots e^{-iq_n\cdot x_n}
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\theta\!\left(\min[x_1^0\cdots x_r^0]
  -\max[x_{r+1}^0\cdots x_n^0]\right)
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\bra{\Omega}T\{A_1(x_1)\cdots A_r(x_r)\}
  T\{A_{r+1}(x_{r+1})\cdots A_n(x_n)\}\ket{\Omega}
  \notag\\
  &+\mathrm{OT},
  \tag{10.2.7}\label{eq:10.2.7}
\end{align}
where `OT' denotes the other terms arising from different time-orderings.
We can evaluate the matrix element here by inserting a complete set of
intermediate states between time-ordered products. Among these may

This result has a classic application to the theory of nuclear forces. Let
$\Phi_a(x)$ be any real field or combination of fields (for instance, proportional
to a quark--antiquark bilinear $\bar q\gamma_5\tau_a q$) that has a non-vanishing matrix
element between a one-pion state of isospin $a$ and the vacuum, normalized
so that
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Omega}\Phi_a(0)\ket{\pi_b,\mathbf p}
  =(2\pi)^{-3/2}(2p^0)^{-1/2}\delta_{ab}.
  \tag{10.2.15}\label{eq:10.2.15}
\end{equation}
The matrix element of $\Phi_a$ between one-nucleon states with four-momenta
$p,p'$ then has a pole at $(p-p')^2\to-m_\pi^2$, which isospin and Lorentz
invariance (including space inversion invariance) dictate must take the
form\footnote{Lorentz and isospin invariance requires this matrix to take the form
$(\bar u'\Gamma\tau_a u)$, where $\Gamma$ is a $4\times4$ matrix for which the bilinear
$(\bar\psi'\Gamma\psi)$ transforms as a pseudoscalar. Like any $4\times4$ matrix, $\Gamma$ can
be expanded as a sum of terms proportional to the Dirac matrices $\mathbf{1}$, $\gamma_\mu$,
$[\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]$, $\gamma_5\gamma_\mu$, and $\gamma_5$. The coefficients must be respectively
pseudoscalar, pseudovector, pseudotensor, vector, and scalar. Out of the two momenta
$p$ and $p'$ it is possible to construct no pseudoscalars or pseudovectors; just one
pseudotensor, proportional to $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_\rho p'_\sigma$; two independent vectors,
proportional to $p_\mu$ or $p'_\mu$; and a scalar proportional to unity, in each case with a
proportionality factor depending on the only independent scalar variable, $(p-p')^2$.
By using the momentum-space Dirac equations for $u$ and $u'$, it is easy to see that
the tensor and pseudovector matrices in $\Gamma$ give contributions proportional to $\gamma_5$.}
\begin{equation}
  \bra{N',\sigma',\mathbf p'}\Phi_a(0)\ket{N,\sigma,\mathbf p}
  \longrightarrow i(2\pi)^{-3}G_\pi\,
  \frac{\bar u'\gamma_5\tau_a u}{(p-p')^2+m_\pi^2},
  \tag{10.2.16}\label{eq:10.2.16}
\end{equation}
where $u$ and $u'$ are the initial and final nucleon spinor coefficient functions,
including the nucleon wave functions in isospin space, and $\tau_a$ with $a=
1,2,3$ are the $2\times2$ Pauli isospin matrices. The constant $G_\pi$ is known as the
\textit{pion--nucleon coupling constant}. This pole is not actually in the physical
region for the matrix element \eqref{eq:10.2.16}, for which $(p-p')^2\geq0$, but it can
be reached by analytic extension of this matrix element, for instance by
considering the off-shell matrix element
\[
  \int d^4x\,d^4x'\,e^{-ip\cdot x}e^{ip'\cdot x'}
  \bra{\Omega}T\{\Phi_a(0)\bar N(x)N'(x')\}\ket{\Omega},
\]
where $N$ and $N'$ are appropriate components of a field operator or
product of field operators with non-vanishing matrix elements between
one-nucleon states and the vacuum. The theorem proved above in this
section shows then that exchange of a pion in the scattering of two
nucleons with initial four-momenta $p_1,p_2$, and final four-momenta $p'_1,p'_2$
yields a pole at $(p_1-p'_1)^2=(p_2-p'_2)^2\to-m_\pi^2$.
\begin{align}
  S_{N'_1N'_2,N_1N_2}\longrightarrow{}&
  -i(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p'_1+p'_2-p_1-p_2)
  \frac{G_\pi^2}{(p_1-p'_1)^2+m_\pi^2}
  \notag\\
  &\cdot(2\pi)^{-3}[\bar u_{1'}\gamma_5\tau_a u_1]
  \cdot(2\pi)^{-3}[\bar u_{2'}\gamma_5\tau_a u_2].
  \tag{10.2.17}\label{eq:10.2.17}
\end{align}
(The easiest way to get the phases and numerical factors right in such
formulas is to use Feynman diagrams; our theorem just says that the pole
structure is the same as would be found in a field theory in which the
Lagrangian involved an elementary pion field.) Again, this pion pole is
not actually in the physical region for scattering of nucleons on the mass
shell, for which $(p_1-p'_1)^2\geq0$, but it can be reached by analytic extension
of the $S$-matrix element, for instance by considering the off-shell matrix
