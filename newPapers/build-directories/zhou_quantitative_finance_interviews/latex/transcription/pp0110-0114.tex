Actually after the 2-noodle case, you probably have found the key to this question. If

\]

probability to yield 1 circle and $n-1$ noodles and $\dfrac{2n-2}{2n-1}$ probability

\begin{problem}{Optimal hedge ratio}
You just bought one share of stock $A$ and want to hedge it by shorting stock $B$. How
many shares of $B$ should you short to minimize the variance of the hedged position?
Assume that the variance of stock $A$'s return is $\sigma_A^2$; the variance of $B$'s
return is $\sigma_B^2$; their correlation coefficient is $\rho$.

\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Suppose that we short $h$ shares of $B$, the variance of the portfolio return is

\[
\operatorname{var}(r_A-hr_B)=\sigma_A^2-2\rho h\sigma_A\sigma_B+h^2\sigma_B^2.
\]

The best hedge ratio should minimize $\operatorname{var}(r_A-hr_B)$. Take the first
order partial derivative with respect to $h$ and set it to zero:

\[
\frac{\partial\operatorname{var}}{\partial h}=-2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B+2h\sigma_B^2=0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad h=\rho\frac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}.
\]

To confirm it's the minimum, we can also check the second-order partial derivative:

\[
\frac{\partial^2\operatorname{var}}{\partial h^2}=2\sigma_B^2>0.
\]

So indeed when $h=\rho\dfrac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}$, the hedge portfolio has the
minimum variance.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{Dice game}
Suppose that you roll a dice. For each roll, you are paid the face value. If a roll gives
4, 5 or 6, you can roll the dice again. Once you get 1, 2 or 3, the game stops. What is
the expected payoff of this game?

\end{problem}
\Needspace{4\baselineskip}
\begin{solution}
This is an example of the law of total expectation. Clearly your payoff will be different
depending on the outcome of first roll. Let $\E[X]$ be your expected payoff and $Y$ be the
outcome of your first throw. You have $1/2$ chance to get $Y\in\{1,2,3\}$, in which case
the expected value is the expected face value 2, so $\E[X\vert Y\in\{1,2,3\}]=2$; you have

% ZHOU-SOURCE-PAGE: 111 PRINTED: 95

$1/2$ chance to get $Y\in\{4,5,6\}$, in which case you get expected face value 5 and
extra throw(s). The extra throw(s) essentially means you start the game again and have an
extra expected value $\E[X]$. So we have $\E[X\vert Y\in\{4,5,6\}]=5+\E[X]$. Apply the
law of total expectation, we have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\E[X]&=\E[\E[X\vert Y]]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot2+\frac{1}{2}\cdot(5+\E[X])\\
&\quad\Rightarrow\quad \E[X]=7.\footnotemark[27]
\end{aligned}
\]
\footnotetext[27]{You will also see that the problem can be solved using Wald's equality in Chapter 5.}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{Card game}
What is the expected number of cards that need to be turned over in a regular 52-card deck
in order to see the first ace?

\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
There are 4 aces and 48 other cards. Let's label them as card 1, 2, $\ldots$, 48. Let

\[
X_i=\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if card $i$ is turned over before 4 aces},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

The total number of cards that need to be turned over in order to see the first ace is
$X=1+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{48}X_i$, so we have
$\E[X]=1+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{48}\E[X_i]$. As shown in the following sequence,
each card $i$ is equally likely to be in one of the five regions separated by 4 aces:

\[
1\qquad A\qquad 2\qquad A\qquad 3\qquad A\qquad 4\qquad A\qquad 5
\]

So the probability that card $i$ appears before all 4 aces is $1/5$, and we have
$\E[X_i]=1/5$.

Therefore,
\[
\E[X]=1+\sum_{i=1}^{48}\E[X_i]=1+48/5=10.6.
\]

This is just a special case for random ordering of $m$ ordinary cards and $n$ special
cards. The expected position of the first special card is

\[
1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\E[X_i]=1+\frac{m}{n+1}.
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{Sum of random variables}
Assume that $X_1$, $X_2$, $\ldots$, and $X_n$ are independent and
identically-distributed (IID) random variables with uniform distribution between 0 and 1.
What is the probability that $S_n=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n\leq1$?\footnote[28]{Hint: start with
the simplest case where $n=1$, 2, and 3. Try to find a general formula and prove it using
induction.}

% ZHOU-SOURCE-PAGE: 112 PRINTED: 96

\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
This problem is a rather difficult one. The general principle to start with the simplest
cases and try to find a pattern will again help you approach the problem; even though it
may not give you the final answer. When $n=1$, $P(S_1\leq1)$ is 1. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:zhou-figure-4-6}, when $n=2$, the probability that $X_1+X_2\leq1$ is
just the area under $X_1+X_2\leq1$ within the square with side length 1 (a triangle). So
$P(S_2\leq1)=1/2$. When $n=3$, the probability becomes the tetrahedron ABCD under the
plane $X_1+X_2+X_3\leq1$ within the cube with side length 1. The volume of tetrahedron
ABCD is $1/6$.\footnote[29]{You can derive it by integration:
$\int_0^1 A(z)\,\dd z=\int_0^1\frac{1}{2}z^2\,\dd z=1/6$, where $A(z)$ is the cross-sectional
area.} So $P(S_3\leq1)=1/6$. Now we can guess that the solution is $1/n!$. To prove it,
let's again resort to induction. Assume $P(S_n\leq1)=1/n!$. We need to prove that
$P(S_{n+1}\leq1)=1/(n+1)!$.

Here we can use probability by conditioning. Condition on the value of $X_{n+1}$, we
have

\[
P(S_{n+1}\leq1)=\int_0^1 f(X_{n+1})P(S_n\leq1-X_{n+1})\,\dd X_{n+1},
\]

where $f(X_{n+1})$ is the probability density function of $X_{n+1}$, so
$f(X_{n+1})=1$. But how do we calculate $P(S_n\leq1-X_{n+1})$? The cases of $n=2$
and $n=3$ have provided us with some clue. For $S_n\leq1-X_{n+1}$ instead of
$S_n\leq1$, we essentially need to shrink every dimension of the $n$-dimensional
simplex\footnote[30]{An $n$-Simplex is the $n$-dimensional analog of a triangle.} from 1 to

% ZHOU-SOURCE-PAGE: 113 PRINTED: 97

$1-X_{n+1}$. So its volume should be
$\dfrac{(1-X_{n+1})^n}{n!}$ instead of $\dfrac{1}{n!}$. Plugging in these results,
we have

\[
\begin{aligned}
P(S_{n+1}\leq1)&=\int_0^1\frac{(1-X_{n+1})^n}{n!}\,\dd X_{n+1}\\
&=\frac{1}{n!}\left[-\frac{(1-X_{n+1})^{n+1}}{n+1}\right]_0^1\\
&=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{1}{n+1}=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}.
\end{aligned}
\]

So the general result is true for $n+1$ as well and we have $P(S_n\leq1)=1/n!$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{Coupon collection}
There are $N$ distinct types of coupons in cereal boxes and each type, independent of
prior selections, is equally likely to be in a box.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[A.] If a child wants to collect a complete set of coupons with at least one of each
type, how many coupons (boxes) on average are needed to make such a complete set?
\item[B.] If the child has collected $n$ coupons, what is the expected number of distinct
coupon types?\footnote[31]{Hint: For part A, let $X_i$ be the number of extra coupons collected
to get the $i$-th distinct coupon after $i-1$ types of distinct coupons have been collected.
Then the total expected number of coupons to collect all distinct types is
$\E[X]=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\E[X_i]$. For part B, which is the expected probability
(P) that the $i$-th coupon type is not in the $n$ coupons?}
\end{enumerate}

\end{problem}
\Needspace{4\baselineskip}
\begin{solution}
For part A, let $X_i$, $i=1,2,\ldots,N$, be the number of additional coupons needed
to obtain the $i$-th type after $(i-1)$ distinct types have been collected. So the total
number of coupons needed is

\[
X=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_N=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i.
\]

For any $i$, $i-1$ distinct types of coupons have already been collected. It follows that a
new coupon will be of a different type with probability $1-(i-1)/N=(N-i+1)/N$.
Essentially we are waiting for the $i$-th distinct type, the random variable $X_i$ follows a
geometric distribution with $p=(N-i+1)/N$ and $\E[X_i]=N/(N-i+1)$. For example, if
$i=1$, we simply have $X_1=\E[X_1]=1$.

\[
\begin{aligned}
\therefore\quad \E[X]&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\E[X_i]\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{N}{N-i+1}\\
&=N\left(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{N-1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{1}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

% ZHOU-SOURCE-PAGE: 114 PRINTED: 98

For part $B$, let $Y$ be the number of distinct types of coupons in the set of $n$ coupons.
We introduce indicator random variables $I_i$, $i=1,2,\ldots,N$, where

\[
\begin{cases}
I_i=1, & \text{if at least one coupon of the $i$-th type is in the set of $n$ coupons},\\
I_i=0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

So we have $Y=I_1+I_2+\cdots+I_N=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_i$.

For each collected coupon, the probability that it is not the $i$-th coupon type is
$\dfrac{N-1}{N}$. Since all $n$ coupons are independent, the probability that none of the
$n$ coupons is the $i$-th coupon type is
$P(I_i=0)=\left(\dfrac{N-1}{N}\right)^n$ and we have
$\E[I_i]=P(I_i=1)=1-\left(\dfrac{N-1}{N}\right)^n$.

\[
\begin{aligned}
\therefore\quad \E[Y]&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\E[I_i]\\
&=N-N\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^n.\footnotemark[32]
\end{aligned}
\]
\footnotetext[32]{A similar question: if you randomly put 18 balls into 10 boxes, what is the expected number of empty boxes?}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{Joint default probability}
If there is a 50\% probability that bond $A$ will default next year and a 30\% probability
that bond $B$ will default. What is the range of probability that at least one bond defaults
and what is the range of their correlation?

\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The range of probability that at least one bond defaults is easy to find. To have the largest
probability, we can assume whenever $A$ defaults, $B$ does not default; whenever $B$
defaults, $A$ does not default. So the maximum probability that at least one bond defaults
is $50\%+30\%=80\%$. (The result only applies if $P(A)+P(B)\leq1$.) For the minimum,
we can assume whenever $A$ defaults, $B$ also defaults. So the minimum probability that at
least one bond defaults is $50\%$.

To calculate the corresponding correlation, let $I_A$ and $I_B$ be the indicator for the event
that bond A/B defaults next year and $\rho_{AB}$ be their correlation. Then we have
$\E[I_A]=0.5$, $\E[I_B]=0.3$, $\operatorname{var}(I_A)=p_A\cdot(1-p_A)=0.25$,
$\operatorname{var}(I_B)=0.21$.
