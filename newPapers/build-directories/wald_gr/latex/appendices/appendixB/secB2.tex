\subsection{Integration}\label{sec:B.2}

Let \(M\) be an \(n\)-dimensional manifold. At each point \(x\in M\), the vector
space of \(n\)-forms will be one-dimensional. If it is possible to find a continuous,
nowhere vanishing \(n\)-form field
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon=\epsilon_{[a_1\ldots a_n]}\) on \(M\), then \(M\) is said to be
\emph{orientable} and \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is said to provide an
orientation.\footnote{For the case where \(M\) is an \(n\)-dimensional surface in the
Euclidean space \(\mathbb R^{n+1}\), this definition of orientability is equivalent to
the more intuitive notion that there exists a consistent (i.e., continuous) choice of
normal vector \(u^a\) to \(M\): If a continuous nonvanishing \(u^a\) exists, then
\(\epsilon_{a_2\ldots a_{n+1}}=
\widetilde\epsilon_{a_1a_2\ldots a_{n+1}}u^{a_1}\) provides an orientation of \(M\),
where \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) is an orientation of \(\mathbb R^{n+1}\).
Conversely, if \(M\) is orientable, then
\(\widetilde\epsilon^{a_1}{}_{a_2\ldots a_{n+1}}
\epsilon_{a_2\ldots a_{n+1}}\) provides a continuous normal vector.} Two
orientations \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) and \(\boldsymbol\epsilon'\) are considered
equivalent if \(\boldsymbol\epsilon=f\boldsymbol\epsilon'\), where \(f\) is a
(strictly) positive function, so any orientable manifold possesses two inequivalent
orientations, usually referred to as \lq\lq right handed\rq\rq\ and
\lq\lq left handed.\rq\rq\ It is easy to check that the manifolds \(\mathbb R^n\) and
\(S^m\) are orientable. Indeed, it is not difficult to show that every simply
connected manifold is orientable. (As discussed further in Chapter~13, a topological
space is said to be \emph{simply connected} if every closed curve can be continuously
deformed to a point. \(\mathbb R^n\) and \(S^m\) for \(m\geq2\) are simply
connected.) Furthermore, the product of any two orientable manifolds is orientable.
Thus, we obtain a wide class of examples of orientable manifolds. On the other hand,
the M\"obius strip [defined as \(\mathbb R^2\) with the identification
\((x,y)=(x+1,-y)\)] provides a simple example of a nonorientable manifold.

We will define the integral of a continuous (or, more generally, a
measurable\footnote{\(\boldsymbol\alpha\) is said to be measurable if for all charts
its coordinate basis components are Lebesgue measurable functions in \(\mathbb R^n\).})
\(n\)-form field \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) over an \(n\)-dimensional orientable manifold
(with respect to the orientation \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\)) as follows. We begin by
considering an open region \(U\subseteq M\) covered by a single coordinate system
\(\psi\). If we expand \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) in the coordinate basis of \(\psi\), we
will obtain
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\epsilon=h\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n
  \tag{B.2.1}\label{eq:B.2.1}
\end{equation}
(i.e.,
\(\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_n}=n!h(dx^1)_{[a_1}\cdots(dx^n)_{a_n]}\)), where the
function \(h\) is nonvanishing. If \(h>0\), the coordinate system, \(\psi\), is said
to be \emph{right handed} with respect to \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\); if \(h<0\),
\(\psi\) is called \emph{left handed}. We may also expand
\(\boldsymbol\alpha\) in the coordinate basis, thereby obtaining
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\alpha
  =a(x^1,\ldots,x^n)\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
  \tag{B.2.2}\label{eq:B.2.2}
\end{equation}
If \(\psi\) is right handed, we define the integral of \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) over the
region \(U\) by
\begin{equation}
  \int_U\boldsymbol\alpha
  =\int_{\psi(U)}a\,dx^1\cdots dx^n,
  \tag{B.2.3}\label{eq:B.2.3}
\end{equation}
where the right-hand side is the standard Riemann (or Lebesgue) integral in
\(\mathbb R^n\). If \(\psi\) is left-handed, we define
\(\int_U\boldsymbol\alpha\) to be minus the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.3}.

First, we note that \(\int_U\boldsymbol\alpha\) is independent of the choice of
coordinate system, \(\psi\), covering \(U\); namely, if we had used a different
coordinate system \(\psi'\) to cover \(U\), then the expansion of
\(\boldsymbol\alpha\) in the new coordinate basis would be
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\alpha
  =a'\,dx'^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx'^n.
  \tag{B.2.4}\label{eq:B.2.4}
\end{equation}
But it follows from the tensor transformation law, Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.3.8}, that
\begin{equation}
  a'=a\det\left(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\right).
  \tag{B.2.5}\label{eq:B.2.5}
\end{equation}
The standard law for transformation of integrals in \(\mathbb R^n\) then shows that
our definition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.3}, is coordinate independent.

To define the integral of \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) over all of \(M\), we use the
paracompactness property of \(M\). As discussed at the end of Appendix~A, a
paracompact manifold can be covered by a countable collection \(\{O_i\}\) of locally
finite coordinate neighborhoods such that each \(\overline{O_i}\) is compact.
Furthermore, a partition of unity \(\{f_i\}\) subordinate to this covering will exist.
If
\[
  \sum_i\int_{\psi_i(O_i)}f_i\lvert a_i\rvert\,dx^1\cdots dx^n<\infty,
\]
we say \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) is integrable and we define
\begin{equation}
  \int_M\boldsymbol\alpha=\sum_i\int_{O_i}f_i\boldsymbol\alpha.
  \tag{B.2.6}\label{eq:B.2.6}
\end{equation}
It can be shown that this definition is independent of the choice of covering
\(\{O_i\}\) and partition of unity \(\{f_i\}\) and thus properly defines
\(\int_M\boldsymbol\alpha\).

We can use the above definition of integration on manifolds to define the integral of
\(p\)-forms on \(M\) over well behaved, orientable \(p\)-dimensional surfaces in
\(M\). First, we must define more precisely the notion of a \lq\lq well behaved
surface.\rq\rq\ Let \(S\) be a manifold of dimension \(p<n\). If
\(\phi:S\to M\) is \(C^\infty\), is locally one-to-one---i.e., each \(q\in S\) has an
open neighborhood \(O\) such that \(\phi\) restricted to \(O\) is one-to-one---and
\(\phi^{-1}:\phi(O)\to S\) is \(C^\infty\), then \(\phi(S)\) is said to be an
\emph{immersed submanifold} of \(M\). If, in addition, \(\phi\) is globally
one-to-one (i.e., \(\phi(S)\) does not \lq\lq intersect itself\rq\rq), then
\(\phi(S)\) is said to be an \emph{embedded submanifold} of \(M\). (In some
references the additional condition is imposed on embedded submanifolds that
\(\phi:S\to\phi(S)\) is a homeomorphism with the topology on \(\phi(S)\) induced
from \(M\). Roughly speaking, this additional condition ensures that \(\phi(S)\) does
not come arbitrarily close to intersecting itself.) We shall use the notion of an
embedded submanifold as our precise notion of a \lq\lq well behaved surface\rq\rq\
in \(M\). An embedded submanifold of dimension \((n-1)\) is called a
\emph{hypersurface}.

For an embedded submanifold, there is a natural manifold structure on \(\phi(S)\)
obtained via \(\phi\) from the manifold structure on \(S\). Thus, at each
\(q\in\phi(S)\), the tangent space \(T_q\phi(S)\) is defined. This tangent space is
naturally identified with a \(p\)-dimensional subspace of \(T_qM\), the tangent space
of \(q\) in \(M\). Thus, a \(p\)-form \(\boldsymbol\beta\) in \(M\) at \(q\) naturally
gives rise to a \(p\)-form \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\beta}\) on \(\phi(S)\) by
restriction of the action of \(\boldsymbol\beta\) to vectors lying in \(T_q\phi(S)\).
The integral of \(\boldsymbol\beta\) over the surface \(\phi(S)\) may then be defined
as simply the integral of the \(p\)-form \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\beta}\) over the
\(p\)-dimensional manifold \(\phi(S)\).

An important special case of an embedded submanifold arises when \(\phi(S)\) is the
\((n-1)\)-dimensional boundary, \(\partial N\), of a closed region
\(N\subseteq M\) such that \(N\) is a \lq\lq manifold with boundary.\rq\rq\ Here,
the notion of an \(n\)-dimensional \emph{manifold with boundary}, \(N\), can be
defined in the abstract in the same way as a manifold (see Chapter~2) except that
\(\mathbb R^n\) is replaced by \lq\lq half of \(\mathbb R^n\),\rq\rq\ i.e., by the
portion of \(\mathbb R^n\) with \(x^1\leq0\). The boundary, \(\partial N\), of \(N\)
is composed of the set of points of \(N\) which are mapped into \(x^1=0\) by the
chart maps. Note that these chart maps of \(N\) with \(x^1\) set to zero give
\(\partial N\) the structure of an \((n-1)\)-dimensional manifold without boundary.
Note also that \(\operatorname{int}(N)=N-\partial N\) is an \(n\)-dimensional
manifold without boundary.

If \(N\) is an orientable manifold with boundary, then an orientation on \(N\)
induces a natural orientation on the boundary as follows: We consider the coordinate
systems on \(\partial N\) which arise from deleting the first coordinate, \(x^1\), of
a right-handed coordinate system on \(N\) in the family of charts that makes \(N\)
into a manifold with boundary. We wish to define an orientation on \(\partial N\)
which makes these coordinate systems be \lq\lq right handed.\rq\rq\ In order to do
so, we verify first that the Jacobian,
\(\det(\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\nu)\), is positive in the overlap region of any
two such coordinate systems. Then, we choose a partition of unity
\((F_i,U_i)\) of \(\partial N\), where each \(U_i\) is a coordinate neighborhood of
this type. Finally, we define \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) on \(\partial N\)
by
\[
  \widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}
  =\sum_iF_i\,dx_i^2\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_i^n.
\]
Then \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) is continuous and nonvanishing and thus
defines the desired orientation of \(\partial N\). Having defined the orientation of
\(\partial N\), we may now state one of the most important results concerning
integration on manifolds, the proof of which can be found in many references (see,
e.g., Flanders 1963).

\renewcommand{\thetheorem}{B.2.\arabic{theorem}}
\setcounter{theorem}{0}
\begin{theorem}[Stokes's theorem]\label{thm:B.2.1}
Let \(N\) be an \(n\)-dimensional compact oriented manifold with boundary and let
\(\boldsymbol\alpha\) be an \((n-1)\)-form on \(M\) which is \(C^1\). Then
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\operatorname{int}(N)}d\boldsymbol\alpha
  =\int_{\partial N}\boldsymbol\alpha.
  \tag{B.2.7}\label{eq:B.2.7}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Integration of functions on an orientable manifold \(M\) can be accomplished if one
is given a \emph{volume element}, that is, a continuous nonvanishing
\(n\)-form \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\). (A volume element differs from an orientation in
that orientations are considered equivalent if they differ by positive multiples
whereas volume elements are not.) The integral of \(f\) over \(M\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \int_M f=\int_M f\boldsymbol\epsilon,
  \tag{B.2.8}\label{eq:B.2.8}
\end{equation}
where the integral of the \(n\)-form \(f\boldsymbol\epsilon\) was defined
previously.\footnote{Integration of functions on a nonorientable manifold can be
defined by choosing a continuous \lq\lq \(n\)-form modulo sign\rq\rq\
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon'\) and performing integrals of \(f\boldsymbol\epsilon'\) over
each of the local coordinate neighborhoods by choosing the sign of
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon'\) which makes the coordinate system \lq\lq right handed\rq\rq\
with respect to it.}

If one is given only the structure of a manifold, \(M\), there is no natural choice of
volume element. However, if \(M\) has a metric, \(g_{ab}\), defined on it, then a
natural choice of \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is specified up to sign (i.e., up to choice
of orientation) by the condition
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{a_1\ldots a_n}\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_n}=(-1)^s n!,
  \tag{B.2.9}\label{eq:B.2.9}
\end{equation}
where \(s\) is the number of minuses appearing in the signature of \(g_{ab}\). (Thus,
\(s=0\) for a Riemannian metric, while \(s=1\) for a Lorentzian metric.) Note that
differentiation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.9} using the derivative operator,
\(\nabla_a\), associated with the metric implies that
\begin{equation}
  2\epsilon^{a_1\ldots a_n}\nabla_b\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_n}=0,
  \tag{B.2.10}\label{eq:B.2.10}
\end{equation}
which, in turn, implies that
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_b\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_n}=0,
  \tag{B.2.11}\label{eq:B.2.11}
\end{equation}
since \(\nabla_b\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_n}\) is totally antisymmetric in its last \(n\)
indices and \(\epsilon^{a_1\ldots a_n}\) is nonvanishing. It is also worth noting that
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{a_1\ldots a_n}\epsilon_{b_1\ldots b_n}
  =(-1)^s n!\,
  \delta^{[a_1}_{b_1}\delta^{a_2}_{b_2}\cdots\delta^{a_n]}_{b_n},
  \tag{B.2.12}\label{eq:B.2.12}
\end{equation}
where \(\delta^a_b\) is the identity map on the tangent space.
Equation~\eqref{eq:B.2.12} follows from the fact that the tensors of type \((n,n)\)
on an \(n\)-dimensional manifold which are totally antisymmetric in all lower and all
upper indices form a one-dimensional vector space and thus must be proportional to
the antisymmetrized product of \(\delta^a_b\) tensors; the constant of proportionality
is fixed by the normalization condition~\eqref{eq:B.2.9}. Contraction of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.12} over \(j\) of its indices yields
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{a_1\ldots a_j a_{j+1}\ldots a_n}
  \epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_j b_{j+1}\ldots b_n}
  =(-1)^s(n-j)!\,j!\,
  \delta^{[a_{j+1}}_{b_{j+1}}\cdots\delta^{a_n]}_{b_n}.
  \tag{B.2.13}\label{eq:B.2.13}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:B.2.9} implies that the components of
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) in a right handed orthonormal basis are
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}
  =
  \begin{cases}
    (-1)^P & \text{if all \(\mu_i\) are distinct},\\
    0 & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \tag{B.2.14}\label{eq:B.2.14}
\end{equation}
where \(P\) is the signature of the permutation
\((1,\ldots,n)\to(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)\). In a coordinate basis, the components of
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n,\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_n}
  g^{\mu_1\nu_1}\cdots g^{\mu_n\nu_n}
  \epsilon_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\ldots\nu_n}
  =(-1)^s n!.
  \tag{B.2.15}\label{eq:B.2.15}
\end{equation}
But the left-hand side of this expression is just
\((n!)(\epsilon_{12\ldots n})^2\) times the determinant of the matrix
\((g^{\mu\nu})\), and \(\det(g^{\mu\nu})=1/\det(g_{\mu\nu})\). Thus, choosing the
plus sign appropriate for a right handed coordinate system, we find
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{12\ldots n}
  =\left[(-1)^s\det(g_{\mu\nu})\right]^{1/2}
  =\sqrt{\lvert g\rvert},
  \tag{B.2.16}\label{eq:B.2.16}
\end{equation}
where \(g=\det(g_{\mu\nu})\). Thus, in any (right handed) coordinate basis, the
natural volume element defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.9} takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\epsilon
  =\sqrt{\lvert g\rvert}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
  \tag{B.2.17}\label{eq:B.2.17}
\end{equation}

Using the natural volume element \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) associated with a metric, we
can convert Stokes's theorem, Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.7}, into a \lq\lq Gauss's law\rq\rq\
form. Let \(N\) be an oriented, compact \(n\)-dimensional manifold with boundary.
Let \(g_{ab}\) be a metric on \(N\) with associated volume element
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon\). Given any \(C^1\) vector field \(v^a\), we obtain an
\((n-1)\)-form \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) by
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{a_1\ldots a_{n-1}}
  =\epsilon_{ba_1\ldots a_{n-1}}v^b.
  \tag{B.2.18}\label{eq:B.2.18}
\end{equation}
We have
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  (d\boldsymbol\alpha)_{ca_1\ldots a_{n-1}}
  & =n\nabla_{[c}\bigl(\epsilon_{\lvert b\rvert a_1\ldots a_{n-1}}v^b\bigr)\\
  & =n\epsilon_{b[a_1\ldots a_{n-1}}\nabla_{c]}v^b,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{B.2.19}\label{eq:B.2.19}
\end{equation}
where Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.11} was used. On the other hand any totally antisymmetric
tensor of type \((0,n)\) must be proportional to \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\), so
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{b[a_1\ldots a_{n-1}}\nabla_{c]}v^b
  =h\epsilon_{ca_1\ldots a_{n-1}}.
  \tag{B.2.20}\label{eq:B.2.20}
\end{equation}
The function \(h\) may be evaluated by contracting with
\(\epsilon^{ca_1\ldots a_{n-1}}\) and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.13}. We obtain
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_bv^b=nh.
  \tag{B.2.21}\label{eq:B.2.21}
\end{equation}
Thus, we find
\begin{equation}
  d\boldsymbol\alpha=(\nabla_av^a)\boldsymbol\epsilon,
  \tag{B.2.22}\label{eq:B.2.22}
\end{equation}
and thus Stokes's theorem states that
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\operatorname{int}(N)}\nabla_av^a
  =\int_{\partial N}\epsilon_{ba_1\ldots a_{n-1}}v^b,
  \tag{B.2.23}\label{eq:B.2.23}
\end{equation}
where the natural volume element \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) on \(N\) is understood in
the integral on the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.23}.

The right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.23} can be reexpressed as follows. The
metric \(g_{ab}\) on \(N\) induces a tensor field \(h_{ab}\) on \(\partial N\) by
restriction of \(g_{ab}\) to vectors tangent to \(\partial N\). If \(h_{ab}\) is
nondegenerate---which will be the case if \(\partial N\) is not a null
surface---we may use it to define a volume element
\(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) on \(\partial N\). It is not difficult to show
that
\begin{equation}
  \frac1n\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_n}
  =n_{[a_1}\widetilde\epsilon_{a_2\ldots a_n]},
  \tag{B.2.24}\label{eq:B.2.24}
\end{equation}
where \(n^b\) is the unit normal to \(\partial N\) and is chosen to be
\lq\lq outward pointing\rq\rq\ if spacelike and \lq\lq inward pointing\rq\rq\ if
timelike in order that \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) be of the orientation class
used in Stokes's theorem. Contracting \(v^a\) into both sides of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.24} and restricting the resulting \((n-1)\)-forms to vectors
tangent to \(\partial N\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{ba_1\ldots a_{n-1}}v^b
  =(n_bv^b)\widetilde\epsilon_{a_1\ldots a_{n-1}},
  \tag{B.2.25}\label{eq:B.2.25}
\end{equation}
where we view both sides of this equation as forms on \(\partial N\). Thus, if
\(\partial N\) is not null, we can express Stokes's theorem in the form
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\operatorname{int}(N)}\nabla_av^a
  =\int_{\partial N}n_av^a
  \tag{B.2.26}\label{eq:B.2.26}
\end{equation}
for all \(C^1\) vector fields \(v^a\) where the natural volume elements
\(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) and \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) on
\(\operatorname{int}(N)\) and \(\partial N\), respectively, are understood. Of
course, if \(\partial N\) is null, Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.23} still applies. Furthermore,
in the null case, if we choose any \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) on
\(\partial N\) in the orientation class used in Stokes's theorem and define \(n^a\)
to be the normal to \(\partial N\) such that Eq.~\eqref{eq:B.2.24} holds, then
Stokes's theorem again takes the form~\eqref{eq:B.2.26}.
