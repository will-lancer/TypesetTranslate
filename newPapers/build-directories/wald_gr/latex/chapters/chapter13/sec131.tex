\subsection{Spinors in Minkowski Spacetime}\label{sec:13.1}

The main purpose of this section is to motivate the definition of spinor fields on
Minkowski spacetime and establish some of their basic properties. We shall do so by
investigating the general issue of what mathematical entities may represent physical
fields in Minkowski spacetime. Our approach will be group-theoretic in nature. We
first shall argue that if \lq\lq special covariance\rq\rq\ of the physical laws holds,
then the isometry group of a spacetime acts in a natural way on the states of a
physical system. In the case of a quantum theory in Minkowski spacetime, we thereby
obtain a unitary representation up to phase of the Poincar\'e group. The study of
these representations leads to consideration of the group \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb
C)\), consisting of linear maps of unit determinant acting on a complex
two-dimensional vector space, \(W\). The notion of spinor fields in Minkowski
spacetime then is obtained by assigning vectors in \(W\) to points of spacetime in an
appropriate way.

To begin, we give a general argument that there should exist an action of the
isometry group of a spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) on the collection, \(\mathcal S\), of
states of a physical theory defined on that spacetime. We shall assume that the
physical properties of each state in \(\mathcal S\) can be characterized by local
measurements made at each spacetime event by a family of observers. A good example
of an \(\mathcal S\) satisfying this property is the collection of states of a physical
system that can be described by tensor fields of a specified type on \(M\) (with the
components of these fields corresponding to the local physically measurable
quantities); but since our purpose is to investigate the possibilities for what
\(\mathcal S\) may consist of, we leave \(\mathcal S\) unspecified. Consider a family
of observers, \(O\), equipped with measuring apparatus on \(M\). We shall assume
that these observers can be characterized by specifying an orthonormal basis
\((e_\alpha)^a\), with \(\alpha=0,1,2,3\), for the tangent space at each point of
\(M\). Here the first vector \((e_0)^a\) in the basis at each event is chosen tangent
to the world line of the observer at that event, and the remaining basis vectors
\((e_\alpha)^a\), for \(\alpha=1,2,3\), serve as references for how the apparatus he
carries is aligned. Since all experiments in physics measure numbers, associated
with each \(s\in\mathcal S\) there should be a collection of numbers corresponding to
the outcomes of a complete set of measurements on the state \(s\) made by these
observers. We shall assume for simplicity that at each \(x\in M\) only a finite
number, \(k\), of measurements need be made. Then for the given spacetime
\((M,g_{ab})\) and the given family, \(O\), of observers, we obtain a map
\(f_O:M\times\mathcal S\longrightarrow\mathbb R^k\) which uniquely characterizes
each \(s\in\mathcal S\) in terms of the measurements made by these observers. A
different family, \(\widetilde O\), of observers would, in general, obtain a different
map, \(f_{\widetilde O}\), i.e., the numerical results of the measurements on \(s\)
may depend on how the observers move and how they orient their measuring apparatus.

Consider, now, a diffeomorphism \(\phi:M\longrightarrow M\) and allow \(\phi\) to
map the basis fields \((e_\alpha)^a\) into \(\phi_*(e_\alpha)^a\) in the manner
described in appendix~C. In general, the basis \(\phi_*(e_\alpha)^a\) will not be
orthonormal at each point and thus will not correspond to a physically realizable
family of observers. However, when (and only when) \(\phi\) is an isometry,
\(\phi_*(e_\alpha)^a\) will be orthonormal, and we can use \(\phi\) to map our
original family, \(O\), of physical observers associated with the basis field
\((e_\alpha)^a\) into a new family \(\widetilde O\) of physical observers associated
with \(\phi_*(e_\alpha)^a\).

If the laws of physics are \lq\lq specially covariant\rq\rq\ under the isometries of
\((M,g_{ab})\) (see chapter~4), then any physically possible result of a set of
measurements made by \(O\) must be a physically possible result for a set of
measurements made by \(\widetilde O\). In other words, given any
\(s\in\mathcal S\), there must exist an \(\widetilde s\in\mathcal S\) such that the
results of measurements by \(O\) on \(s\) are identical to the results of
measurements by \(\widetilde O\) on \(\widetilde s\), i.e., for each \(x\in M\) we
have \(f_O(x,s)=f_{\widetilde O}(\phi(x),\widetilde s)\). Thus, associated with each
isometry \(\phi\), we obtain a map \(\widetilde\phi:\mathcal S\longrightarrow
\mathcal S\), defined by the requirement that \(\widetilde\phi(s)\)
\lq\lq look the same\rq\rq\ to the observers \(\widetilde O\) as \(s\)
\lq\lq looks\rq\rq\ to \(O\). In the case where \(\mathcal S\) consists of tensor
fields, this map is simply the map \(\phi_*\) defined in appendix~C.

The isometries on \((M,g_{ab})\) form a Lie group (see section~7.2). We shall denote
the abstract group isomorphic to the group of isometries by \(G\) and denote the
isometry associated with \(g\in G\) as \(\phi_g\). By the above remarks, for each
\(g\in G\) we obtain a map \(\widetilde\phi_g:\mathcal S\longrightarrow\mathcal S\).
Furthermore, from the physical criteria which defined the map
\(\widetilde\phi_g\), it is clear that for all \(g_1,g_2\in G\), we have
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde\phi_{g_1}\circ\widetilde\phi_{g_2}
  =\widetilde\phi_{g_1g_2}.
  \tag{13.1.1}\label{eq:13.1.1}
\end{equation}

We specialize, now, to the case of Minkowski spacetime
\((\mathbb R^4,\eta_{ab})\). The isometry group of Minkowski spacetime is the
extended Poincar\'e group, but since, as mentioned in section~4.2, the laws of
physics in Minkowski spacetime are believed to be \lq\lq specially covariant\rq\rq\
only under proper Poincar\'e transformations, we shall take \(G\) to be the group of
proper Poincar\'e transformations. In order to proceed further, we must specify the
nature of the physical theory in more detail. We shall take the framework of our
physical theory to be that of quantum theory. As we shall see below, spinors will
emerge as candidates for physical fields in quantum theory.

In quantum theory, states of a system are represented as vectors with unit norm in a
Hilbert space\footnote{See the beginning of section~14.2 for the definition of a
Hilbert space. An introduction to some of the basic properties of a Hilbert space also
is given there, but this discussion is not essential for the present chapter.}
\(\mathcal H\). However, two vectors which differ by an overall phase factor, i.e.,
a complex number \(c\) with \(\lvert c\rvert=1\), represent the same physical state.
Thus, the physical states, \(\mathcal S\), are the unit rays in the Hilbert space,
i.e., the equivalence classes of unit norm vectors differing only by a phase factor.
Let \(\widetilde\phi_g:\mathcal S\longrightarrow\mathcal S\) denote the map of
\(\mathcal S\) into itself associated with the isometry \(\phi_g\). We may associate
with \(\widetilde\phi_g\) a map \(U_g:\mathcal H\longrightarrow\mathcal H\), where
the phase of \(U_g(\psi)\) for all \(\psi\in\mathcal H\) can be chosen arbitrarily.
The requirement that \(\widetilde\phi_g\) take the states in \(\mathcal S\)
characterized by observer \(O\) to states which \lq\lq look the same\rq\rq\ to
observer \(\widetilde O\) implies that all transition probabilities must be preserved
by \(\widetilde\phi_g\). This implies that each \(U_g\) must satisfy
\(\lvert(U_g\psi_1,U_g\psi_2)\rvert=\lvert(\psi_1,\psi_2)\rvert\) for all
\(\psi_1,\psi_2\in\mathcal H\). As shown by Wigner (1959), this implies that \(U_g\)
can be re-phased so that it is either unitary or antiunitary. Since all proper
Poincar\'e transformations can be continuously deformed to the identity element, the
continuous dependence of \(\widetilde\phi_g\) with \(g\) requires that \(U_g\) be
unitary. The requirement~\eqref{eq:13.1.1} on \(\widetilde\phi_g\) implies that
\(U_g\) must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  U_{g_1}U_{g_2}=\omega(g_1,g_2)U_{g_1g_2},
  \tag{13.1.2}\label{eq:13.1.2}
\end{equation}
where \(\omega\) is a phase factor,
\(\lvert\omega(g_1,g_2)\rvert=1\).

We take this opportunity to introduce some terminology. Let \(G\) and \(G'\) be
groups. A map \(h:G\longrightarrow G'\) is said to be a \emph{homomorphism} if for
all \(g_1,g_2\in G\) we have \(h(g_1g_2)=h(g_1)h(g_2)\). Now, the collection,
\(\mathrm{GL}(V)\), of one-to-one, onto linear maps of a (not necessarily
finite-dimensional) vector space, \(V\), into itself has a natural group structure.
A homomorphism \(h:G\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}(V)\) is called a
\emph{representation} of the group \(G\), and \(V\) is said to be its
\emph{representation space}. A map of \(G\) into \(\mathrm{GL}(V)\) satisfying a
relation of the form~\eqref{eq:13.1.2} is called a \emph{projective representation}
or a \lq\lq representation up to phase.\rq\rq\

Our strategy for obtaining physical fields on spacetime now may be explained. We
have seen above that the Hilbert space of quantum states is the representation space
for a unitary representation up to phase of the proper Poincar\'e group. Therefore,
we may pose the mathematical problem of finding all the unitary representations up
to phase of the Poincar\'e group on a Hilbert space which depend continuously on the
Poincar\'e group elements in the sense described by Wigner (1939). We then may seek
to define fields on spacetime corresponding to all the representations we have found.

The problem of finding the continuous unitary representations up to phase of the
Poincar\'e group was systematically analyzed by Wigner (1939). The first key result
of Wigner's analysis is that the unitary maps \(U_g\) can be redefined by
multiplication by phase factors in such a way as to make
\(\omega(g_1,g_2)=\pm1\). Thus, the \(U_g\) may be chosen so that they yield a
\lq\lq representation up to sign.\rq\rq\ (The proof of this result is nontrivial and
comprises a substantial portion of Wigner's analysis.) The next key result (see
Bargmann 1954) is that the representations up to sign of the Poincar\'e group
correspond precisely to the true representations of its universal covering group. We
digress, now, to define the term \lq\lq universal covering group.\rq\rq\

Let \(M\) be a connected manifold and let \(p,q\in M\). Let
\(\gamma:[0,1]\longrightarrow M\) and \(\gamma':[0,1]\longrightarrow M\) be
continuous curves with \(\gamma(0)=\gamma'(0)=p\) and
\(\gamma(1)=\gamma'(1)=q\). We say that \(\gamma\) and \(\gamma'\) are
\emph{homotopic} if they can be continuously deformed into each other keeping their
endpoints fixed, i.e., if there exists a continuous function
\(F:[0,1]\times[0,1]\longrightarrow M\) such that \(F(0,t)=\gamma(t)\) and
\(F(1,t)=\gamma'(t)\) for all \(t\in[0,1]\) and \(F(s,0)=p\),
\(F(s,1)=q\) for all \(s\in[0,1]\). It is easy to check that homotopy defines an
equivalence relation between curves from \(p\) to \(q\). \(M\) is said to be
\emph{simply connected} if every closed curve in \(M\) [i.e., every curve with
\(\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)\)] is homotopic to the trivial curve
\(\gamma(t)=\gamma(0)\) for all \(t\in[0,1]\). Equivalently, \(M\) is simply
connected if for each \(p,q\in M\) all the curves connecting \(p\) and \(q\) are
homotopic. Note that the number of homotopy equivalence classes of curves between
\(p,q\in M\) is independent of the choice of \(p\) and \(q\). Note also that the set
of homotopy equivalence classes of closed curves through \(p\) can be given a natural
group structure as follows. If \(\Gamma_1\) denotes the equivalence class of curve
\(\gamma_1\), and \(\Gamma_2\) denotes the equivalence class of curve \(\gamma_2\),
we define the product \(\Gamma_1\Gamma_2\) to be the equivalence class, \(\Gamma\),
of the curve \(\gamma\) defined by
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(t)=
  \begin{cases}
    \gamma_1(2t),&0\le t\le\tfrac12,\\
    \gamma_2(1-2t),&\tfrac12\le t\le1,
  \end{cases}
  \tag{13.1.3}\label{eq:13.1.3}
\end{equation}
i.e., \(\gamma\) goes first around \(\gamma_1\) and then around \(\gamma_2\). It is
easy to check that \(\Gamma\) is independent of the choice of representative curves
\(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) in \(\Gamma_1\) and \(\Gamma_2\) and that the above
product law defines a group structure, with the identity element consisting of the
equivalence class of the trivial closed curve. This group is called the
\emph{fundamental group} of \(M\) and is denoted \(\pi_1(M)\). Thus, \(M\) is simply
connected if and only if \(\pi_1(M)\) is the trivial group consisting only of the
identity element.

Now, fix \(p\in M\) and define \(\widehat M\) to be the set of equivalence classes
of curves between \(p\) and \(q\) as \(q\) ranges over \(M\). There is a natural map
\(f:\widehat M\longrightarrow M\) which takes each \(\widehat q\in\widehat M\) into
the endpoint \(q\in M\) of the equivalence class of curves \(\widehat q\). This map
will be one-to-one if and only if \(M\) is simply connected. However, even if \(M\)
is not simply connected, we always can find a simply connected neighborhood, \(U\),
of \(q\in M\) and look at the equivalence classes of curves obtained by following a
curve in \(\widehat q\) from \(p\) to \(q\) and adjoining to it a curve lying entirely
within \(U\). In this way, we obtain a one-to-one, onto map from a region,
\(\widehat U\), of \(\widehat M\) into \(U\). By requiring that all such maps be
diffeomorphisms we define a manifold structure on \(\widehat M\). The manifold
\(\widehat M\) thus obtained is called the \emph{universal covering manifold} of
\(M\). (Note that \(\widehat M\) is independent of the choice of \(p\in M\) used in
the construction, i.e., different choices of \(p\) result in diffeomorphic universal
covering manifolds.) It follows directly from the construction of \(\widehat M\) that
we have a \lq\lq covering map\rq\rq\ \(f:\widehat M\longrightarrow M\) such that
for every simply connected open set \(U\subseteq M\), \(f\) is a diffeomorphism
between each connected component of \(f^{-1}(U)\) and \(U\). Furthermore,
\(\widehat M\) is simply connected, as can be seen from the fact that \(f\) maps every
closed curve in \(\widehat M\) into a closed curve in \(M\) which is homotopically
trivial. In fact, the preceding two properties characterize \(\widehat M\).

Since a Lie group, \(G\), also is a manifold (see section~7.2), the above
construction can be performed on \(G\) to obtain a universal covering manifold,
\(\widehat G\). Furthermore, a group structure can be defined on \(\widehat G\) as
follows. We choose the fixed point \(p\) in the construction of \(\widehat G\) to be
the identity element, \(e\), of \(G\). Let \(\widehat g_1,\widehat g_2\in\widehat G\)
and let \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\) be curves in \(G\) belonging, respectively, to the
equivalence classes \(\widehat g_1,\widehat g_2\) of curves from \(e\) to points of
\(G\). We define \(\widehat g_1\widehat g_2\) to be the equivalence class of the
curve \(\gamma(t)=\gamma_1(t)\gamma_2(t)\), where
\(\gamma_1(t)\gamma_2(t)\) is defined by the group product in \(G\). The equivalence
class of \(\gamma\) is independent of the choice of representative curves
\(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\), so \(\widehat g_1\widehat g_2\) is well defined. This product
makes \(\widehat G\) a Lie group, and it also makes the covering map
\(f:\widehat G\longrightarrow G\) a group homomorphism. \(\widehat G\) is called
the \emph{universal covering group} of \(G\), and it is characterized by the
conditions that \(\widehat G\) is the universal covering manifold of \(G\) and that
its covering map, \(f\), is a homomorphism.

The Poincar\'e Lie group fails to be simply connected. A rotation by \(2\pi\) cannot
be continuously deformed to the identity transformation. More precisely, the curve
\(\gamma\) in the group manifold defined by \(\gamma(t)=\) rotation about a fixed
axis by the angle \(2\pi t\) begins and ends at the identity element, \(e\), but is
not homotopic to the trivial curve \(\gamma'(t)=e\) for all \(t\). What is perhaps
more surprising is that the homotopy class of the \(2\pi\)-rotation is the only
nontrivial homotopy class of closed curves through \(e\) in the Poincar\'e group,
i.e., every closed curve passing through \(e\) either is homotopic to the
\(2\pi\)-rotation curve \(\gamma\) or the trivial curve \(\gamma'\). In particular, a
rotation by \(4\pi\) about a fixed axis can be continuously deformed to
\(\gamma'\) as illustrated in Figure~13.1. Thus, the universal covering group,
\(\widehat G\), of the Poincar\'e group, \(G\), will yield a twofold covering of
\(G\). As we shall see below, \(\widehat G\) is isomorphic to the group
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) of translations and linear maps of unit determinant on
a two-dimensional complex vector space. Our next major task, therefore, will be to
establish some properties of the groups \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) and
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) and the vector space upon which they naturally act.
These properties are basic to the notion of spinors, and we now shall make a lengthy
digression to study these properties.

Let \(W\) be a two-dimensional vector space over the complex numbers. Following the
index notation conventions discussed in chapter~2, we shall use Latin superscripts
to denote vectors in \(W\) and Greek superscripts to denote components of vectors in
\(W\) with respect to a basis. However, in order to distinguish vectors in \(W\) from
tangent vectors in spacetime, we shall use capital letters in the superscripts. Thus,
for example, \(\xi^A\) denotes an element of \(W\), and \(\xi^\Sigma\) denotes a
component of \(\xi^A\). As in the case of a real vector space, starting with \(W\) we
can construct the dual space, \(W^\vee\), composed of linear maps from \(W\) into
\(\mathbb C\). Then \(W^\vee\) is a two-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb C\),
and we shall denote its elements with subscripts. Thus, for example, \(\lambda_A\)
denotes an element of \(W^\vee\). However, for a vector space, \(W\), over
\(\mathbb C\), we also can form the \emph{complex conjugate dual space}
\(\overline{W}^{\,\vee}\), composed of the antilinear maps from \(W\) into \(\mathbb C\).
(Here a map \(f:W\longrightarrow\mathbb C\) is said to be \emph{antilinear} if
\(f(\xi_1^A+\xi_2^A)=f(\xi_1^A)+f(\xi_2^A)\) and
\(f(c\xi^A)=\overline c\,f(\xi^A)\) for all \(\xi_1^A,\xi_2^A,\xi^A\in W\) and for
all \(c\in\mathbb C\).) Then \(\overline{W}^{\,\vee}\) also is a two-dimensional vector
space over \(\mathbb C\), and we shall denote its elements with a primed lower
index, e.g., \(\psi_{A'}\in\overline{W}^{\,\vee}\). Finally, we define the
\emph{complex conjugate space}, \(\overline W\), to be the dual space of
\(\overline{W}^{\,\vee}\), and we denote elements of \(\overline W\) with a primed
upper index, e.g., \(\phi^{A'}\in\overline W\). Note that there is a natural
antilinear one-to-one correspondence between elements \(\xi\in W\) and
\(\phi\in\overline W\) defined by the requirement that \(\psi(\xi)=\phi(\psi)\) for
all \(\psi\in\overline{W}^{\,\vee}\). (We omitted the indices here since their presence
could cause confusion.) We call this map of \(W\) onto \(\overline W\) (as well as
the inverse map of \(\overline W\) onto \(W\)) \emph{complex conjugation}. We denote
the image of \(\xi^A\in W\) under complex conjugation as
\(\overline\xi^{A'}\in\overline W\), and similarly denote the image of
\(\phi^{A'}\in\overline W\) as \(\overline\phi^A\in W\).

A tensor, \(T\), of type \((k,l;k',l')\) over \(W\) is defined as a multilinear map,
\[
  T:\underbrace{W^\vee\times\cdots\times W^\vee}_{k}
  \times\underbrace{W\times\cdots\times W}_{l}
  \times\underbrace{\overline{W}^{\,\vee}\times\cdots\times\overline{W}^{\,\vee}}_{k'}
  \times\underbrace{\overline W\times\cdots\times\overline W}_{l'}
  \longrightarrow\mathbb C.
\]
We shall use a natural generalization of the index notation for tensors over a real
vector space to denote tensors over \(W\). Thus, for example, \(T^{AB}{}_{C}{}^{D'}\)
denotes a tensor of type \((2,1;1,0)\). The relative ordering of the primed and
unprimed indices is irrelevant, e.g., \(T^{AD'}{}_{C}{}^B\) denotes the same tensor
as \(T^{AB}{}_{C}{}^{D'}\). However, the ordering within the unprimed indices and
within the primed indices is as relevant as in the case of tensors over a real vector
space. The complex conjugation map of vectors extends to tensors and maps a tensor
\(T\) of type \((k,l;k',l')\) into a tensor, denoted \(\overline T\), of type
\((k',l';k,l)\). Note that \(\overline{\overline T}=T\). Finally, we now have two
distinct notions of contraction: contraction over unprimed indices, taking tensors of
type \((k,l;k',l')\) into tensors of type \((k-1,l-1;k',l')\), and contraction over
primed indices, taking tensors of type \((k,l;k',l')\) into tensors of type
\((k,l;k'-1,l'-1)\). However, contraction over one primed index and one unprimed
index is not defined. Again, we shall adopt the notation of using the same letter
twice in the indices to denote contraction.

For a two-dimensional vector space, \(W\), over \(\mathbb C\), the vector space of
antisymmetric tensors of type \((0,2;0,0)\) is one-dimensional. If a particular such
tensor \(\epsilon_{AB}=-\epsilon_{BA}\) is chosen, the pair
\((W,\epsilon_{AB})\) is called a \emph{spinor space}. The elements of \(W\) are
called \emph{spinors}, and tensors over \(W\) are called \emph{spinorial tensors}.
We can use \(\epsilon_{AB}\) to map spinors into dual spinors via
\(\xi^A\mapsto\epsilon_{AB}\xi^A\). Since \(\epsilon_{AB}\) is nondegenerate
(i.e., \(\epsilon_{AB}\xi^A\ne0\) unless \(\xi^A=0\)) we obtain from
\(\epsilon_{AB}\) an isomorphism of \(W\) and \(W^\vee\) much like the isomorphism that
would be obtained from a metric on \(W\). We shall take advantage of this similarity
by employing notational conventions for \(\epsilon_{AB}\) similar to those employed
for metrics in chapter~2. First, we shall denote the dual spinor
\(\epsilon_{AB}\xi^A\) as simply \(\xi_B\), and more generally, use
\(\epsilon_{AB}\) to lower unprimed indices on all spinorial tensors.

Note, however, that since \(\epsilon_{AB}\) is antisymmetric in \(A\) and \(B\), it
makes a difference which index of \(\epsilon_{AB}\) is contracted with \(\xi^C\) in
the index-lowering operation. We follow the standard convention of using contraction
over the \emph{first} index of \(\epsilon_{AB}\) to lower indices. Thus, we have
\begin{equation}
  \xi_B=\epsilon_{AB}\xi^A=-\epsilon_{BA}\xi^A.
  \tag{13.1.4}\label{eq:13.1.4}
\end{equation}
Following standard conventions, we define \(\epsilon^{AB}\) to be \emph{minus} the
inverse of \(\epsilon_{AB}\), i.e., \(\epsilon^{AB}\) is the antisymmetric tensor of
type \((2,0;0,0)\) which satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{AB}\epsilon_{BC}=-\delta^A{}_C,
  \tag{13.1.5}\label{eq:13.1.5}
\end{equation}
where \(\delta^A{}_C\) denotes the identity map on \(W\). In order to compensate for
the minus sign in equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.5} we use contraction over the
\emph{second} index of \(\epsilon^{AB}\) to raise an index; e.g., for
\(\mu_A\in W^\vee\) we have
\begin{equation}
  \mu^A=\epsilon^{AB}\mu_B=-\epsilon^{BA}\mu_B.
  \tag{13.1.6}\label{eq:13.1.6}
\end{equation}
Thus, it is essential to pay careful attention to index positions in order to prevent
sign errors. Note that we have
\begin{align}
  \xi_A\phi^A
    &=\left(\epsilon_{BA}\xi^B\right)\phi^A \notag\\
    &=-\epsilon_{AB}\xi^B\phi^A \notag\\
    &=-\xi^B\phi_B.
  \tag{13.1.7}\label{eq:13.1.7}
\end{align}
In particular, for any spinor \(\xi^A\), we have \(\xi_A\xi^A=0\). It also should be
noted that confusion can arise with the symbol \(\delta^A{}_C\), which could be
interpreted either as (a) the identity operator on \(W\) or as (b) the identity
operator, \(\delta_B{}^D\), on \(W^\vee\) with its first index raised and its second
index lowered. [Tensors (a) and (b) differ by a minus sign.] Thus, it is preferable
to use \(\epsilon_C{}^A\) to denote the identity operators on both \(W\) and \(W^\vee\),
since no confusion in interpretation arises for this symbol. Finally, we denote the
tensors obtained from \(\epsilon_{AB}\) and \(\epsilon^{AB}\) via complex
conjugation as \(\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\) and
\(\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}\) and use them to lower and raise primed indices, with
the same convention of contraction over the first index of
\(\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\) and the second index of
\(\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}\).

A linear map \(L:W\longrightarrow W\) is represented by a tensor \(L^A{}_B\). The
determinant of \(L\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \det(L)=\frac12\epsilon_{AB}\epsilon^{CD}L^A{}_CL^B{}_D.
  \tag{13.1.8}\label{eq:13.1.8}
\end{equation}
It is well known that \(L^A{}_B\) is one-to-one, onto, and hence invertible if and
only if its determinant is nonvanishing. We define \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) to
be the group of linear maps of \(W\) into itself which have unit determinant. Here
the group product is defined by composition, i.e.,
\((LM)^A{}_B=L^A{}_CM^C{}_B\), and the group inverse is given by the inverse of the
linear map. Since the determinant condition is one complex equation on the four
complex components of \(L^A{}_B\), it takes six real parameters to specify an
element of \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\). Indeed, using the polar decomposition
theorem---which states that with respect to any inner product introduced on \(W\),
every element of \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) can be written uniquely as the
composition of a unitary map of determinant one [i.e., an element of
\(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)] and a positive, self-adjoint map---it can be shown that
\(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) has the natural manifold structure
\(S^3\times\mathbb R^3\). Thus, since the group operations are smooth with respect
to this manifold structure, \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is a six-dimensional Lie
group. Since \(S^3\) and \(\mathbb R^3\) are simply connected, it also follows that
\(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is simply connected. Note that the condition that
\(L^A{}_B\) have unit determinant is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  L^A{}_CL^B{}_D\epsilon_{AB}=\epsilon_{CD},
  \tag{13.1.9}\label{eq:13.1.9}
\end{equation}
which states that \(\epsilon_{AB}\) is preserved under the action of \(L^A{}_B\).

The relation between \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) and the Lorentz group now may be
established. The tensors of type \((1,0;1,0)\) comprise a four (complex)
dimensional vector space, \(Y\). A convenient basis of \(Y\) can be defined as
follows. Let \(o^A,\iota^A\) be a basis of \(W\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
  o_A\iota^A=\epsilon_{AB}o^A\iota^B=1.
  \tag{13.1.10}\label{eq:13.1.10}
\end{equation}
Then the tensors
\begin{align}
  t^{AA'}&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(o^A\overline o^{A'}+
    \iota^A\overline\iota^{A'}\right),
  \tag{13.1.11}\label{eq:13.1.11}\\
  x^{AA'}&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(o^A\overline\iota^{A'}+
    \iota^A\overline o^{A'}\right),
  \tag{13.1.12}\label{eq:13.1.12}\\
  y^{AA'}&=\frac{\ii}{\sqrt2}\left(o^A\overline\iota^{A'}-
    \iota^A\overline o^{A'}\right),
  \tag{13.1.13}\label{eq:13.1.13}\\
  z^{AA'}&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(o^A\overline o^{A'}-
    \iota^A\overline\iota^{A'}\right)
  \tag{13.1.14}\label{eq:13.1.14}
\end{align}
comprise a basis of \(Y\). Now, complex conjugation maps \(Y\) into itself, and the
elements \(\phi^{AA'}\) of \(Y\) which are taken into themselves under complex
conjugation, \(\overline\phi^{A'A}=\phi^{AA'}\), are called \emph{real}. It is
straightforward to check that the above basis elements of \(Y\) are real and,
further, that the real elements of \(Y\) are precisely those which can be written as
sums of these basis elements with real coefficients. Thus, the real elements of \(Y\)
comprise a four-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb R\), which we shall denote
as \(V\). The tensor
\begin{equation}
  g_{AA'BB'}=\epsilon_{AB}\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}
  \tag{13.1.15}\label{eq:13.1.15}
\end{equation}
yields a multilinear map \(V\times V\longrightarrow\mathbb R\), since it is easily
verified that \(g_{AA'BB'}\phi^{AA'}\psi^{BB'}\) is real for
\(\phi^{AA'},\psi^{BB'}\in V\). Furthermore, \(g_{AA'BB'}\) is nondegenerate with
signature \(+---\) and thus defines a Lorentz metric on \(V\). (This can be verified
explicitly by checking that the basis vectors
\eqref{eq:13.1.11}--\eqref{eq:13.1.14} are orthogonal with respect to
\(g_{AA'BB'}\), that \(g_{AA'BB'}t^{AA'}t^{BB'}=1\), and that the similar norms of
\(x^{AA'}\), \(y^{AA'}\), and \(z^{AA'}\) are \(-1\).)

Now, associated with each map \(L^A{}_B\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is the map
\(\Lambda:V\longrightarrow V\) defined by
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}=L^A{}_B\overline L^{A'}{}_{B'}.
  \tag{13.1.16}\label{eq:13.1.16}
\end{equation}
Since by equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.9} \(L^A{}_B\) preserves
\(\epsilon_{AB}\), it follows that \(\Lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}\) preserves
\(g_{AA'BB'}\), i.e., we have
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda^{AA'}{}_{CC'}\Lambda^{BB'}{}_{DD'}g_{AA'BB'}
  =g_{CC'DD'}.
  \tag{13.1.17}\label{eq:13.1.17}
\end{equation}
But, by definition, the extended Lorentz group---denoted \(O(3,1)\)---consists
precisely of the metric preserving linear maps on a four-dimensional real vector
space with a Lorentz signature metric. Thus, \(\Lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}\) is a Lorentz
transformation on \(V\)! Indeed, the Lorentz transformations which arise in this way
comprise the proper Lorentz group---denoted \(\Lambda\)---as can be verified
explicitly from the component form of this correspondence which we shall obtain below
(see equations~[13.1.31] and~[13.1.32]). Hence, we have found that associated with
every element \(L^A{}_B\) of \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is a proper Lorentz
transformation \(\Lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}\), defined by equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.16}.
Furthermore, \(L^A{}_B\) and \(M^A{}_B\) give rise to the same Lorentz group element
if and only if \(M^A{}_B=\pm L^A{}_B\). Indeed, the map
\(f:\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\longrightarrow\Lambda\) obtained from
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.16} satisfies all the properties required of the universal
covering map; namely, it is a homomorphism from the simply connected Lie group
\(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) onto \(\Lambda\), and for any simply connected open set
\(U\subseteq\Lambda\) it is a diffeomorphism between each connected component of
\(f^{-1}(U)\) and \(U\). Thus, we conclude that \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is the
universal covering group of the Lorentz group. Similarly, the group
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\)---defined by composing in the natural way the elements
of \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) with the elements of the two-complex-dimensional
translation group of \(W\)---can be seen to be the universal covering group of the
proper Poincar\'e group.

Since \(g_{AA'BB'}\) is a metric on \(V\), we can define its inverse metric
\(g^{AA'BB'}\). From equations~\eqref{eq:13.1.5} and~\eqref{eq:13.1.15}, it follows
that
\begin{equation}
  g^{AA'BB'}=\epsilon^{AB}\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}.
  \tag{13.1.18}\label{eq:13.1.18}
\end{equation}
Let \(T\) be a tensor of type \((k,l)\) over \(V\). Then, viewed as a tensor over
\(W\), \(T\) is of type \((k,l;k,l)\), i.e., \(T\) has \(k\) upper
primed--unprimed index pairs and \(l\) lower primed--unprimed index pairs. If we
view \(T\) as a tensor over \(V\), it would be natural to use \(g_{AA'BB'}\) and
\(g^{AA'BB'}\) to raise and lower \lq\lq \(V\)-indices,\rq\rq\ i.e.,
primed--unprimed index pairs. On the other hand, if we view \(T\) as a tensor over
\(W\), we already have defined the raising and lowering of indices via
\(\epsilon_{AB}\), \(\epsilon^{AB}\), \(\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\), and
\(\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}\). Fortunately, it is easily verified that these two
distinct notions of raising and lowering indices for tensors over \(V\) always yield
the same result. This is the reason why we choose to use metric signature \(+---\) in
this chapter. We could have conformed to our previous signature conventions by
defining \(g_{AA'BB'}\) to be
\(-\epsilon_{AB}\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\), but then the two notions of raising and
lowering \(V\)-indices would differ by a sign and in each calculation we would need
to specify which raising and lowering convention we are using.

It is instructive to express some of the above relations in basis component form.
With respect to a basis \(o^A,\iota^A\) satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.10}, it
is easy to check that
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{AB}=o_A\iota_B-\iota_Ao_B,
  \tag{13.1.19}\label{eq:13.1.19}
\end{equation}
since both sides give the same result when applied to \(o^A\) or \(\iota^A\). Thus,
we may represent the components \(\epsilon_{\Sigma\Omega}\) of
\(\epsilon_{AB}\) by the matrix
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\Sigma\Omega}=
  \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{13.1.20}\label{eq:13.1.20}
\end{equation}
Similarly, we have
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{AB}=o^A\iota^B-\iota^Ao^B
  \tag{13.1.21}\label{eq:13.1.21}
\end{equation}
and may represent its components as
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{\Sigma\Omega}=
  \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{13.1.22}\label{eq:13.1.22}
\end{equation}
The components of an \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) transformation \(L^A{}_B\) are
\begin{equation}
  L^\Sigma{}_\Omega=
  \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&\dd\end{pmatrix},
  \tag{13.1.23}\label{eq:13.1.23}
\end{equation}
where \(a,b,c,d\) are complex numbers satisfying
\begin{equation}
  ad-bc=1.
  \tag{13.1.24}\label{eq:13.1.24}
\end{equation}
The components of the basis elements~\eqref{eq:13.1.11}--\eqref{eq:13.1.14} of
\(V\) are
\begin{align}
  t^{\Sigma\Sigma'}&=\frac1{\sqrt2}
  \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
  \tag{13.1.25}\label{eq:13.1.25}\\
  x^{\Sigma\Sigma'}&=\frac1{\sqrt2}
  \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
  \tag{13.1.26}\label{eq:13.1.26}\\
  y^{\Sigma\Sigma'}&=-\frac1{\sqrt2}
  \begin{pmatrix}0&-\ii\\\ii&0\end{pmatrix},
  \tag{13.1.27}\label{eq:13.1.27}\\
  z^{\Sigma\Sigma'}&=\frac1{\sqrt2}
  \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{13.1.28}\label{eq:13.1.28}
\end{align}
Note that the right-hand sides of equations~\eqref{eq:13.1.25}--\eqref{eq:13.1.28}
are---apart from the factor of \(1/\sqrt2\) and the minus sign in
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.27}---just the Pauli spin matrices. An arbitrary vector
\(v^{AA'}\in V\) can be written as
\begin{equation}
  v^{AA'}=t\,t^{AA'}+x\,x^{AA'}+y\,y^{AA'}+z\,z^{AA'},
  \tag{13.1.29}\label{eq:13.1.29}
\end{equation}
and thus its components with respect to the basis of \(V\) associated with
\(o^A,\iota^A\) may be represented by the matrix
\begin{equation}
  v^{\Sigma\Sigma'}=\frac1{\sqrt2}
  \begin{pmatrix}t+z&x+\ii y\\x-\ii y&t-z\end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{13.1.30}\label{eq:13.1.30}
\end{equation}
The transformation on the components of \(v^{AA'}\) induced via
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.16} by the \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) transformation
\(L^A{}_B\) is given by
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}t'+z'&x'+\ii y'\\x'-\ii y'&t'-z'\end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&\dd\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}t+z&x+\ii y\\x-\ii y&t-z\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}\overline a&\overline c\\\overline b&\overline \dd\end{pmatrix},
  \tag{13.1.31}\label{eq:13.1.31}
\end{equation}
where ordinary matrix multiplication is understood on the right-hand side. By
rewriting equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.31} in the form
\begin{equation}
  x'{}^\mu=\sum_\nu\lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu,
  \tag{13.1.32}\label{eq:13.1.32}
\end{equation}
where \(x^\mu=(t,x,y,z)\), one obtains in explicit component form the map \(f\)
taking elements of \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) (represented as \(2\times2\) complex
matrices, \(L^\Sigma{}_\Omega\)) into elements of \(\Lambda\) (represented as
\(4\times4\) real matrices, \(\lambda^\mu{}_\nu\)).

Given a spinor \(\psi^A\in W\), we can construct a vector \(k^{AA'}\in V\) by
\begin{equation}
  k^{AA'}=\psi^A\overline\psi^{A'}.
  \tag{13.1.33}\label{eq:13.1.33}
\end{equation}
We have
\begin{equation}
  g_{AA'BB'}k^{AA'}k^{BB'}
  =(\epsilon_{AB}\psi^A\psi^B)
    (\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\overline\psi^{A'}\overline\psi^{B'})=0,
  \tag{13.1.34}\label{eq:13.1.34}
\end{equation}
so \(k^{AA'}\) is a null vector with respect to the Lorentz metric on \(V\). We
thus may view \(\psi^A\) as a \lq\lq square root\rq\rq\ of the null vector
\(k^{AA'}\). Note that for any two spinors \(\psi^A,\phi^A\) we have
\begin{equation}
  g_{AA'BB'}\psi^A\overline\psi^{A'}\phi^B\overline\phi^{B'}
  =(\psi_B\phi^B)(\overline\psi_{B'}\overline\phi^{B'})
  =\lvert\psi_B\phi^B\rvert^2.
  \tag{13.1.35}\label{eq:13.1.35}
\end{equation}
Thus, the null vectors associated with arbitrary spinors \(\psi^A\) and \(\phi^A\)
have manifestly nonnegative inner product which means (with our new metric
signature \(+---\)) that these null vectors lie on the same half of the light cone. By
convention we call this the future light cone. Thus, the vector space \(V\) has a
natural time orientation. Furthermore, the real spinorial tensor
\begin{equation}
  e_{AA'BB'CC'DD'}=\ii\left(
    \epsilon_{AB}\epsilon_{CD}\overline\epsilon_{A'C'}\overline\epsilon_{B'D'}
    -\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\overline\epsilon_{C'D'}\epsilon_{AC}\epsilon_{BD}
  \right)
  \tag{13.1.36}\label{eq:13.1.36}
\end{equation}
defines a totally antisymmetric tensor of type \((0,4)\) over \(V\) and thus yields
an orientation of \(V\).

If \(\phi^A\) differs by a phase factor from \(\psi^A\), i.e.,
\(\phi^A=c\psi^A\) with \(\lvert c\rvert=1\), then \(\phi^A\) defines the same null
vector \(k^{AA'}\) as \(\psi^A\), so the same \(k^{AA'}\) is associated with a
one-parameter family of spinors. However, we can define the real tensor
\(F^{AA'BB'}\) by
\begin{equation}
  F^{AA'BB'}=\psi^A\psi^B\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}
    +\overline\psi^{A'}\overline\psi^{B'}\epsilon^{AB}.
  \tag{13.1.37}\label{eq:13.1.37}
\end{equation}
Viewed as a tensor of type \((2,0)\) over \(V\), \(F^{AA'BB'}\) is antisymmetric,
i.e., \(F^{AA'BB'}=-F^{BB'AA'}\). Furthermore, \(F^{AA'BB'}\) satisfies
\begin{align}
  F^{AA'BB'}F_{AA'BB'}&=0,
  \tag{13.1.38}\label{eq:13.1.38}\\
  F^{AA'BB'}k_{BB'}&=0,
  \tag{13.1.39}\label{eq:13.1.39}
\end{align}
where \(k^{AA'}=\psi^A\overline\psi^{A'}\) as before. Thus, viewed as a tensor over
\(V\), \(F^{AA'BB'}\) is a null bi-vector with null vector \(k^{AA'}\), i.e.,
\(F^{AA'BB'}\) is of the form
\begin{equation}
  F^{AA'BB'}=k^{AA'}m^{BB'}-k^{BB'}m^{AA'},
  \tag{13.1.40}\label{eq:13.1.40}
\end{equation}
where \(k^{AA'}\) is null and \(m^{AA'}\) is orthogonal to \(k^{AA'}\). We call
\(F^{AA'BB'}\) the \emph{null flag} associated with spinor \(\psi^A\). Two spinors
\(\psi^A\) and \(\phi^A\) give rise to the same null flag if and only if they differ
at most by sign, \(\phi^A=\pm\psi^A\). No tensor over \(V\) can be constructed from a
spinor \(\psi^A\) which distinguishes between \(\psi^A\) and \(-\psi^A\).

We return, now, to the general issue of obtaining the possible fields on spacetime
which may arise in quantum theory. We were led by the considerations discussed above
to seek unitary representations of the group \(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) on a
Hilbert space. We now shall define spinor fields and spinorial tensor fields on
spacetime which have well-defined \lq\lq transformation laws\rq\rq\ under
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\). By constructing Hilbert spaces out of these fields (as
we shall do later), these transformation laws yield the desired representations of
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\).

We define a spinor field on Minkowski spacetime \((\mathbb R^4,\eta_{ab})\) to be
simply a map of spacetime into spinor space, \(W\). Similarly, a spinorial tensor
field of a specified type is defined as a map of spacetime into the tensor space over
\(W\) of that type. We define an action of \(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) on spinor
fields as follows. Associated with any \(g\in\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is the
transformation \(\psi^A(x)\mapsto L^A{}_B\psi^B[P^{-1}(x)]\) where
\(L^A{}_B\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)\) is the \lq\lq homogeneous part\rq\rq\ of
\(g\) and \(P\) is the Poincar\'e group element associated with \(g\). In this way,
we obtain by brute force a representation of \(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) on the
vector space of spinor fields. However, this representation does not correspond to a
true representation of the Poincar\'e group. Since there are two
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) elements for every Poincar\'e element, \(P\), when we
attempt to define a transformation, \(P^*\), on spinor fields associated with a
Poincar\'e element, \(P\), we encounter a sign ambiguity,
\begin{equation}
  (P^*\psi)^A(x)=\pm L^A{}_B\psi^B[P^{-1}(x)].
  \tag{13.1.41}\label{eq:13.1.41}
\end{equation}
This ambiguity is resolved if we are given not only \(P\) but a continuous curve in
the Poincar\'e group from \(e\) to \(P\), since such a curve is uniquely associated
with an element of \(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\). Thus, in this well-defined sense,
a spinor at point \(x\) changes sign under a rotation by \(2\pi\) about a fixed axis
at \(x\). However, given only \(P\) (and not a curve from \(e\) to \(P\)), we cannot
resolve the sign ambiguity in equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.41} in a natural way. No choice
of sign can be made so that we obtain a true representation of the Poincar\'e group.
Note that although we have defined an action up to sign of the Poincar\'e group on
spinor fields, we have \emph{not} defined the action of an arbitrary diffeomorphism
on spinor fields. Thus, in particular, the Lie derivative of a spinor field with
respect to a non-Killing vector field is undefined.

The relation between spinor fields and ordinary vector fields now may be established.
As discussed above, the real tensors of type \((1,0;1,0)\) over \(W\) form a
four-dimensional real vector space \(V\), on which the Lorentz metric
\eqref{eq:13.1.15} is defined. Let \(t^a,x^a,y^a,z^a\) be an orthonormal basis field
in Minkowski spacetime associated with a global family of inertial observers \(O\).
Let \(o^A,\iota^A\) be a basis for \(W\) satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.10}. For
each point \(x\) in Minkowski spacetime, we define the linear map \(\sigma\) which
takes vectors in \(V\) to vectors in the tangent space \(T_xM\) at \(x\) by
identifying \(t^a,x^a,y^a,z^a\) with the basis
\eqref{eq:13.1.11}--\eqref{eq:13.1.14} of \(V\). In other words, we define the
hybrid vector/spinorial tensor field \(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\) by
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^a{}_{AA'}=t^a t_{AA'}-x^a x_{AA'}-y^a y_{AA'}-z^a z_{AA'}.
  \tag{13.1.42}\label{eq:13.1.42}
\end{equation}
Then at each \(x\), \(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\) is a vector space isomorphism between
\(V\) and \(T_xM\) which preserves the Lorentz metric defined on these spaces; i.e.,
we have
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{ab}=\sigma_a{}^{AA'}\sigma_b{}^{BB'}g_{AA'BB'}.
  \tag{13.1.43}\label{eq:13.1.43}
\end{equation}
(Here all lower case indices are raised and lowered with \(\eta^{ab}\) and
\(\eta_{ab}\), while capital letter indices are raised and lowered with
\(\epsilon^{AB}\), \(\epsilon_{AB}\), and their complex conjugates.) Using
\(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\), we may map real spinorial tensor fields of type \((1,0;1,0)\)
on Minkowski spacetime into vector fields. We find, then, that the action of the
Poincar\'e group preserves this association; namely, the action
\eqref{eq:13.1.41} of Poincar\'e group element \(P\) on spinor fields induces on a
spinorial tensor \(v^{AA'}\) of type \((1,0;1,0)\) the transformation
\begin{equation}
  (P^*v)^{AA'}(x)
  =L^A{}_B\overline L^{A'}{}_{B'}v^{BB'}[P^{-1}(x)]
  =\lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}v^{BB'}[P^{-1}(x)],
  \tag{13.1.44}\label{eq:13.1.44}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}\) is the Lorentz transformation corresponding to the
\lq\lq homogeneous part\rq\rq\ of \(P\). Hence, we have
\begin{align}
  \sigma^a{}_{AA'}(P^*v)^{AA'}(x)
    &=\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}v^{BB'}[P^{-1}(x)]\notag\\
    &=\left(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\lambda^{AA'}{}_{BB'}\sigma_b{}^{BB'}\right)
      \sigma^b{}_{CC'}v^{CC'}[P^{-1}(x)]\notag\\
    &=\lambda^a{}_b v^b[P^{-1}(x)]\notag\\
    &=(P^*v)^a(x),
  \tag{13.1.45}\label{eq:13.1.45}
\end{align}
where in the last line \(P^*\) is the natural action of \(P\) on the vector field
\(v^a=\sigma^a{}_{AA'}v^{AA'}\) as defined in appendix~C for a general
diffeomorphism. Consequently, by selecting---once and for all---a map
\(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\) of the form~\eqref{eq:13.1.42}, we may consistently identify
real spinorial tensor fields of type \((1,0;1,0)\) with vector fields. It is customary
to literally make this identification\footnote{It also is customary and convenient
to make the identifications \(a=AA'\), \(b=BB'\), etc., in the labeling indices (see
Penrose and Rindler 1984), so that, for example, one may write
\(\eta_{ab}=\epsilon_{AB}\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\). However, we shall not follow
this practice here because we wish to maintain the spinor indices so that equations
in this chapter (where the metric signature is \(+---\)) will easily be distinguished
from those in other chapters (where the signature is \(-+++\)).} in our notation by
omitting the map \(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\) from our expressions and denoting, for example,
the vector field associated with \(v^{AA'}\) by simply \(v^{AA'}\) rather than by
\(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}v^{AA'}\). We shall follow this practice here. Because of our new
metric signature convention no confusion will arise as to whether indices are raised
and lowered with the metric or with \(\epsilon^{AB}\) and \(\epsilon_{AB}\).

Our physical interpretation of a spinor field \(\psi^A\) now may be given. As
described above, associated with every spinor is a null flag \(F^{AA'BB'}\) defined
by equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.37}. Using the map \(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\), we may view
\(F^{AA'BB'}\) as a tensor field of type \((2,0)\) on spacetime. However, tensor
fields are objects whose interpretation and measurability are well understood. We
take the physically measurable properties of \(\psi^A\) to be the quantities
determinable from \(F^{AA'BB'}\). Note that this implies that \(\psi^A\) and
\(-\psi^A\) are physically indistinguishable. It may seem strange that we have gone
to a great deal of trouble to define spinor fields only to interpret them by
associating them with tensor fields. However, the dynamical evolution of physical
fields represented by spinors (see below) is given by differential equations
involving the spinor fields, not just their null flags. If the region where a spinor
field, \(\psi^A\), initially is nonvanishing is disconnected, then a knowledge of the
initial value of its null flag \(F^{AA'BB'}\) (as well as time derivatives of
\(F^{AA'BB'}\)) will not suffice to determine the subsequent evolution of \(\psi^A\)
or \(F^{AA'BB'}\) since there will be a sign ambiguity in \(\psi^A\) in each of the
initial regions where it is nonvanishing, and relative sign differences in these
regions will affect its subsequent evolution. Thus, in this sense, a spinor field
contains more physically relevant information than is present in its null flag.
Furthermore, even in the case where the set where \(\psi^A\ne0\) is connected so
that it can be recovered (up to sign) from its null flag, the formulation of the
dynamical laws in terms of null flags would be extremely cumbersome. On the other
hand, these laws take a simple and natural form when formulated in terms of spinor
fields.

We take this opportunity to point out several identities which are very useful in
calculations involving spinors. Let \(T_{ab}\) be a (real) tensor of type \((0,2)\),
and let \(T_{AA'BB'}\) be the corresponding spinorial tensor. It is straightforward
to verify that
\begin{equation}
  \frac12\left(T_{AA'BB'}-T_{BB'AA'}\right)
  =T_{(AB)[A'B']}+\overline T_{[AB](A'B')},
  \tag{13.1.46}\label{eq:13.1.46}
\end{equation}
where, as in the case of ordinary tensors, round and square brackets denote,
respectively, symmetrization and antisymmetrization and we remind the reader that the
relative order of primed and unprimed indices is irrelevant. However, since
\(\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}\) spans the one-dimensional vector space of
antisymmetric spinorial tensors of type \((0,0;0,2)\), we must have
\begin{equation}
  T_{(AB)[A'B']}=\phi_{AB}\overline\epsilon_{A'B'},
  \tag{13.1.47}\label{eq:13.1.47}
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_{AB}\) is symmetric. Contracting equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.47} with
\(\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}\), we find
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{AB}=\frac12T_{(AB)A'}{}^{A'}.
  \tag{13.1.48}\label{eq:13.1.48}
\end{equation}
Similarly, we have
\begin{equation}
  \overline T_{[AB](A'B')}=\epsilon_{AB}\psi_{A'B'}.
  \tag{13.1.49}\label{eq:13.1.49}
\end{equation}
However, reality of \(T_{AA'BB'}\) implies that
\(\overline\psi_{AB}=\phi_{AB}\). Thus, we find
\begin{equation}
  \frac12\left(T_{AA'BB'}-T_{BB'AA'}\right)
  =\phi_{AB}\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}+
   \overline\phi_{A'B'}\epsilon_{AB},
  \tag{13.1.50}\label{eq:13.1.50}
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_{AB}\) is given by equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.48}. In particular, every
antisymmetric tensor \(T_{ab}=T_{[ab]}\) can be written in the form given by the
right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.50}. Similarly, we have
\begin{align}
  \frac12\left(T_{AA'BB'}+T_{BB'AA'}\right)
    &=T_{(AB)(A'B')}+T_{[AB][A'B']}\notag\\
    &=T_{(AB)(A'B')}+\frac14\epsilon_{AB}\overline\epsilon_{A'B'}T,
  \tag{13.1.51}\label{eq:13.1.51}
\end{align}
where \(T=T_A{}^A{}_{A'}{}^{A'}=T_a{}^a\). In particular, every symmetric tensor
\(T_{ab}=T_{(ab)}\) can be written in the form given by the right-hand side of
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.51}. Contracting equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.51} with
\(\overline\epsilon^{A'B'}\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \overline T_{[AB]A'}{}^{A'}=\frac12\epsilon_{AB}T.
  \tag{13.1.52}\label{eq:13.1.52}
\end{equation}
If \(T_{ab}\) is symmetric, the square brackets can be omitted from the left-hand
side of equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.52}.

Derivatives of spinor fields on Minkowski spacetime may be defined as follows. Since
a spinor field \(\psi^A\) is a map of spacetime points into \(W\), we may take the
ordinary partial derivatives of \(\psi^A\) with respect to global inertial coordinates
of Minkowski spacetime. We define \(\partial_{BB'}\psi^A\) to be the spinorial tensor
field of type \((1,1;0,1)\) whose components with respect to the basis
\(o^A,\iota^A\) are
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{\Lambda\Lambda'}\psi^\Gamma
  =\sum_\mu\sigma^\mu{}_{\Lambda\Lambda'}\frac{\partial\psi^\Gamma}{\partial x^\mu}.
  \tag{13.1.53}\label{eq:13.1.53}
\end{equation}
For a given fixed choice of \(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\), the spinorial tensor field
\(\partial_{BB'}\psi^A\) determined in this manner is independent of the choice of
global inertial coordinates, \(x^\mu\), and of spin basis \(o^A,\iota^A\), so
\(\partial_{BB'}\psi^A\) is well defined. The derivative of a spinorial tensor field
of arbitrary rank is defined similarly by taking partial derivatives of its components
with respect to global inertial coordinates and applying \(\sigma^a{}_{AA'}\). It is
easily verified that \(\partial_{AA'}\) is linear, satisfies the Leibniz rule,
commutes with contraction, and also satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{AA'}\epsilon_{BC}=0.
  \tag{13.1.54}\label{eq:13.1.54}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, for a vector field \(v^{BB'}\) we have
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{AA'}v^{BB'}=
  \sigma^a{}_{AA'}\sigma_b{}^{BB'}\partial_av^b.
  \tag{13.1.55}\label{eq:13.1.55}
\end{equation}
More generally, for an ordinary tensor field of arbitrary rank, the action of
\(\partial_{AA'}\) agrees with that of the usual derivative operator, \(\partial_a\).
Thus, \(\partial_{AA'}\) may be viewed as a generalization to spinorial tensor fields
of the usual derivative operator \(\partial_a\) on Minkowski spacetime.

Note that the derivative operators commute when applied to an arbitrary spinorial
tensor field on Minkowski spacetime,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{AA'}\partial_{BB'}=\partial_{BB'}\partial_{AA'}.
  \tag{13.1.56}\label{eq:13.1.56}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the same derivation as led to equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.52} above now
yields
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{AA'}\partial_B{}^{A'}=\frac12\epsilon_{AB}\Box,
  \tag{13.1.57}\label{eq:13.1.57}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \Box=\partial_{AA'}\partial^{AA'}.
  \tag{13.1.58}\label{eq:13.1.58}
\end{equation}

Our motivation for introducing spinor fields arose from seeking unitary
representations up to phase of the Poincar\'e group on a Hilbert space. We finally are
ready to return to this issue now and show how Hilbert spaces can be built out of
spinorial tensor fields satisfying certain equations such that the transformation law
\eqref{eq:13.1.41} leads to the desired unitary representations. Recall, first, that
a representation is said to be \emph{reducible} if all the linear maps occurring in
the representation take a fixed proper subspace of the vector space into itself. It
is easy to show that on a finite-dimensional vector space, \(V\), every unitary
representation can be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible representations,
i.e., \(V\) can be written as a direct sum of subspaces each of which is invariant
but has no proper invariant sub-subspaces. For an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
this result does not always hold, but Wigner (1939) has shown that the unitary
representations of \(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) can be decomposed into irreducible
representations. Thus, it suffices to consider only the irreducible representations,
since all representations can be constructed out of these. This simplifies the
analysis considerably.

The irreducible representations are conveniently labeled by the values of the Casimir
operators,\footnote{The \emph{universal enveloping algebra}, \(\mathcal U\), of a Lie
algebra, \(L\), is obtained by taking the direct sum,
\(\bigoplus_{k=0}^\infty\mathcal T(k,0)\), of all \lq\lq upper index\rq\rq\ tensors
over \(L\) and defining two elements to be equivalent if they can be reduced to each
other by any formal calculation in which for any \(v^a,w^a\in L\) we replace
\(v^aw^b-w^bv^a\) by \([v,w]^a\). An element \(X\in\mathcal U\) which commutes with
all \(v\in L\) is called a \emph{Casimir element}. By Stone's theorem (see, e.g.,
Reed and Simon 1972) and results of G\aa rding (1947) every unitary representation
of a Lie group \(G\) gives rise to a self-adjoint representation of its Lie algebra,
\(L\). The representatives of the Casimir elements, called \emph{Casimir operators},
commute with all representatives of \(L\) and hence with all representatives of
\(G\). Therefore, by Schur's lemma, in an irreducible representation every Casimir
operator must be a multiple of the identity operator. These numbers provide
convenient labels of the irreducible representation. The universal enveloping algebra
of the Poincar\'e group possesses two independent Casimir elements. Their
interpretation in terms of mass and spin arises from identifying the Poincar\'e Lie
algebra with the Killing fields of Minkowski spacetime.} \(m^2\) and \(S^2\), of the
Lie algebra of \(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) (which is isomorphic to the Poincar\'e
Lie algebra), which can be interpreted as representing, respectively, squared
4-momentum (i.e., mass squared) and squared angular momentum about the center of
mass. We may classify the representations into the following four cases according to
the value of \(m^2\): (a) \(m^2>0\), (b) \(m^2=0\) but the translations are not all
represented by the identity operator, \(I\), (c) \(m^2=0\) and the translations are
all represented by \(I\), and (d) \(m^2<0\).

In the representations (c), all states are translationally invariant. Thus, these
representations appear to be of no physical significance. The \lq\lq tachyonic\rq\rq\
representations, (d), also do not appear to be of physical significance, although
some tachyon field theories have been investigated (Feinberg 1967). The
representations in these classes (c) and (d) have been obtained by Bargmann (1947),
but a systematic construction of fields on Minkowski spacetime which realize these
representations does not appear to have been given. The representations of classes
(a) and (b) were obtained by Wigner (1939), and a realization of all these
representations as fields on spacetime was first given in a systematic way by
Bargmann and Wigner (1948). The representations of class (a) are characterized by
the values of \(m^2\) and \(S^2\) with \(S^2=s(s+1)\), where the \emph{spin} \(s\)
takes the values \(s=0,\tfrac12,1,\ldots\). The representations of class (b) can be
divided into two subclasses: (b1) representations characterized by a helicity
parameter \(s\) (whose magnitude also is called the \emph{spin}) with values
\(s=0,\pm\tfrac12,\pm1,\ldots\), and (b2) the so-called \lq\lq continuous
spin\rq\rq\ representations.

The fields on spacetime associated with the representations (b2) do not appear to
have any physical significance or mathematical utility. However, the representations
of classes (a) and (b1) describe all physical fields known to occur in quantum
theory. The equations which select the subspaces of spinorial tensor fields which
realize the representations of classes (a) and (b1) can be given in many equivalent
forms. A convenient choice for the representations of class (a) of mass \(m\) and
spin \(s\) is
\begin{equation}
  \left(\Box+m^2\right)\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}=0,
  \tag{13.1.59}\label{eq:13.1.59}
\end{equation}
where \(\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}\) is totally symmetric,
\(\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}=\phi^{(A_1\cdots A_n)}\), and the number of indices is
\(n=2s\). For \(s>0\), equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} also can be expressed in the
following form. We define the auxiliary variable
\(\sigma_{A_1'}{}^{A_2\cdots A_n}\) by
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{A_1'A_1}\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}
  =\frac{m}{\sqrt2}\sigma_{A_1'}{}^{A_2\cdots A_n}.
  \tag{13.1.60}\label{eq:13.1.60}
\end{equation}
From equations~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} and~\eqref{eq:13.1.57} we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \partial^{A_1'A_1}\sigma_{A_1'}{}^{A_2\cdots A_n}
  =-\frac{m}{\sqrt2}\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}.
  \tag{13.1.61}\label{eq:13.1.61}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, using equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.57} again, one may verify that the pair
of equations~\eqref{eq:13.1.60} and~\eqref{eq:13.1.61} imply
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59}, so the coupled first order system
\eqref{eq:13.1.60}, \eqref{eq:13.1.61} is equivalent to
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59}. Indeed, by repeated differentiation of
\(\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}\) and contraction over the unprimed indices, a whole
hierarchy of auxiliary variables may be defined, each of which is coupled to the
preceding variable by equations of the form~\eqref{eq:13.1.60},
\eqref{eq:13.1.61}.

Many equivalent forms also may be given of an inner product which gives the solutions
of equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} the structure of a Hilbert space. A convenient
expression is obtained as follows.\footnote{I am indebted to P.~Yip for providing me
with this form of the inner product.} For two solutions
\(\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}\) and \(\psi^{A_1\cdots A_n}\) of
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} with auxiliary variables
\(\sigma_{A_1'}{}^{A_2\cdots A_n}\),
\(\rho_{A_1'}{}^{A_2\cdots A_n}\), respectively, we define the particle current
vector \(j^{AA'}(\phi,\psi)\) by
\begin{align}
  j^{AA'}={}&(-\ii)^{n-1}\bigl\{
  \overline\phi^{A'A_2'\cdots A_n'}
  \partial_{A_2'A_2}\cdots\partial_{A_n'A_n}
  \psi^{AA_2\cdots A_n}\notag\\
  &\mathrel{\phantom{(-\ii)^{n-1}\bigl\{}}+
  \overline\sigma^{AA_2'\cdots A_n'}
  \partial_{A_2'A_2}\cdots\partial_{A_n'A_n}
  \rho^{A'A_2\cdots A_n}\bigr\}.
  \tag{13.1.62}\label{eq:13.1.62}
\end{align}
It follows from equations~\eqref{eq:13.1.60} and~\eqref{eq:13.1.61} that
\(j^{AA'}\) is conserved, \(\partial_{AA'}j^{AA'}=0\). We define the inner product
of \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) by integrating the normal component of \(j^{AA'}\) over a
Cauchy surface,
\begin{equation}
  (\phi,\psi)=\int_\Sigma j^{AA'}n_{AA'}\,\dd V.
  \tag{13.1.63}\label{eq:13.1.63}
\end{equation}
Although it is not obvious from this expression, the inner product
\eqref{eq:13.1.63} is positive definite when \(n\) is odd (i.e., half-integral
\(s\)) and is positive definite for positive frequency solutions when \(n\) is even
(i.e., integral \(s\)). (This result can be proven by reexpressing our inner product
as an integral in Fourier-transform space, where it can be written in a manifestly
positive definite form; see Bargmann and Wigner 1948.) Thus in all cases, the
positive frequency solutions of equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} with finite norm in the
inner product~\eqref{eq:13.1.63} form a Hilbert space. The natural action of
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) on \(\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}\) gives rise to the
irreducible representations of class (a) characterized by \(m^2\) and \(s=n/2\).
For integral \(s\), these representations are true representations of the Poincar\'e
group, and the above construction could be reformulated using only ordinary tensor
fields. However, for half-integral \(s\), these representations of
\(\mathrm{ISL}(2,\mathbb C)\) are only representations up to sign of the Poincar\'e
group, and the use of spinorial tensor fields is essential.

When \(m=0\), the representation selected by equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} becomes
reducible except in the case \(s=0\). The irreducible representations in class (b1)
with \(s>0\) are obtained from the equation (Penrose 1965b)
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{A_1'A_1}\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}=0,
  \tag{13.1.64}\label{eq:13.1.64}
\end{equation}
where again \(\phi^{A_1\cdots A_n}\) is totally symmetric and \(n=2s\). (The
negative \(s\) representations are obtained from the complex conjugate of
equation~[13.1.64].) For \(s=\tfrac12\), the current
\begin{equation}
  j^{AA'}(\phi,\psi)=\overline\phi^{A'}\psi^A
  \tag{13.1.65}\label{eq:13.1.65}
\end{equation}
can be used to define an inner product as before. For \(s>\tfrac12\) we must
introduce potentials (Penrose 1965b), and a gauge independent expression for a
current vector cannot be given. However, a simple expression for the inner product
in momentum space can be obtained (Bargmann and Wigner 1948). Again, the unitary
representations thus obtained are true representations of the Poincar\'e group if and
only if \(s\) is integral.

We comment that in the case \(s=\tfrac12\) equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.59} written in
the form~\eqref{eq:13.1.60} and~\eqref{eq:13.1.61} is known as the \emph{Dirac
equation} and the pair of spinors \((\phi^A,\sigma_{A'})\) is called a \emph{Dirac
spinor}. By choosing a basis of spinor space and denoting the four components of
\((\phi^A,\sigma_{A'})\) as \(\psi_0,\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi_3\), respectively, the
component form of equations~\eqref{eq:13.1.60} and~\eqref{eq:13.1.61} yields the
usual form of the Dirac equation (problem~2). Similarly,
equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.64} in the case \(s=\tfrac12\) is known as the (Weyl)
\emph{neutrino equation}. Note that as claimed in section~12.4, the Dirac current
vector~\eqref{eq:13.1.62} with \(\phi^A=\psi^A\) is the sum of two future directed
null vectors and hence is a future directed timelike vector, while the neutrino
current vector~\eqref{eq:13.1.65} is future directed and null. Finally, we point
out that equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.64} in the case \(s=1\) is equivalent to Maxwell's
equations (problem~3) while in the case \(s=2\) it is equivalent to the linearized
Einstein equation (see problem~6).

In summary, we have found that spinorial tensor fields give rise to all the unitary
representations up to phase of the Poincar\'e group which are believed to have
physical relevance. This suggests that spinorial tensor fields may be the most
general type of fields in Minkowski spacetime which can arise in a
\lq\lq specially covariant\rq\rq\ quantum theory. In any case, we have completed
the primary task of motivating the introduction of spinor fields from a general and
systematic viewpoint.
