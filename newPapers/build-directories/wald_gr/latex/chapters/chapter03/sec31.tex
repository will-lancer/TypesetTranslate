\subsection{Derivative Operators and Parallel Transport}\label{sec:3.1}

A derivative operator, $\nabla$, (sometimes called a \emph{covariant derivative}) on a manifold
$M$ is a map which takes each smooth (or merely differentiable) tensor field of type
$(k,l)$ to a smooth tensor field of type $(k,l+1)$ and satisfies the five properties listed
below. In the index notation, if $T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\in\mathcal T(k,l)$, we denote the tensor field
resulting from the action of $\nabla$ on $T$ by
$\nabla_cT^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}$. It is often notationally convenient to attach an
index directly to the derivative operator and write it as $\nabla_a$, although this is to
some extent an abuse of the index notation since $\nabla_a$ is not a covector. Expressed
in the index notation, the five conditions required of a derivative operator are

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Linearity:} For all $A,B\in\mathcal T(k,l)$ and $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$,
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    \nabla_c\bigl(\alpha A^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}
    +\beta B^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\bigr)
    ={}&\alpha\nabla_c A^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\\
    &+\beta\nabla_c B^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\item \textbf{Leibniz rule:} For all $A\in\mathcal T(k,l)$, $B\in\mathcal T(k',l')$,
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    \nabla_e\bigl[A^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}
    B^{c_1\cdots c_{k'}}{}_{d_1\cdots d_{l'}}\bigr]
    ={}&\bigl[\nabla_eA^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\bigr]
    B^{c_1\cdots c_{k'}}{}_{d_1\cdots d_{l'}}\\
    &+A^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}
    \bigl[\nabla_eB^{c_1\cdots c_{k'}}{}_{d_1\cdots d_{l'}}\bigr].
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\item \textbf{Commutativity with contraction:} For all $A\in\mathcal T(k,l)$,
\begin{equation*}
  \nabla_d\bigl(A^{a_1\cdots c\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots c\cdots b_l}\bigr)
  =\nabla_d A^{a_1\cdots c\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots c\cdots b_l}.
\end{equation*}

\item \textbf{Consistency with the notion of tangent vectors as directional derivatives on scalar
fields:} For all $f\in C^\infty(M)$ and all $t^a\in T_pM$,
\begin{equation*}
  t(f)=t^a\nabla_af.
\end{equation*}

\item \textbf{Torsion free:}\footnote{If condition 5 is not imposed, it can be shown that there
must exist a tensor $T^c{}_{ab}$ antisymmetric in $a$ and $b$ such that
$\nabla_a\nabla_bf-\nabla_b\nabla_af=-T^c{}_{ab}\nabla_cf$ (see problem 1).
$T^c{}_{ab}$ is called the \emph{torsion tensor}, and thus our condition 5 states that the torsion
tensor vanishes.} For all $f\in C^\infty(M)$,
\begin{equation*}
  \nabla_a\nabla_bf=\nabla_b\nabla_af.
\end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}

The fifth condition is sometimes dropped, and indeed there are theories of gravitation where it is not imposed. However, in general relativity the derivative operator
is assumed to satisfy condition 5, and, unless otherwise stated, all derivative operators considered in this book will be assumed to be torsion free.

It is worth noting that the conditions 4 and 5 together with the Leibniz rule allow
us to derive a simple expression for the commutator of two vector fields $v^a$, $w^b$ in
terms of any derivative operator $\nabla_a$. Applied to any smooth function $f$, we have
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    [v,w](f)
    &=v\{w(f)\}-w\{v(f)\}\\
    &=v^a\nabla_a(w^b\nabla_bf)-w^a\nabla_a(v^b\nabla_bf)\\
    &=\bigl\{v^a\nabla_aw^b-w^a\nabla_av^b\bigr\}\nabla_bf.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{3.1.1}\label{eq:3.1.1}
\end{equation}
Thus we have
\begin{equation}
  [v,w]^b=v^a\nabla_aw^b-w^a\nabla_av^b.
  \tag{3.1.2}\label{eq:3.1.2}
\end{equation}

Our first important task is to show that derivative operators exist. Let $\psi$ be a
coordinate system and let $\{\partial/\partial x^\mu\}$ and $\{dx^\mu\}$ be the associated coordinate
bases. Then in the region covered by these coordinates we may define a derivative operator,
$\partial_a$, called an \emph{ordinary derivative}, as follows. For any smooth tensor field
$T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}$ we take its components
$T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_k}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_l}$ in this coordinate basis and define
$\partial_cT^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}$ to be the tensor whose components in this coordinate basis are the
partial derivatives $\partial(T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_k}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_l})/\partial x^\sigma$. All five conditions follow immediately from
the standard properties of partial derivatives. Indeed, by the equality of mixed partial
derivatives, the fifth condition holds for all tensor fields, not just scalar fields. Thus,
given a coordinate system $\psi$, we can construct an associated derivative operator $\partial_a$.
Of course, a different choice of coordinate system $\psi'$ will yield a different derivative
operator $\partial'_a$, that is, the components of the tensor
$\partial_cT^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}$ in the new (primed)
coordinates will not be equal to the partial derivatives of the primed components of
$T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}$ with respect to the primed coordinates. Thus, a given ordinary derivative
operator is coordinate dependent, i.e., it is not naturally associated with the structure
of the manifold.

How unique are derivative operators? By condition (4), any two derivative operators $\nabla_a$ and $\widetilde\nabla_a$ must agree in their action on scalar fields. To investigate their possible
disagreements on tensors of the next highest rank, let $\omega_b$ be a covector field and
consider the difference $\widetilde\nabla_a(f\omega_b)-\nabla_a(f\omega_b)$ for an arbitrary scalar field $f$. By the
Leibniz rule we have
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \widetilde\nabla_a(f\omega_b)-\nabla_a(f\omega_b)
    &=(\widetilde\nabla_af)\omega_b+f\widetilde\nabla_a\omega_b
      -(\nabla_af)\omega_b-f\nabla_a\omega_b\\
    &=f(\widetilde\nabla_a\omega_b-\nabla_a\omega_b),
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{3.1.3}\label{eq:3.1.3}
\end{equation}
where we have used property (4) again. Now at a point $p$, $\widetilde\nabla_a\omega_b$ and
$\nabla_a\omega_b$ each depend on how $\omega_b$ changes as one moves away from $p$. However,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.3} shows that their difference $\widetilde\nabla_a\omega_b-\nabla_a\omega_b$ depends only on the value of $\omega_b$ at point $p$. To see
this, we suppose that $\omega'_b$ equals $\omega_b$ at $p$ and show that we get the same answer if we
replace $\omega_b$ by $\omega'_b$. By problem 2 of chapter 2, it follows that since $\omega'_b-\omega_b$ vanishes
at $p$ we can find smooth functions, $f_{(\alpha)}$, which vanish at $p$ and smooth covector
fields, $\mu_b^{(\alpha)}$, such that
\begin{equation}
  \omega'_b-\omega_b=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}f_{(\alpha)}\mu_b^{(\alpha)}.
  \tag{3.1.4}\label{eq:3.1.4}
\end{equation}
Hence, using Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.3}, at point $p$ we have
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \widetilde\nabla_a(\omega'_b-\omega_b)-\nabla_a(\omega'_b-\omega_b)
    &=\sum_\alpha\bigl\{\widetilde\nabla_a(f_{(\alpha)}\mu_b^{(\alpha)})
      -\nabla_a(f_{(\alpha)}\mu_b^{(\alpha)})\bigr\}\\
    &=\sum_\alpha f_{(\alpha)}
      \bigl\{\widetilde\nabla_a\mu_b^{(\alpha)}-\nabla_a\mu_b^{(\alpha)}\bigr\}=0,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{3.1.5}\label{eq:3.1.5}
\end{equation}
since each $f_{(\alpha)}=0$ at $p$. Thus, we have
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde\nabla_a\omega'_b-\nabla_a\omega'_b
  =\widetilde\nabla_a\omega_b-\nabla_a\omega_b,
  \tag{3.1.6}\label{eq:3.1.6}
\end{equation}
which proves our assertion.

Thus, we have shown that $\widetilde\nabla_a-\nabla_a$ defines a map of covectors at $p$ (as opposed
to covector fields defined in a neighborhood of $p$) to tensors of type $(0,2)$ at $p$.
By property (1), this map is linear. Consequently $(\widetilde\nabla_a-\nabla_a)$ defines a tensor of type
$(1,2)$ at $p$, which we will denote as $C^c{}_{ab}$. Thus, we have shown that given any two
derivative operators $\widetilde\nabla_a$ and $\nabla_a$ there exists a tensor field $C^c{}_{ab}$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_a\omega_b=\widetilde\nabla_a\omega_b-C^c{}_{ab}\omega_c.
  \tag{3.1.7}\label{eq:3.1.7}
\end{equation}
This displays the possible disagreements of the actions of $\nabla_a$ and $\widetilde\nabla_a$ on covector
fields.

A symmetry property of $C^c{}_{ab}$ follows immediately from condition (5). If we let
$\omega_b=\nabla_bf=\widetilde\nabla_bf$, we find
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_a\nabla_bf=\widetilde\nabla_a\widetilde\nabla_bf-C^c{}_{ab}\widetilde\nabla_cf.
  \tag{3.1.8}\label{eq:3.1.8}
\end{equation}
Since both $\nabla_a\nabla_bf$ and $\widetilde\nabla_a\widetilde\nabla_bf$ are symmetric in $a$ and $b$, it follows that $C^c{}_{ab}$ must also
have this property
\begin{equation}
  C^c{}_{ab}=C^c{}_{ba}.
  \tag{3.1.9}\label{eq:3.1.9}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:3.1.9}, of course, need not hold if the torsion-free requirement is dropped.

The difference in the action of $\widetilde\nabla_a$ and $\nabla_a$ on vector fields and all higher rank tensor
fields is determined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.7}, the Leibniz rule, and property (4). For
every vector field $t^a$ and one-form field $\omega_a$, property (4) tells us that
\begin{equation}
  (\widetilde\nabla_a-\nabla_a)(\omega_bt^b)=0.
  \tag{3.1.10}\label{eq:3.1.10}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, by the Leibniz rule, we have
\begin{equation}
  (\widetilde\nabla_a-\nabla_a)(\omega_bt^b)
  =(C^c{}_{ab}\omega_c)t^b+\omega_b(\widetilde\nabla_a-\nabla_a)t^b.
  \tag{3.1.11}\label{eq:3.1.11}
\end{equation}
Thus, index substituting on contracted indices, we find
\begin{equation}
  \omega_b\bigl[(\widetilde\nabla_a-\nabla_a)t^b+C^b{}_{ac}t^c\bigr]=0
  \tag{3.1.12}\label{eq:3.1.12}
\end{equation}
for all $\omega_b$. This implies
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_at^b=\widetilde\nabla_at^b+C^b{}_{ac}t^c.
  \tag{3.1.13}\label{eq:3.1.13}
\end{equation}

Continuing in a similar manner, we can derive the general formula for the action
of $\nabla_a$ on an arbitrary tensor field in terms of $\widetilde\nabla_a$ and $C^c{}_{ab}$. For $T\in\mathcal T(k,l)$ we find
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \nabla_aT^{b_1\cdots b_k}{}_{c_1\cdots c_l}
    ={}&\widetilde\nabla_aT^{b_1\cdots b_k}{}_{c_1\cdots c_l}
    +\sum_i C^{b_i}{}_{ad}T^{b_1\cdots d\cdots b_k}{}_{c_1\cdots c_l}\\
    &-\sum_j C^d{}_{ac_j}T^{b_1\cdots b_k}{}_{c_1\cdots d\cdots c_l}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{3.1.14}\label{eq:3.1.14}
\end{equation}
Thus, the difference between the two derivative operators $\nabla_a$ and $\widetilde\nabla_a$ is completely
characterized by the tensor field $C^c{}_{ab}$. Conversely, it is not difficult to check that if
$\widetilde\nabla_a$ is a derivative operator and $C^c{}_{ab}$ is an arbitrary smooth tensor field which is
symmetric in its lower indices, then $\nabla_a$ defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.14} will also be a
derivative operator. This shows that there is a great deal of freedom involved in the
choice of a derivative operator, as on an $n$-dimensional manifold $C^c{}_{ab}$ has $n^2(n+1)/2$
independent components to be specified at each point.

The most important application of Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.14} arises from the case where $\widetilde\nabla_a$
is an ordinary derivative operator $\partial_a$. In this case, the tensor field $C^c{}_{ab}$ is denoted $\Gamma^c{}_{ab}$
and called a \emph{Christoffel symbol}. Thus, for example, we write
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_at^b=\partial_at^b+\Gamma^b{}_{ac}t^c.
  \tag{3.1.15}\label{eq:3.1.15}
\end{equation}
Since we know how to compute the ordinary derivative associated with a given
coordinate system, Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.15} (and, more generally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.14} with $\partial_a$ and
$\Gamma^b{}_{ac}$ replacing $\widetilde\nabla_a$ and $C^b{}_{ac}$) tells us how to compute the derivative $\nabla_a$ if we know
$\Gamma^b{}_{ac}$. Note that, as defined here, a Christoffel symbol is a tensor field associated with the
derivative operator $\nabla_a$ and the coordinate system used to define $\partial_a$. However, if we
change coordinates, we also change our ordinary derivative operator from $\partial_a$ to $\partial'_a$
and thus we change our tensor $\Gamma^c{}_{ab}$ to a new tensor $\Gamma'^c{}_{ab}$. Hence the coordinate
components of $\Gamma^c{}_{ab}$ in the unprimed coordinates will not be related to the components
of $\Gamma'^c{}_{ab}$ in the primed coordinates by the tensor transformation law, equation (2.3.8),
since we change tensors as well as coordinates.

Given a derivative operator $\nabla_a$ we can define the notion of the parallel transport
of a vector along a curve $C$ with a tangent $t^a$. A vector $v^a$ given at each point on the
curve is said to be \emph{parallelly transported} as one moves along the curve if the equation
\begin{equation}
  t^a\nabla_av^b=0
  \tag{3.1.16}\label{eq:3.1.16}
\end{equation}
is satisfied along the curve. More generally, one can define the parallel transport of
a tensor of arbitrary rank by
\begin{equation}
  t^a\nabla_aT^{b_1\cdots b_k}{}_{c_1\cdots c_l}=0.
  \tag{3.1.17}\label{eq:3.1.17}
\end{equation}
Choosing a coordinate system and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.15}, we can express equation~\eqref{eq:3.1.16} as
\begin{equation}
  t^a\partial_av^b+t^a\Gamma^b{}_{ac}v^c=0,
  \tag{3.1.18}\label{eq:3.1.18}
\end{equation}
or, in terms of components in the coordinate basis and the parameter $t$ along the
curve,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dv^\nu}{dt}+\sum_{\mu,\lambda}t^\mu\Gamma^\nu{}_{\mu\lambda}v^\lambda=0.
  \tag{3.1.19}\label{eq:3.1.19}
\end{equation}
This shows that the parallel transport of $v^a$ depends only on the values of $v^a$ on the
curve, so we may consider the parallel transport properties of vectors defined only
along the curve as opposed to vector fields. Furthermore, from the properties of
ordinary differential equations it follows that Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.19} always has a unique
solution for any given initial value of $v^a$. Thus, a vector at a point $p$ on the curve
uniquely defines a \lq\lq parallel transported vector\rq\rq\ everywhere else on the curve. We
may use this notion of parallel transport to identify (i.e., map into each other) the
tangent spaces $T_pM$ and $T_qM$ of points $p$ and $q$ if we are given a derivative operator and
a curve connecting $p$ and $q$. The mathematical structure arising from such a curve-dependent identification of the tangent spaces of different points is called a \emph{connection}. Conversely, one could start with the general notion of a connection and use
it to define the notion of derivative operator.

As we have seen above, given only the manifold structure, many distinct derivative operators are possible and no one of them is naturally preferred over the others.
However, we show now that if one is given a metric $g_{ab}$ on the manifold, a natural
choice of derivative operator is uniquely picked out. This is because the metric gives
rise to a natural condition which we may impose on the notion of parallel transport.
Given two vectors $v^a$ and $w^a$, we demand that their inner product $g_{ab}v^aw^b$ remain
unchanged if we parallel-transport them along any curve. Thus we require
\begin{equation}
  t^a\nabla_a(g_{bc}v^bw^c)=0
  \tag{3.1.20}\label{eq:3.1.20}
\end{equation}
for $v^b$ and $w^c$ satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.16}. Using the Leibniz rule, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  t^av^bw^c\nabla_ag_{bc}=0.
  \tag{3.1.21}\label{eq:3.1.21}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:3.1.21} will hold for all curves and parallelly transported vectors if and
only if
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_ag_{bc}=0,
  \tag{3.1.22}\label{eq:3.1.22}
\end{equation}
which is the additional condition we wish to impose on $\nabla_a$. That this equation
uniquely determines $\nabla_a$ is shown by the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:3.1.1}
Let $g_{ab}$ be a metric. Then there exists a unique derivative operator
$\nabla_a$ satisfying $\nabla_ag_{bc}=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\widetilde\nabla_a$ be any derivative operator, e.g., an ordinary derivative operator
associated with a coordinate system. We attempt to solve for $C^c{}_{ab}$ so that the
derivative operator determined by $\widetilde\nabla_a$ and $C^c{}_{ab}$ will satisfy the required property. We
will prove the theorem by showing that a unique solution for $C^c{}_{ab}$ exists.

By Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.14}, $C^c{}_{ab}$ must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  0=\nabla_ag_{bc}=\widetilde\nabla_ag_{bc}-C^d{}_{ab}g_{dc}-C^d{}_{ac}g_{bd},
  \tag{3.1.23}\label{eq:3.1.23}
\end{equation}
that is,
\begin{equation}
  C_{cab}+C_{bac}=\widetilde\nabla_ag_{bc}.
  \tag{3.1.24}\label{eq:3.1.24}
\end{equation}
By index substitution, we also have
\begin{equation}
  C_{cba}+C_{abc}=\widetilde\nabla_bg_{ac},
  \tag{3.1.25}\label{eq:3.1.25}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C_{bca}+C_{acb}=\widetilde\nabla_cg_{ab}.
  \tag{3.1.26}\label{eq:3.1.26}
\end{equation}
We add Eqs.~\eqref{eq:3.1.24} and~\eqref{eq:3.1.25} and then subtract Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.26}. Using
the symmetry property of $C^c{}_{ab}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.9}, we find
\begin{equation}
  2C_{cab}=\widetilde\nabla_ag_{bc}+\widetilde\nabla_bg_{ac}-\widetilde\nabla_cg_{ab};
  \tag{3.1.27}\label{eq:3.1.27}
\end{equation}
that is,
\begin{equation}
  C^c{}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}g^{cd}
  \bigl\{\widetilde\nabla_ag_{bd}+\widetilde\nabla_bg_{ad}-\widetilde\nabla_dg_{ab}\bigr\}.
  \tag{3.1.28}\label{eq:3.1.28}
\end{equation}
This choice of $C^c{}_{ab}$ solves Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.22} and is manifestly unique, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

Thus, a metric $g_{ab}$ naturally determines a derivative operator $\nabla_a$. For the remainder
of this book, when a metric is present we will always choose the derivative operator
to be this natural one. Furthermore, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:3.1.14} and~\eqref{eq:3.1.28} tell us how to
compute $\nabla_a$ in terms of any other derivative operator $\widetilde\nabla_a$. In particular, in terms of an
ordinary derivative operator the Christoffel symbol is
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^c{}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}g^{cd}
  \bigl\{\partial_ag_{bd}+\partial_bg_{ad}-\partial_dg_{ab}\bigr\},
  \tag{3.1.29}\label{eq:3.1.29}
\end{equation}
and thus the coordinate basis components of the Christoffel symbol are
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_\sigma g^{\rho\sigma}
  \left(\frac{\partial g_{\sigma\nu}}{\partial x^\mu}
  +\frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial x^\nu}
  -\frac{\partial g_{\mu\nu}}{\partial x^\sigma}\right).
  \tag{3.1.30}\label{eq:3.1.30}
\end{equation}
Thus, we can compute $\Gamma^c{}_{ab}$ (and thence $\nabla_a$) by taking partial derivatives of the
coordinate basis components of the metric.
