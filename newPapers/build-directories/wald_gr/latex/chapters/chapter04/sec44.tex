\subsection{Linearized Gravity: The Newtonian Limit and Gravitational
Radiation}\label{sec:4.4}

The aim of this section is to treat the approximation in which gravity is
``weak.'' In the context of general relativity this means that the spacetime metric
is nearly flat. In practice, this is an excellent approximation in nature except for
phenomena dealing with gravitational collapse and black holes and phenomena
dealing with the large scale structure of the universe.

We will systematically develop the theory of small gravitational perturbations of
an arbitrary solution in chapter 7. For the present, we simply shall assume that the
deviation, \(h_{ab}\), of the actual spacetime metric
\begin{equation}
  g_{ab}=\eta_{ab}+h_{ab}
  \tag{4.4.1}\label{eq:4.4.1}
\end{equation}
from a flat metric \(\eta_{ab}\) is ``small.'' (Since there is no natural positive
definite metric on spacetime, there is no natural norm by which ``smallness'' of
tensors can be measured. An adequate definition of ``smallness'' in this context is
that the components \(h_{\mu\nu}\) of \(h_{ab}\) be much smaller than 1 in some
global inertial coordinate system of \(\eta_{ab}\).) We mean by ``linearized
gravity'' that approximation to general relativity which is obtained by substituting
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.1} for \(g_{ab}\) in Einstein's equation and retaining only
the terms linear in \(h_{ab}\).

We denote by \(\partial_a\) the derivative operator associated with the flat metric
\(\eta_{ab}\). In order not to have \(h_{ab}\) hidden in a raised or lowered index,
it is convenient to raise and lower tensor indices with \(\eta_{ab}\) and
\(\eta^{ab}\) rather than \(g_{ab}\) and \(g^{ab}\). We will adopt this notational
convention for the remainder of this section with one exception: The tensor
\(g^{ab}\) itself will still denote the inverse metric, not
\(\eta^{ac}\eta^{bd}g_{cd}\). It should be noted that in the linear approximation
we have
\begin{equation}
  g^{ab}=\eta^{ab}-h^{ab}
  \tag{4.4.2}\label{eq:4.4.2}
\end{equation}
since the composition of the right-hand sides of equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.1} and
\eqref{eq:4.4.2} differs from the identity operator only by terms quadratic in
\(h_{ab}\).

The linearized Einstein equation can be obtained in a straightforward manner as
follows. In a global inertial coordinate system, to linear order in \(h_{ab}\) the
Christoffel symbol is
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^c{}_{ab}=\frac12\eta^{cd}
  (\partial_ah_{bd}+\partial_bh_{ad}-\partial_dh_{ab}) .
  \tag{4.4.3}\label{eq:4.4.3}
\end{equation}
To linear order in \(h_{ab}\), the Ricci tensor~\eqref{eq:3.4.5} is
\begin{align}
  R^{(1)}_{ab}
    &=\partial_c\Gamma^c{}_{ab}-\partial_a\Gamma^c{}_{cb}\notag\\
    &=\partial^c\partial_{(b}h_{a)c}
      -\frac12\partial^c\partial_ch_{ab}
      -\frac12\partial_a\partial_bh ,
  \tag{4.4.4}\label{eq:4.4.4}
\end{align}
where \(h=h^c{}_c\). Hence, the Einstein tensor to linear order is
\begin{align}
  G^{(1)}_{ab}
    &=R^{(1)}_{ab}-\frac12\eta_{ab}R^{(1)}\notag\\
    &=\partial^c\partial_{(b}h_{a)c}
      -\frac12\partial^c\partial_ch_{ab}
      -\frac12\partial_a\partial_bh
      -\frac12\eta_{ab}
        (\partial^c\partial^dh_{cd}-\partial^c\partial_ch) .
  \tag{4.4.5}\label{eq:4.4.5}
\end{align}
This expression can be simplified by defining
\begin{equation}
  \bar h_{ab}=h_{ab}-\frac12\eta_{ab}h .
  \tag{4.4.6}\label{eq:4.4.6}
\end{equation}
In terms of \(\bar h_{ab}\), the linearized Einstein equation is found to be
\begin{equation}
  G^{(1)}_{ab}=-\frac12\partial^c\partial_c\bar h_{ab}
    +\partial^c\partial_{(b}\bar h_{a)c}
    -\frac12\eta_{ab}\partial^c\partial^d\bar h_{cd}=8\pi T_{ab} .
  \tag{4.4.7}\label{eq:4.4.7}
\end{equation}

As discussed in detail in appendix C, there is a gauge freedom in general relativity
corresponding to the group of diffeomorphisms: If \(\phi:M\to M\) is a
diffeomorphism of spacetime, the metrics \(g_{ab}\) and \(\phi^*g_{ab}\)
represent the same spacetime geometry, where \(\phi^*\) is the map on tensor
fields induced by \(\phi\) (see appendix C). In the linear approximation, this
implies that two perturbations \(h_{ab}\) and \(h'_{ab}\) represent the same
physical perturbation if (and only if) they differ by the action of an
``infinitesimal diffeomorphism'' on the flat metric \(\eta_{ab}\). As discussed in
section 2.2, an ``infinitesimal diffeomorphism'' is generated by a vector field,
\(\xi^a\), and, as discussed in appendix C, the change in a tensor field induced by
such an infinitesimal diffeomorphism defines the Lie derivative. This means that
\(h_{ab}\) and \(h_{ab}+\mathcal L_\xi\eta_{ab}\) describe the same physical
perturbation. From appendix C, we see that we can express
\(\mathcal L_\xi\eta_{ab}\) in terms of the flat derivative operator
\(\partial_a\) as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_\xi\eta_{ab}=\partial_a\xi_b+\partial_b\xi_a .
  \tag{4.4.8}\label{eq:4.4.8}
\end{equation}
This means that linearized gravity has a gauge freedom given by
\begin{equation}
  h_{ab}\longrightarrow h_{ab}+\partial_a\xi_b+\partial_b\xi_a
  \tag{4.4.9}\label{eq:4.4.9}
\end{equation}
which is closely analogous to the electromagnetic gauge freedom
\(A_a\longrightarrow A_a+\partial_a\chi\). This gauge freedom of \(h_{ab}\)
also can be derived without employing the machinery of appendix C from the tensor
transformation law~\eqref{eq:2.3.8}. According to equation~\eqref{eq:2.3.8}, the
components of \(h_{ab}\) and
\(h_{ab}+\partial_a\xi_b+\partial_b\xi_a\) differ, to first order, merely by a
coordinate transformation and, hence, represent the same physical perturbation.

We may use this gauge freedom to simplify the linearized Einstein equation. By
solving the equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial^b\partial_b\xi_a=-\partial^b\bar h_{ab}
  \tag{4.4.10}\label{eq:4.4.10}
\end{equation}
for \(\xi^a\), we can make a gauge transformation, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.9},
to obtain
\begin{equation}
  \partial^b\bar h_{ab}=0 ,
  \tag{4.4.11}\label{eq:4.4.11}
\end{equation}
which is the de Donder gauge condition. In this gauge, the linearized Einstein
equation simplifies to become
\begin{equation}
  \partial^c\partial_c\bar h_{ab}=-16\pi T_{ab}
  \tag{4.4.12}\label{eq:4.4.12}
\end{equation}
and is closely analogous to Maxwell's equation~\eqref{eq:4.2.32}.

In vacuum (\(T_{ab}=0\)) equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.11} and \eqref{eq:4.4.12}
are precisely the equations written down by Fierz and Pauli (1939) to describe a
massless spin-2 field propagating in flat spacetime (see chapter 13). Thus, in the
linear approximation, general relativity reduces to the theory of a massless spin-2
field. The full theory of general relativity thus may be viewed as that of a massless
spin-2 field which undergoes a nonlinear self-interaction. It should be noted,
however, that the notion of the mass and spin of a field require the presence of a
flat background metric \(\eta_{ab}\) which one has in the linear approximation but
not in the full theory, so the statement that, in general relativity, gravity is treated
as a massless spin-2 field is not one that can be given precise meaning outside the
context of the linear approximation.

\subsubsection*{The Newtonian Limit}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{The Newtonian Limit}

The theory of general relativity may have great aesthetic appeal, but this does not
mean that its predictions are in accord with nature. We know that the Newtonian
theory of gravitation gives excellent predictions under a wide range of conditions.
Thus, the first crucial test of general relativity is that its predictions reduce to those
of Newtonian gravity under the circumstances when Newtonian theory is known to
be valid---specifically, when gravity is weak, the relative motion of the sources is
much slower than the speed of light \(c\), and the material stresses are much smaller
than the mass-energy density (in units where \(c=1\)).

When gravity is weak, the linear approximation to general relativity should be
valid. The assumptions about the sources then can be reformulated more precisely
as follows: There exists a global inertial coordinate system of \(\eta_{ab}\) such
that
\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}\approx\rho t_at_b ,
  \tag{4.4.13}\label{eq:4.4.13}
\end{equation}
where \(t^a=(\partial/\partial x^0)^a\) is the ``time direction'' of this coordinate
system. (Equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.13} asserts that \(T_{ab}\) has only a
``time-time'' component; the neglect of the ``time-space'' components is essentially
the statement that velocities [and thus, momentum densities] are small while the
neglect of the ``space-space'' components is the statement that the stresses are
small.) Since the sources are ``slowly varying,'' we expect the spacetime geometry
to change slowly as well, and thus we seek solutions of equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.12}
where the time derivatives of \(\bar h_{ab}\) are negligible.

With these assumptions, the components of equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.12} in our
global inertial coordinate system become
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\nabla^2\bar h_{\mu\nu}=0
  \tag{4.4.14}\label{eq:4.4.14}
\end{equation}
for all \(\mu,\nu\) except \(\mu=\nu=0\), while
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\nabla^2\bar h_{00}=-16\pi\rho ,
  \tag{4.4.15}\label{eq:4.4.15}
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol\nabla^2\) denotes the usual Laplace operator of space. The
unique solution of equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.14} which is well behaved at infinity is
\(\bar h_{\mu\nu}=0\). (The solutions \(\bar h_{\mu\nu}=\text{constant}\) are
also permissible, but they can be eliminated by a further gauge transformation.)
Thus, in the Newtonian limit our solution for the perturbed metric \(h_{ab}\) is
\begin{equation}
  h_{ab}=\bar h_{ab}-\frac12\eta_{ab}\bar h
    =-(4t_at_b+2\eta_{ab})\phi ,
  \tag{4.4.16}\label{eq:4.4.16}
\end{equation}
where \(\phi=-\tfrac14\bar h_{00}\) satisfies Poisson's equation,
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\nabla^2\phi=4\pi\rho .
  \tag{4.4.17}\label{eq:4.4.17}
\end{equation}
The motion of test bodies in this curved spacetime geometry is governed by the
geodesic equation,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2x^\mu}{\dd\tau^2}
    +\sum_{\rho,\sigma}\Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\sigma}
      \left(\frac{\dd x^\rho}{\dd\tau}\right)
      \left(\frac{\dd x^\sigma}{\dd\tau}\right)=0 ,
  \tag{4.4.18}\label{eq:4.4.18}
\end{equation}
where \(x^\mu(\tau)\) is the world line of the particle in global inertial
coordinates. For motion much slower than the speed of light, we may approximate
\(\dd x^\mu/\dd\tau\) as \((1,0,0,0)\) in the second term, and the proper time
\(\tau\) may be approximated by the coordinate time \(t\). Thus, we find
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2x^\mu}{\dd t^2}=-\Gamma^\mu{}_{00} .
  \tag{4.4.19}\label{eq:4.4.19}
\end{equation}
From our solution, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.16}, we have, for \(\mu=1,2,3\):
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^\mu{}_{00}=-\frac12\frac{\partial h_{00}}{\partial x^\mu}
    =\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^\mu} ,
  \tag{4.4.20}\label{eq:4.4.20}
\end{equation}
where, again, time derivatives of \(\phi\) have been neglected. Thus, the motion of
test bodies is governed by the equation,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi ,
  \tag{4.4.21}\label{eq:4.4.21}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{a}=\dd^2\mathbf{x}/\dd t^2\) is the acceleration of the body relative to
global inertial coordinates of \(\eta_{ab}\).

Equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.17} and \eqref{eq:4.4.21} are, of course, the basic
equations of Newtonian gravity, and thus general relativity does indeed reduce to
Newtonian gravity in the appropriate limit. Note, however, that although the
predictions of general relativity agree with those of Newtonian gravity, the
underlying viewpoint is radically different. In the Newtonian viewpoint, the Sun
creates a gravitational field that exerts a force upon the Earth, which, in turn,
causes it to orbit the Sun rather than move in a straight line. In the general
relativistic viewpoint, the mass-energy of the Sun produces a curvature of the
spacetime geometry. The Earth is in free motion (no forces act upon it) and it
travels on a geodesic of the spacetime metric; but because spacetime is curved, it
orbits the Sun. From the Newtonian viewpoint, the Earth undergoes acceleration;
from the general relativistic viewpoint, it is the inertial observers of the flat metric,
\(\eta_{ab}\), who must accelerate.

It is instructive to examine the predictions of linearized gravity when the lowest
order effects of the motion of the sources are taken into account. If we continue to
neglect stresses, the stress-energy tensor is approximated to linear order in velocity
by
\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}=2t_{(a}J_{b)}-\rho t_at_b ,
  \tag{4.4.22}\label{eq:4.4.22}
\end{equation}
where \(J_b=-T_{ab}t^a\) is the mass-energy current density 4-vector. The
linearized Einstein equation again predicts that the space-space components of
\(\bar h_{ab}\) satisfy the source-free wave equation, but the space-time and
time-time components now satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a\partial_a\bar h_{0\mu}=16\pi J_\mu .
  \tag{4.4.23}\label{eq:4.4.23}
\end{equation}
Thus, \(A_a=-\tfrac14\bar h_{ab}t^b\) satisfies precisely Maxwell's equations in
the Lorenz gauge with source \(J_a\). If again we assume that the time derivatives
of \(\bar h_{ab}\) are negligible, then the space-space components of
\(\bar h_{ab}\) vanish, and we find that to linear order in the velocity of the test
body, the geodesic equation now yields (problem 3)
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}=-\mathbf{E}-4\mathbf{v}\mathbin{\times}\mathbf{B} ,
  \tag{4.4.24}\label{eq:4.4.24}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are defined in terms of \(A_a\) by the same
formulas as in electromagnetism. This is identical to the Lorentz force equation of
electromagnetism (with \(q=m\)) except for an overall minus sign and a factor of 4
in the ``magnetic force'' term. Thus, linearized gravity predicts that the motion of
masses produces magnetic gravitational effects very similar to those of
electromagnetism.

One final, somewhat troublesome point deserves further comment. Above, we
showed that general relativity reduces to Newtonian gravity in an appropriate limit,
but, strictly speaking, we went beyond the linear approximation to show this. The
reason has to do with our use of the geodesic equation to get the motion of a test
body. As mentioned at the end of section 4.3, the ``geodesic hypothesis'' follows as
a consequence of the condition \(\nabla^aT_{ab}=0\) which, in turn, follows as a
consequence of Einstein's equation. However, in the linear approximation,
Einstein's equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.7} or \eqref{eq:4.4.12} actually implies the
condition \(\partial^aT_{ab}=0\). (This is reasonable since in the linear
approximation, \(T_{ab}\) is already ``small,'' so deviations of the derivative
operator from the flat derivative operator \(\partial_a\) contribute only to higher
order.) But the condition \(\partial^aT_{ab}=0\) implies that test bodies move on
geodesics of the \emph{flat} metric \(\eta_{ab}\); i.e., if one stays consistently
within the linear approximation, one predicts that test bodies are unaffected by
gravity. Thus, in obtaining equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.21} we actually have gone beyond
the linear approximation. This, of course, does not invalidate our discussion.
However, it illustrates the difficulties which occur when one tries to derive the
equations of motion of bodies from Einstein's equation via a perturbation expansion
in the departure from flatness. In order to obtain a good approximation to a
solution to a given order, one must use some aspects of the higher order equations.

\subsubsection*{Gravitational Radiation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{Gravitational Radiation}

One of the most important changes which occurs when one goes from Coulomb's
theory of electrostatics to Maxwell's theory of electromagnetism is that the
electromagnetic field becomes a dynamical entity. Electromagnetic radiation can
propagate freely through spacetime. A similar change occurs when one goes from
Newtonian gravitation to general relativity: Gravitational radiation exists; i.e.,
ripples in the curvature of spacetime can propagate through spacetime. In the
linear approximation
the propagation of gravitational radiation is governed by the source-free,
linearized Einstein equation (see equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.11} and
\eqref{eq:4.4.12} above),
\begin{align}
  \partial^a\bar h_{ab}&=0 ,
    \tag{4.4.25}\label{eq:4.4.25}\\
  \partial^c\partial_c\bar h_{ab}&=0 .
    \tag{4.4.26}\label{eq:4.4.26}
\end{align}
In obtaining these equations at the beginning of this section, the gauge
choice~\eqref{eq:4.4.25} was made. However, there remains the freedom to make
further gauge transformations
\(h_{ab}\longrightarrow h_{ab}+\partial_a\xi_b+\partial_b\xi_a\) provided that
\begin{equation}
  \partial^b\partial_b\xi^a=0 ,
  \tag{4.4.27}\label{eq:4.4.27}
\end{equation}
as such transformations leave equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.25} unchanged. This is
closely analogous to the fact that in electromagnetism the Lorenz gauge condition
does not uniquely fix the vector potential \(A_a\); we have the restricted gauge
freedom \(A_a\longrightarrow A_a+\partial_a\chi\) with
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a\partial_a\chi=0 .
  \tag{4.4.28}\label{eq:4.4.28}
\end{equation}
When treating electromagnetic radiation it is convenient to use the remaining gauge
freedom to set the component \(A_0\) in some global inertial coordinate system
equal to zero in a source-free region (\(j_a=0\)). This gauge condition, called the
\emph{Coulomb} or \emph{radiation gauge}, can be achieved as follows. On a
constant time surface \(t=t_0\) of our global inertial coordinate system we solve
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\chi=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{A} .
  \tag{4.4.29}\label{eq:4.4.29}
\end{equation}
We define \(\chi\) throughout spacetime to be the solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.28} whose initial value on the \(t=t_0\) surface is given by
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.29}, and whose initial time derivative is
\(\partial\chi/\partial t=-A_0\). (That a unique solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.28} exists for arbitrarily specified initial values of
\(\chi\) and \(\partial\chi/\partial t\) follows from the results of section 10.1
below.) Then the function \(f\) defined by
\begin{equation}
  f=A_0+\partial\chi/\partial t
  \tag{4.4.30}\label{eq:4.4.30}
\end{equation}
will satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a\partial_af=-4\pi j_0
  \tag{4.4.31}\label{eq:4.4.31}
\end{equation}
by equations~\eqref{eq:4.2.32} and \eqref{eq:4.4.28}. Furthermore, on the initial
surface \(t=t_0\) we have
\begin{align}
  f&=0 ,
    \tag{4.4.32}\label{eq:4.4.32}\\
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
    &=\frac{\partial A_0}{\partial t}
      +\frac{\partial^2\chi}{\partial t^2}
      =\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{A}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\chi=0 .
    \tag{4.4.33}\label{eq:4.4.33}
\end{align}
Hence, if no sources are present in the region under consideration (or, more
precisely, if for each point \(p\) we have \(j_0=0\) on the light cone of \(p\)
between it and the \(t=t_0\) surface), the unique solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.31} with initial data~\eqref{eq:4.4.32} and
\eqref{eq:4.4.33} is \(f=0\), and the gauge transformation
\(A_a\longrightarrow A_a+\partial_a\chi\) achieves the desired condition
\(A_0=0\) while maintaining the Lorenz gauge condition.

In a very similar manner, in the case of linearized gravity we can use the restricted
gauge freedom, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.27}, to achieve the radiation gauge
\(h=0\), \(h_{0\mu}=0\) for \(\mu=1,2,3\) in a source-free region
(\(T_{ab}=0\)). As an extra bonus, we also obtain \(h_{00}=0\) if no sources are
present throughout spacetime (i.e., not just in our region) and good behavior at
infinity is assumed. To achieve the radiation gauge, we solve on the initial surface
\(t=t_0\) the equations
\begin{align}
  2\left(-\frac{\partial\xi_0}{\partial t}
    +\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)&=-h ,
    \tag{4.4.34a}\label{eq:4.4.34a}\\
  2\left[-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\xi_0
    +\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}
      \left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\partial t}\right)\right]
    &=-\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} ,
    \tag{4.4.34b}\label{eq:4.4.34b}\\
  \frac{\partial\xi_\mu}{\partial t}
    +\frac{\partial\xi_0}{\partial x^\mu}&=-h_{0\mu}
    \quad(\mu=1,2,3) ,
    \tag{4.4.34c}\label{eq:4.4.34c}\\
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\xi_\mu
    +\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
      \left(\frac{\partial\xi_0}{\partial t}\right)
    &=-\frac{\partial h_{0\mu}}{\partial t}
    \quad(\mu=1,2,3) ,
    \tag{4.4.34d}\label{eq:4.4.34d}
\end{align}
to obtain the initial values of \(\xi_0,\xi_1,\xi_2,\xi_3\), and their first time
derivatives. Then we define \(\xi^a\) to be the solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.27} with these initial data. By the same argument as used in
the electromagnetic case, the gauge transformation generated by \(\xi^a\) will
achieve \(h=0\) and \(h_{0\mu}=0\) (\(\mu=1,2,3\)) in a source-free region while
preserving the gauge condition, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.25}.

Our bonus, \(h_{00}=0\), comes about as follows. Since \(h=0\), we have
\(h_{ab}=\bar h_{ab}\). Since \(h_{0\mu}=0\) for \(\mu=1,2,3\), the gauge
condition, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.25}, yields
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial h_{00}}{\partial t}=0 .
  \tag{4.4.35}\label{eq:4.4.35}
\end{equation}
The linearized Einstein equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.12} then yields
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2h_{00}=-16\pi T_{00} .
  \tag{4.4.36}\label{eq:4.4.36}
\end{equation}
But if \(T_{00}=0\) throughout the spacetime, the only solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.36} which is well behaved at infinity is
\(h_{00}=\text{constant}\). A further gauge transformation then achieves
\(h_{00}=0\) without violating any of the previous conditions.

We employ this radiation gauge to seek solutions of the source-free linearized
Einstein equation. Plane waves,
\begin{equation}
  h_{ab}=H_{ab}\exp\left(\ii\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}k_\mu x^\mu\right) ,
  \tag{4.4.37}\label{eq:4.4.37}
\end{equation}
where \(H_{ab}\) is a constant tensor field, will satisfy
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.26} if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\mu k^\mu k_\mu
    =\sum_{\mu,\nu}\eta^{\mu\nu}k_\mu k_\nu=0 .
  \tag{4.4.38}\label{eq:4.4.38}
\end{equation}
The radiation gauge conditions require (for \(\nu=0,1,2,3\))
\begin{align}
  \sum_{\mu=0}^{3}k^\mu H_{\mu\nu}&=0 ,
    \tag{4.4.39a}\label{eq:4.4.39a}\\
  H_{0\nu}&=0 ,
    \tag{4.4.39b}\label{eq:4.4.39b}\\
  \sum_{\mu=0}^{3}H^\mu{}_\mu&=0 .
    \tag{4.4.39c}\label{eq:4.4.39c}
\end{align}
Since equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.39a} and \eqref{eq:4.4.39b} both imply
\(\sum_\nu H_{0\nu}k^\nu=0\), only eight of these nine equations are
independent. Since there are 10 independent components, \(H_{\mu\nu}\), this
leaves two linearly independent solutions for \(H_{ab}\). These two solutions
describe the two independent polarization states of plane gravitational waves. An
arbitrary well behaved solution of the vacuum linearized Einstein equation, i.e., an
arbitrary packet of gravitational radiation, can be expressed as a superposition of
these plane wave solutions.

How could we detect the presence of gravitational radiation? The most
straightforward way is to study the relative acceleration of two masses, i.e., to
measure the gravitational tidal force. For two nearby freely falling bodies, this
acceleration is governed by the geodesic deviation equation~\eqref{eq:3.3.18}. In
our case, if the two bodies are nearly ``at rest'' in a global inertial coordinate system
of \(\eta_{ab}\), we have
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2X^\mu}{\dd t^2}\approx
    \sum_\nu R_{\nu00}{}^\mu X^\nu ,
  \tag{4.4.40}\label{eq:4.4.40}
\end{equation}
where \(X^a\) is the deviation vector. In the radiation gauge (assuming
\(h_{00}=0\)), we obtain from equation~\eqref{eq:3.4.4} a very simple expression
for the relevant components of the linearized Riemann tensor,
\begin{equation}
  R_{\nu00\mu}=\frac12\frac{\partial^2h_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t^2}
  \qquad\text{(radiation gauge)} .
  \tag{4.4.41}\label{eq:4.4.41}
\end{equation}
(Incidentally, this formula shows that the plane wave solutions we obtained above
are physically meaningful---that is, that they cannot be eliminated by further gauge
transformations---since they produce nonzero curvature.) Thus, in principle, one
could detect gravitational radiation by accurately tracking (say, with laser beams)
the separation of two masses suspended freely from supports; such a detection
scheme may become practical in the near future. Alternatively, if the masses are not
in free motion but are connected by a solid piece of material, the gravitational tidal
forces will stress the material. A solid bar of matter would be set into oscillation by
these periodic stresses, and this oscillation could be detectable if the frequency of
the gravitational radiation is near the resonant frequency of the bar. This scheme
for detecting gravitational radiation was pioneered by Joseph Weber and is being
pursued by a number of research groups. (The details of this and other schemes for
detecting gravitational waves are reviewed by Douglass and Braginsky 1979.) The
extreme sensitivity required for the detection of gravitational radiation should be
stressed. For physically reasonable astrophysical sources of gravitational waves,
one does not expect the radiation gauge components \(h_{\mu\nu}\) in the relevant
frequency range to have a magnitude greater than about \(10^{-17}\) (see, e.g.,
Thorne 1978). According to equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.40} and \eqref{eq:4.4.41}, this
means that the fractional relative displacement \(\Delta X/X\) of the two free
masses should not exceed \(10^{-17}\), i.e., free masses separated by 1 meter will
be displaced by no more than about 1/100 of a nuclear diameter by a gravitational
wave! The stresses on a solid bar are correspondingly small. Nevertheless, many
researchers believe that an unambiguous detection of gravitational radiation will be
made in the near future.

How are gravitational waves produced? The most likely sources of (relatively)
strong bursts of gravitational radiation arise from collapse phenomena (see
chapter 12) where gravity is not weak and the linear approximation cannot be used.
In such cases, we must solve the full nonlinear Einstein equation, and because of the
difficulty of this task, our knowledge of these processes is rudimentary. It is
instructive, therefore, to study the radiation generation problem in the linear
approximation where the complete solution is readily obtained. Since each
component of \(\bar h_{ab}\) satisfies the ordinary, inhomogeneous scalar wave
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.12}, the solution is given in terms of the source by the same
retarded Green's function as used for a scalar field and in electromagnetism, namely,
\begin{equation}
  \bar h_{\mu\nu}(x)=4\int_\Lambda
    \frac{T_{\mu\nu}(x')}{\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert}\,\dd S(x') ,
  \tag{4.4.42}\label{eq:4.4.42}
\end{equation}
where \(\Lambda\) denotes the past light cone of the point \(x\) and the volume
element on the light cone is \(\dd S=r^2\dd r\,\dd\Omega\). The gauge
condition~\eqref{eq:4.4.11} on \(\bar h_{ab}\) will be satisfied by virtue of the
linearized conservation equation \(\partial^aT_{ab}=0\), so
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.42} gives the solution for the gravitational effects produced
by sources in the linear approximation. (The radiation gauge conditions, of course,
are not imposed since sources are present.)

It is interesting to evaluate our solution in the slow motion limit where the typical
source velocities are much smaller than the speed of light. (More precisely, we
consider the limit where the spatial extent of the source is much smaller than the
typical wavelengths of the emitted radiation.) In electromagnetism this limit is
known as the dipole approximation, since in that case the dominant radiation is
generated by the changing dipole moment of the source.

To analyze this limit in the gravitational case, we Fourier transform all quantities
in the time variable, \(t\), of our global inertial coordinate system of \(\eta_{ab}\),
leaving the space variables untransformed. We define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\bar h}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\mathbf{x})
    =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
      \bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})e^{\ii\omega t}\,\dd t ,
  \tag{4.4.43}\label{eq:4.4.43}
\end{equation}
and we similarly Fourier transform \(T_{\mu\nu}\). From
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.42} it follows that
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\bar h}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\mathbf{x})=4\int
    \frac{\widehat T_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\mathbf{x}')}
      {\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert}
    \exp\!\left(\ii\omega\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert\right)\dd^3x' ,
  \tag{4.4.44}\label{eq:4.4.44}
\end{equation}
where the ``extra'' factor of
\(\exp(\ii\omega\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert)\) arises from the fact that the
original integral~\eqref{eq:4.4.42} was over the past light cone. We need only
solve for the space-space components of \(\widehat{\bar h}_{\mu\nu}\) since the
components \(\widehat{\bar h}_{0\mu}\) are readily obtained in terms of them from
the gauge condition~\eqref{eq:4.4.11} which yields
\begin{equation}
  -\ii\omega\widehat{\bar h}_{0\mu}
    =\sum_{\nu=1}^{3}\frac{\partial\widehat{\bar h}_{\nu\mu}}
      {\partial x^\nu} .
  \tag{4.4.45}\label{eq:4.4.45}
\end{equation}
Since we are interested in calculating the radiation, it suffices to obtain our
solution in the ``far zone,'' \(R\gg1/\omega\), where \(R\) denotes the distance
from the source. For the limit in which we are interested, the frequencies of interest
are sufficiently small that the factor
\(\exp(\ii\omega\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert)\) varies negligibly over the source, so
we may replace
\(\exp(\ii\omega\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert)/\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rvert\) by
\(\exp(\ii\omega R)/R\) and pull it out of the integral. We evaluate the remaining
integral of the space-space components of \(\widehat T_{ab}\) as follows:
\begin{align}
  \int\widehat T^{\mu\nu}\dd^3x
    &=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\left\{
      \int\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\alpha}
        (\widehat T^{\alpha\nu}x^\mu)\dd^3x
      -\int\frac{\partial\widehat T^{\alpha\nu}}{\partial x^\alpha}
        x^\mu\dd^3x\right\}\notag\\
    &=-\ii\omega\int\widehat T^{0\nu}x^\mu\dd^3x\notag\\
    &=-\frac{\ii\omega}{2}\int
      (\widehat T^{0\nu}x^\mu+\widehat T^{0\mu}x^\nu)\dd^3x\notag\\
    &=-\frac{\ii\omega}{2}\sum_{\beta=1}^{3}\left\{
      \int\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\beta}
        (\widehat T^{0\beta}x^\mu x^\nu)\dd^3x
      -\int\frac{\partial\widehat T^{0\beta}}{\partial x^\beta}
        x^\mu x^\nu\dd^3x\right\}\notag\\
    &=-\frac{\omega^2}{2}\int\widehat T^{00}x^\mu x^\nu\dd^3x ,
  \tag{4.4.46}\label{eq:4.4.46}
\end{align}
where in the second and fifth lines we used Gauss's law to get rid of the total
divergence and also used the conservation of \(T_{ab}\) to express the divergence
of spatial components in terms of the time derivative of time components. Thus, we
obtain our far-zone solution,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\bar h}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\mathbf{x})
    =-\frac{2\omega^2}{3}\frac{e^{\ii\omega R}}{R}\widehat q_{\mu\nu}(\omega)
    \qquad(\mu,\nu=1,2,3) ,
  \tag{4.4.47}\label{eq:4.4.47}
\end{equation}
where \(\widehat q_{\mu\nu}\) is the Fourier transform of the quadrupole moment
tensor,
\begin{equation}
  q_{\mu\nu}=3\int T^{00}x^\mu x^\nu\dd^3x .
  \tag{4.4.48}\label{eq:4.4.48}
\end{equation}
The inverse Fourier transform of equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.47} yields
\begin{equation}
  \bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})
    =\left.\frac{2}{3R}\frac{\dd^2q_{\mu\nu}}{\dd t^2}\right|_{\mathrm{ret}}
    \qquad(\mu,\nu=1,2,3) ,
  \tag{4.4.49}\label{eq:4.4.49}
\end{equation}
where the derivative is evaluated at the retarded time \(t'=t-R\). Thus, the
dominant gravitational radiation in the slow motion approximation arises from the
time rate of change of the quadrupole moment of the source. The absence of dipole
radiation can be understood, physically, as resulting from conservation of
momentum, which does not permit a time-varying mass dipole moment. On account
of the absence of dipole radiation, the emission of gravitational radiation in the
slow motion limit is smaller than the radiation in comparable situations in
electromagnetism.

The issue of energy in general relativity is a rather delicate one. In general
relativity there is no known meaningful notion of local energy density of the
gravitational field. The basic reason for this is closely related to the fact that the
spacetime metric, \(g_{ab}\), describes both the background spacetime structure and
the dynamical aspects of the gravitational field, but no natural way is known to
decompose it into its ``background'' and ``dynamical'' parts. Since one would
expect to attribute energy to the dynamical aspect of gravity but not to the
background spacetime structure, it seems unlikely that a notion of local energy
density could be obtained without a corresponding decomposition of the spacetime
metric. However, for an isolated system, the \emph{total} energy can be defined by
examining the gravitational field at large distances from the system. In addition,
for an isolated system the flux of energy carried away from the system by
gravitational radiation also is well defined.

We shall postpone a full discussion of energy in general relativity until chapter 11.
However, for small deviations from flat spacetime, we would expect that---in
analogy with the scalar and electromagnetic field (see equations~\eqref{eq:4.2.20}
and \eqref{eq:4.2.27})---the total energy and energy flux of the gravitational field
will be quadratic in the field \(h_{ab}\). A formula for this energy and energy flux
is suggested by the following considerations. The linearized vacuum Einstein
equation
\begin{equation}
  G^{(1)}_{ab}[h_{cd}]=0
  \tag{4.4.50}\label{eq:4.4.50}
\end{equation}
states that the Einstein tensor for the metric \(\eta_{ab}+h_{ab}\) vanishes to first
order in \(h_{ab}\). However, to second order in \(h_{ab}\), the vacuum Einstein
equation will, in general, fail to be satisfied. Indeed, the terms in the Ricci tensor
quadratic in \(h_{ab}\) are (problem 4),
\begin{align}
  R^{(2)}_{ab}
    &=\frac12h^{cd}\partial_a\partial_bh_{cd}
      -h^{cd}\partial_c\partial_{(a}h_{b)\dd}\notag\\
    &\quad+\frac14(\partial_ah_{cd})\partial_bh^{cd}
      +(\partial^dh^c{}_b)\partial_{[d}h_{c]a}\notag\\
    &\quad+\frac12\partial_d(h^{\dd c}\partial_ch_{ab})
      -\frac14(\partial^ch)\partial_ch_{ab}\notag\\
    &\quad-\left(\partial_dh^{cd}-\frac12\partial^ch\right)
      \partial_{(a}h_{b)c} .
  \tag{4.4.51}\label{eq:4.4.51}
\end{align}
Thus, in order to maintain a solution of the vacuum Einstein equation to second
order, we must correct \(h_{ab}\) by adding to it the term \(h^{(2)}_{ab}\), where
\(h^{(2)}_{ab}\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
  G^{(1)}_{ab}[h^{(2)}_{cd}]+G^{(2)}_{ab}[h_{cd}]=0 ,
  \tag{4.4.52}\label{eq:4.4.52}
\end{equation}
where (in the case \(R^{(1)}_{ab}=0\)) we have
\(G^{(2)}_{ab}=R^{(2)}_{ab}-\tfrac12\eta_{ab}R^{(2)}\). We may write
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.52} in the form
\begin{equation}
  G^{(1)}_{ab}[h^{(2)}_{cd}]=8\pi t_{ab} ,
  \tag{4.4.53}\label{eq:4.4.53}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  t_{ab}=-\frac{1}{8\pi}G^{(2)}_{ab}[h_{cd}] .
  \tag{4.4.54}\label{eq:4.4.54}
\end{equation}

Thus, in second order, \(h_{ab}\) causes the same correction to the spacetime metric
as would be produced by ordinary matter with stress-energy tensor \(t_{ab}\).
Furthermore, \(t_{ab}\) is symmetric and is conserved, \(\partial^at_{ab}=0\),
assuming, of course, that \(h_{ab}\) satisfies the vacuum linearized Einstein
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.50}. This suggests that we should view \(t_{ab}\) as the
effective stress-energy tensor of the gravitational field, valid to second order in
deviation from flatness. However, this interpretation cannot be taken too literally.
First, the local construction of \(t_{ab}\) quadratically from \(h_{ab}\) as well as
its symmetry and conservation properties will not be affected if we add to it a tensor
of the form \(\partial^c\partial^dU_{acbd}\), where \(U_{acbd}\) is locally
constructed from \(h_{ab}\), is quadratic in \(h_{ab}\), and satisfies the tensor
symmetries \(U_{acbd}=U_{[ac]bd}=U_{ac[bd]}=U_{bdac}\). (Indeed, the terms
quadratic in \(h_{ab}\) of the Landau--Lifshitz ``pseudotensor'' [Landau and
Lifshitz 1962] differ from our expression for \(t_{ab}\) by such a term.)
Furthermore, \(t_{ab}\) is not even gauge invariant; i.e., if we replace \(h_{ab}\)
by \(h_{ab}+2\partial_{(a}\xi_{b)}\), then \(t_{ab}\) does not remain unchanged.
This reflects the above mentioned fact that there is no meaningful notion of the
local stress-energy of the gravitational field in general relativity. However, the
\emph{total} energy associated with \(h_{ab}\),
\begin{equation}
  E=\int_\Sigma t_{00}\dd^3x
  \tag{4.4.55}\label{eq:4.4.55}
\end{equation}
(where the integral is taken over the spacelike hyperplane \(\Sigma\) depicted in
figure~\ref{fig:4.2}) is gauge invariant in the following sense: Suppose the
perturbed spacetime metric
\input{figures/chapter04/fig04-02.tex}
\(\eta_{ab}+h_{ab}\) is asymptotically flat in the sense that the inertial components
of \(h_{ab}\) and its derivatives go to zero as \(r\to\infty\) as:
\(h_{\mu\nu}=O(1/r)\), \(\partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}=O(1/r^2)\), and
\(\partial_\sigma\partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}=O(1/r^3)\). (Note that these conditions
ensure the convergence of the integral~\eqref{eq:4.4.55} which defines \(E\).)
Then for any gauge transformation, \(\xi^a\), which preserves these asymptotic
conditions, the value of \(E\) is unchanged,
\(E[h_{ab}]=E[h_{ab}+2\partial_{(a}\xi_{b)}]\). Rather lengthy calculations are
needed to demonstrate this gauge invariance of \(E\) directly, but a simple proof of
restricted gauge invariance is outlined in problem 7. Furthermore, it is not difficult
to verify that \(E\) is unchanged if a term \(\partial^c\partial^dU_{acbd}\) with
the above properties is added to \(t_{ab}\), since the volume integral of this term
can be converted by Gauss's law to a surface integral which vanishes on account of
the asymptotic conditions.

Similarly, although the local flux of energy \(-t^a{}_0\) is not gauge invariant, if
the spacetime is initially time independent, goes through a time-dependent phase,
and becomes time independent again, then the total radiated energy
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E=-\int_S t_{a0}\dd S^a
  \tag{4.4.56}\label{eq:4.4.56}
\end{equation}
is gauge independent, where the integral is taken over the three-dimensional
timelike surface \(S\) depicted in figure~\ref{fig:4.3}. Here the limit as
\(r\to\infty\) for that surface is understood, and the above conditions of
asymptotic flatness on \(h_{ab}\) are imposed as \(r\to\infty\) along the
asymptotically null surfaces \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2\) in the stationary
regimes. [Note that in the time-dependent regime these conditions would not be
appropriate since according to our solution~\eqref{eq:4.4.42} we expect
\(\partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}=O(1/r)\). In chapter 11 we will make precise the taking
of limits as \(r\to\infty\) along null surfaces by introducing the notion of null
infinity.]

Using equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.54} and equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.56}, we can calculate
the energy carried away by gravitational radiation for our
solution~\eqref{eq:4.4.49} corresponding to the metric perturbation produced by a
slowly varying source. A lengthy calculation (where many terms which integrate to
zero are dropped) yields the final result,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E=\int P\,\dd t ,
  \tag{4.4.57}\label{eq:4.4.57}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  P=\left.\frac{1}{45}\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{3}
    \left(\frac{\dd^3Q_{\mu\nu}}{\dd t^3}\right)^2\right|_{\mathrm{ret}} ,
  \tag{4.4.58}\label{eq:4.4.58}
\end{equation}
and \(Q_{\mu\nu}\) is the trace-free quadrupole moment tensor
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\mu\nu}=q_{\mu\nu}-\frac13\delta_{\mu\nu}q .
  \tag{4.4.59}\label{eq:4.4.59}
\end{equation}

This formula shows that the energy which is carried away by gravitational radiation
in ``ordinary laboratory processes'' is extremely small. For example, according to
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.58}, the gravitational energy flux from a rod of mass \(M\)
and length \(L\) which spins about its center at frequency \(\Omega\) (so that
\(T_{ab}\) oscillates with frequency \(2\Omega\)) is
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{rod}}=\frac{2G}{45c^5}M^2L^4\Omega^6 ,
  \tag{4.4.60}\label{eq:4.4.60}
\end{equation}
where we have put back the \(G\)'s and \(c\)'s in our formula to convert back to
``nongeometrized'' units. Thus, a 1 kilogram rod, of length 1 meter, spinning at an
angular velocity of 1 radian per second radiates energy in the form of gravitational
waves at the remarkably small rate of about \(10^{-47}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}\).
Even if we scale the rod up to astronomical dimensions, the gravitational energy
flux remains small. It is only in phenomena involving strong gravitational
fields---such as occurs in gravitational collapse---that one expects large amounts of
energy to be radiated away.

Finally, we comment on the validity of our solutions for the gravitational radiation,
equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.42} and \eqref{eq:4.4.49}, and energy flux,
equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.58}, from self-gravitating sources. As should be clear from
the above derivation, our solution, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.42}, is valid for the
gravitational radiation from sources such as spinning rods or masses connected by
springs, provided that gravity is sufficiently weak that the linear approximation is
valid. Equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.49} and \eqref{eq:4.4.58} hold if, in addition, the
source velocities are small. However, our derivation is \emph{not} valid, as it
stands, for the radiation from sources where self-gravitation is important---e.g., a
nearly Newtonian binary star system---even though gravity may be weak in the sense
that the inertial components \(h_{\mu\nu}\) are much less than 1 and the source
motion may be slow. The reason is that, as already mentioned at the end of
section 4.4a, to obtain the Newtonian limit consistently one must go beyond the
linear approximation. In the linear approximation, two stars would not orbit each
other but would move on geodesics of the flat metric, i.e., straight lines. Any
assumption to the contrary would be inconsistent with \(\partial^aT_{ab}=0\), which
follows from the linearized Einstein equation. Even though \(h_{\mu\nu}\ll1\),
higher order terms in \(h_{ab}\) are \emph{not} negligible compared with stress
terms in \(T_{ab}\). However, formulas for the radiation from a self-gravitating,
nearly Newtonian system can be obtained by restoring all the higher order terms in
\(h_{ab}\) into the Einstein field equation. Nearly Newtonian motion of the matter
source then is no longer inconsistent. The nonlinear terms in \(h_{ab}\) can be
brought to the right-hand side of the equation and viewed as an effective
gravitational stress-energy tensor \(\tilde t_{ab}\). (Here \(\tilde t_{ab}\) includes
all the nonlinear terms in \(G_{ab}\), not just the quadratic terms, as for \(t_{ab}\)
defined above.) Our solution, equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.42}, is then valid provided we
replace \(T_{ab}\) by \(T_{ab}+\tilde t_{ab}\), although it is no longer really a
``solution'' since \(\tilde t_{ab}\) depends on \(h_{ab}\). In the slow motion
approximation, we again get equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.49} and \eqref{eq:4.4.58},
where \(\tilde t_{00}\) is now included in the definition of the quadrupole moment.
However, in nearly Newtonian situations, we expect to have
\(T_{00}\gg\tilde t_{00}\),
so the gravitational contribution to the quadrupole moment should be negligible.
Thus, equations~\eqref{eq:4.4.49} and \eqref{eq:4.4.58} should be valid without
modification for a nearly Newtonian system. However, a derivation of these equations
with the same level of clarity and rigor as in the non-self-gravitating case has not
been given.

The above predictions of energy loss by systems due to emission of gravitational
radiation have now been confirmed by observations. If two stars in orbit around
each other lose energy, their orbital radius will decrease and their orbital frequency
will correspondingly increase. However, since the energy loss by gravitational
radiation from such a system is extremely small (problem 9), any possibility for
observing this speedup caused by emission of gravitational radiation requires (i) an
extremely close binary orbit (to make the general relativistic effects as large as
possible) and (ii) an ability to measure changes in the period to extremely high
accuracy. Remarkably, a binary system satisfying both of these properties was
discovered by Hulse and Taylor (1975). This system consists of two compact bodies
in an elliptical orbit with a maximum separation of only \(\sim10^{11}\,\mathrm{cm}\)
(i.e., approximately one solar radius). Most importantly, one of the bodies is a
pulsar (see the end of section 6.2 above) and emits radio pulses in a regular,
clocklike fashion. Since the arrival time of the pulsar signals can be measured to
very high precision, the time delays or advances caused by changes in the pulsar
position and velocity are very well determined, and the orbital parameters of the
binary system can be determined to correspondingly high accuracy. Furthermore,
the accuracy continues to improve the longer the system is observed. Recently, the
accuracy of the determination of the orbital period has been high enough to observe
a speedup in the orbital frequency (Taylor and McCulloch 1980). The magnitude of
this speedup is in excellent agreement with that associated with the energy loss due
to gravitational radiation predicted by equation~\eqref{eq:4.4.58}. Thus, unless
some other cause is producing an orbital period change of exactly the same
magnitude as that predicted by general relativity, it appears that the effects of
energy loss by gravitational radiation have been observed in this system.
