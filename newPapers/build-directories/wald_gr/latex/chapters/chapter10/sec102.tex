\subsection{Initial Value Formulation of General Relativity}\label{sec:10.2}
\setcounter{theorem}{0}

In this section, we shall show that general relativity has a well posed initial value
formulation. We prove this by casting Einstein's equation into the
form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} for which theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} applies. As we shall
see, the analysis of Einstein's equation differs from that of the Klein--Gordon field in
that there are initial value constraints and in that we will need to make a ``gauge
choice,'' i.e., a choice of coordinates, so that Einstein's equation takes the desired
form. In order to gain more insight into the nature of these differences, we begin by
analyzing the simpler problem of the initial value formulation of Maxwell's equations
for the vector potential \(A_a\) in Minkowski spacetime. As we shall see, the initial
value formulation of Einstein's equation is very closely analogous to the initial value
formulation of these equations.

As discussed in section~4.2, the vacuum Maxwell's equations for the vector potential
\(A_a\) in Minkowski spacetime take the form
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a(\partial_aA_b-\partial_bA_a)=0.
  \tag{10.2.1}\label{eq:10.2.1}
\end{equation}
At first glance, equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} may appear satisfactory for determining
\(A_a\): We have four equations for the four unknown components of \(A_a\).
However, a closer examination of equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} indicates the
possibility of serious trouble, since equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} is not of the
form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} for which a well posed initial value formulation is known
to exist. Indeed, if we choose a surface, \(\Sigma_0\), of constant inertial time,
\(t=t_0\), as our initial hypersurface, we see that the time component of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} contains no second time derivatives at all. In ordinary
vector notation, this equation is
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}^{,2}A_0
  -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}=0,
  \tag{10.2.2}\label{eq:10.2.2}
\end{equation}
i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{E}=0,
  \tag{10.2.3}\label{eq:10.2.3}
\end{equation}
where the electric field, \(\mathbf{E}\), is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{E}=\boldsymbol{\nabla} A_0-\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t},
  \tag{10.2.4}\label{eq:10.2.4}
\end{equation}
i.e., in index notation,
\begin{equation}
  E_a=(\partial_aA_b-\partial_bA_a)n^b=F_{ab}n^b,
  \tag{10.2.5}\label{eq:10.2.5}
\end{equation}
where \(n^a\) is the unit normal to \(\Sigma_0\). Thus,
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.2} (or, equivalently, Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.3}) gives an
\emph{initial value constraint} on the initial data
\((A_\mu,\partial A_\mu/\partial t)\). Initial data which fail to satisfy
\eqref{eq:10.2.2} cannot possibly yield a solution of Maxwell's equations.

The remaining three components of Maxwell's equations do contain second time
derivatives of the spatial components of \(A_a\), so we can solve for
\(\partial^2A_\mu/\partial t^2\) for \(\mu=1,2,3\) in the manner required by the
Cauchy--Kowalewski theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1}. (Note, however, that even these
three equations are not of the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} for which a well posed initial
value formulation is known to exist.) One might expect that by differentiating the
initial value constraint~\eqref{eq:10.2.2}, one could end up with an equation for
\(\partial^2A_0/\partial t^2\) which then would permit an initial value formulation,
at least in the sense of theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1}. However, this is not the case. We
have the identity
\begin{equation}
  \partial^b\partial^a(\partial_aA_b-\partial_bA_a)=0,
  \tag{10.2.6}\label{eq:10.2.6}
\end{equation}
and this shows that the time derivative of equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.2} vanishes
identically if the spatial components of Maxwell's equations are satisfied. Hence, the
complete Maxwell's equations are equivalent to the spatial components of Maxwell's
equations together with the initial value constraint~\eqref{eq:10.2.2}. Thus,
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} is an underdetermined system for \(A_a\); we really have
only three equations (plus an initial value constraint) for four unknown functions.
Indeed, by theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1} it is not difficult to see that in the analytic case
we may specify \(A_0\) arbitrarily throughout the spacetime and still obtain a solution.
Thus Maxwell's equations do not admit an initial value formulation for \(A_a\) in the
straightforward, mathematical sense discussed in the previous section.

However, this difficulty is not of a physical character. As already mentioned in
section~4.2, two vector potentials which differ by the gradient, \(\partial_a\chi\), of a
function, \(\chi\), represent the same physical electromagnetic field. Thus, on account
of this gauge arbitrariness, Maxwell's equations cannot possibly be expected to
determine \(A_a\) from initial conditions. On the other hand, we now shall show that
the initial values of \(A_\mu\) and \(\partial A_\mu/\partial t\) do uniquely determine a
solution up to gauge, and that, physically, Maxwell's equations do admit a well posed
initial value formulation.

The most direct way of showing this is to choose an appropriate gauge for \(A_a\) and
show that Maxwell's equations for \(A_a\) in this gauge are of the
form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20}, thereby yielding a well posed initial value formulation. We
choose the Lorenz gauge
\begin{equation}
  \partial^aA_a=0
  \tag{10.2.7}\label{eq:10.2.7}
\end{equation}
(see Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.2.31} above). Maxwell's equations in this gauge are simply
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a\partial_aA_b=0.
  \tag{10.2.8}\label{eq:10.2.8}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} together with equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.7} is physically
equivalent to the original system~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} in the sense that solutions of
\eqref{eq:10.2.1} can differ from solutions of \eqref{eq:10.2.7} and
\eqref{eq:10.2.8} only by a gauge transformation.

Given our initial data \((A_\mu,\partial A_\mu/\partial t)\), we make a gauge
transformation so that \(\partial^aA_a=0\) on \(\Sigma_0\). Now,
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} implies that
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a\partial_a(\partial^bA_b)
  =\partial^b(\partial^a\partial_aA_b)=0,
  \tag{10.2.9}\label{eq:10.2.9}
\end{equation}
and thus, by theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.2}, if equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} is satisfied
everywhere, then the gauge condition~\eqref{eq:10.2.7} also will be satisfied
everywhere if and only if \(\partial^bA_b=\partial(\partial^bA_b)/\partial t=0\) on
\(\Sigma_0\). We already have ensured that \(\partial^aA_a=0\) on \(\Sigma_0\),
and using equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8}, we see that the initial condition
\(\partial(\partial^bA_b)/\partial t=0\) is equivalent to the initial value constraint,
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.2}. Thus, if
\(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{E}=0\) on \(\Sigma_0\), then for our gauge
transformed initial data, equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.7} will be satisfied throughout the
spacetime if equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} holds. Thus, we need only solve
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} with the given values of the initial data. However, we
can do this because equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} \emph{does} have the
form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} for which a well posed initial value formulation has been
established. Thus, by theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.2}, there exists a unique solution of
\eqref{eq:10.2.8} for the given (new) values of the initial data. Furthermore, this
solution depends continuously on the initial data and has the desired domain of
dependence property.

To obtain a solution with the original values \((A_\mu,\partial A_\mu/\partial t)\)
of the initial data, we simply ``undo'' the gauge transformation which made
\(\partial^aA_a=0\) on \(\Sigma_0\). To show that this solution is physically
unique, we note that two solutions of the original Maxwell's
equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.1} with identical initial data can be brought by gauge
transformations into solutions of \eqref{eq:10.2.8} with identical initial data. Since
solutions of \eqref{eq:10.2.8} with given initial data are unique, this shows that our
two solutions can differ, at most, by a gauge transformation. Thus, Maxwell's
equations in Minkowski spacetime possess a well posed initial value formulation in
this sense.

Indeed, we may reformulate this result in a physically more satisfactory manner as
follows: Let \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) be specified as arbitrary smooth vector fields
on \(\Sigma_0\), subject only to the constraints
\(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{E}
=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{B}=0\) on \(\Sigma_0\). Then there exists a unique
solution, \(F_{ab}\), of Maxwell's equations with these initial data. Furthermore,
\(F_{ab}\) depends continuously on the initial data \((\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})\), and \(F_{ab}\)
at \(p\in J^+(\Sigma_0)\) depends only on the initial data on
\(J^-(p)\cap\Sigma_0\). This result can be proven by introducing a vector potential,
\(A_a\), whose initial data on \(\Sigma_0\) satisfies \(\partial^aA_a=0\) and
reproduces the given values of \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) on \(\Sigma_0\). (A
particularly simple choice is to take \(A_0=0\),
\(\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t=-\mathbf{E}\), choose \(\mathbf{A}\) to be any solution of
\(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}\), and set
\(\partial A_0/\partial t=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{A}\).) Then we simply apply
the results of the previous paragraph to construct a solution with the desired
properties. Finally, we prove uniqueness by verifying that two different solutions,
\(A_a\) and \(A'_a\), of Maxwell's equations which reproduce the given \(\mathbf{E}\)
and \(\mathbf{B}\) on \(\Sigma_0\) can be brought by a gauge transformation into
solutions of equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8} with the same initial data. Hence, by
uniqueness of solutions of equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.8}, \(A_a\) and \(A'_a\) differ at
most by a gauge transformation. Thus, they produce the same \(F_{ab}\).

Thus, Maxwell's equations in Minkowski spacetime physically possess a well posed
initial value formulation. This result may be generalized to curved spacetime (see
problem~2).

We turn our attention, now, to Einstein's equation in a vacuum, \(G_{ab}=0\). The first
issue to consider is the nature of the initial value formulation in this theory. In other
theories of classical physics we are given the spacetime background and our task is to
determine the time evolution of quantities in the background from their initial values
and time derivatives. However, in general relativity we are solving for the spacetime
itself. What should be the quantity or quantities to prescribe initially in general
relativity in order that spacetime structure be determined?

In order to answer this question, we must view general relativity as describing the time
evolution of some quantity. Let \((M,g_{ab})\) be a globally hyperbolic spacetime. (We
consider only globally hyperbolic spacetimes since the initial value formulation should
be relevant only in this case.) As proven in theorem~\ref{thm:8.3.14}, we can foliate
\((M,g_{ab})\) by Cauchy surfaces, \(\Sigma_t\), parameterized by a global time
function, \(t\). Let \(n^a\) be the unit normal vector field to the hypersurfaces
\(\Sigma_t\). The spacetime metric, \(g_{ab}\), induces a spatial metric (i.e., a
three-dimensional Riemannian metric) \(h_{ab}\) on each \(\Sigma_t\) by the formula
\begin{equation}
  h_{ab}=g_{ab}+n_an_b.
  \tag{10.2.10}\label{eq:10.2.10}
\end{equation}
Let \(t^a\) be a vector field on \(M\) satisfying \(t^a\nabla_at=1\). We decompose
\(t^a\) into its parts normal and tangential to \(\Sigma_t\) by defining the \emph{lapse
function}, \(N\), and the \emph{shift vector}, \(N^a\), with respect to \(t^a\) by
\begin{equation}
  N=-t^an_a=(n^a\nabla_at)^{-1}
  \tag{10.2.11}\label{eq:10.2.11}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  N^a=h^a{}_bt^b
  \tag{10.2.12}\label{eq:10.2.12}
\end{equation}
(see Fig.~\ref{fig:10.2}).

\input{figures/chapter10/fig10_2.tex}

We may interpret the vector field \(t^a\) as representing the ``flow of time''
throughout spacetime. As we ``move forward in time'' by parameter time \(t\) starting
from the \(t=0\) surface \(\Sigma_0\), we go to the surface \(\Sigma_t\). If we
identify the hypersurfaces \(\Sigma_0,\Sigma_t\) by the diffeomorphism resulting from
following integral curves of \(t^a\), we may view the effect of ``moving forward in
time'' as that of changing the spatial metric on an abstract three-dimensional manifold
\(\Sigma\) from \(h_{ab}(0)\) to \(h_{ab}(t)\). Thus, we may view a globally
hyperbolic spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) as representing the time development of a
Riemannian metric on a fixed three-dimensional manifold. This suggests that we view
the spatial metric on a three-dimensional hypersurface as the dynamical variable in
general relativity. (The lapse function, \(N\), and shift vector, \(N^a\), are not
considered dynamical, since they merely prescribe how to ``move forward in time.''
Further motivation for viewing the spatial metric as the dynamical variable in general
relativity arises from the Hamiltonian formulation given in appendix~E.) Hence, we
would expect appropriate initial data to consist of a Riemannian metric, \(h_{ab}\), and
its ``time derivative'' on a three-dimensional manifold \(\Sigma\).

In section~9.3 above, we introduced the notion of extrinsic curvature as representing
a well-defined notion of the ``time derivative'' of the spatial metric on a hypersurface
\(\Sigma\) embedded in spacetime. In equation~\eqref{eq:9.3.19}, \(\xi^a\) was the
unit tangent to the congruence of timelike geodesics orthogonal to \(\Sigma\). However,
if \(n^a\) is any other unit timelike vector field which is normal to \(\Sigma\), then
its derivative along a direction tangential to \(\Sigma\) must agree on \(\Sigma\) with
that of \(\xi^a\), so we have
\begin{align}
  K_{ab}
  &=\nabla_a\xi_b=h_a{}^c\nabla_c\xi_b \notag\\
  &=h_a{}^c\nabla_cn_b \notag\\
  &=\frac12\mathcal L_nh_{ab},
  \tag{10.2.13}\label{eq:10.2.13}
\end{align}
where the reader is asked to verify the last equality in problem~3. This generalizes
the formula for extrinsic curvature to nongeodesic normal slicings of spacetime. The
relation between \(K_{ab}\) and the coordinate time derivative
\(\mathcal L_th_{ab}\) is given by equation~(E.2.30) of appendix~E.
Figure~\ref{fig:10.3} illustrates the interpretation of \(K_{ab}\) in terms of the
``bending'' of \(\Sigma\) in spacetime.

The above considerations suggest that in general relativity, appropriate initial data
should consist of a triple \((\Sigma,h_{ab},K_{ab})\), where \(\Sigma\) is a
three-dimensional manifold, \(h_{ab}\) is a Riemannian metric on \(\Sigma\), and
\(K_{ab}\) is a symmetric tensor field on \(\Sigma\). We shall show below that given
such initial data---subject to certain initial value constraints---there exists a globally
hyperbolic spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) satisfying Einstein's equation which possesses a
Cauchy surface diffeomorphic to \(\Sigma\) on which the induced metric,
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.10}, is \(h_{ab}\), and the induced extrinsic curvature,
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.13}, is \(K_{ab}\). Furthermore, this solution depends
continuously on the initial data, satisfies the desired domain of dependence property,
and is unique in a sense made precise below.

First, however, we establish some useful relations between the spacetime metric,
derivative operator, and curvature, and the corresponding quantities they induce on a
spacelike hypersurface \(\Sigma\) embedded in \(M\). We already noted that the
spacetime metric \(g_{ab}\) induces a Riemannian metric \(h_{ab}\) on \(\Sigma\) by
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.10}. However, by theorem~\ref{thm:3.1.1}, \(h_{ab}\)
uniquely determines a natural derivative operator on \(\Sigma\), which we denote as
\(D_a\). Furthermore, the derivative operator, \(D_a\), on \(\Sigma\) gives rise to a
curvature tensor \({}^{(3)}R_{abc}{}^d\) on \(\Sigma\). We obtain, now, formulas
relating \(D_a\) and \({}^{(3)}R_{abc}{}^d\) to four-dimensional quantities.

Let \(v^a\) be a (spacetime) vector at a point \(p\in\Sigma\). We may uniquely
decompose \(v^a\) into components tangent to and perpendicular to \(\Sigma\) via
\begin{equation}
  v^a=v_\perp n^a+v_\parallel^a,
  \tag{10.2.14}\label{eq:10.2.14}
\end{equation}
where \(n^a\) is the unit normal to \(\Sigma\) and \(v_\parallel^an_a=0\). If
\(v_\perp=0\), so that \(v^a=v_\parallel^a\), we may view \(v^a\) as a vector lying
in the tangent space to \(\Sigma\) at \(p\). The condition that \(v_\perp=0\) is
equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  v^a=h^a{}_bv^b,
  \tag{10.2.15}\label{eq:10.2.15}
\end{equation}
with \(h_{ab}\) given by equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.10} and the first index of \(h_{ab}\)
is raised by \(g^{ab}\). More generally, we may view a spacetime tensor
\(T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) at \(p\in\Sigma\) as a tensor over the
tangent space to \(\Sigma\) at \(p\) if
\begin{equation}
  T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}
  =h^{a_1}{}_{c_1}\cdots h^{a_k}{}_{c_k}
   h^{d_1}{}_{b_1}\cdots h^{d_l}{}_{b_l}
   T^{c_1\cdots c_k}{}_{d_1\cdots d_l}.
  \tag{10.2.16}\label{eq:10.2.16}
\end{equation}
Conversely, any tensor defined at point \(p\) on the manifold \(\Sigma\) uniquely
gives rise to a spacetime tensor at \(p\) (i.e., a tensor over the tangent space to
\(M\) at \(p\)) which satisfies equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.16}. Note that \(h^a{}_b\)
plays the role of a projection operator from the tangent space to \(M\) at \(p\) to the
tangent space to \(\Sigma\) at \(p\).

Let \(T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) be a tensor field on the manifold
\(\Sigma\). If we view \(T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) as a spacetime
tensor satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.16}, we still cannot define
\(\nabla_cT^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\), since in order to calculate this
quantity we would need to know how \(T^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) varies
as we move off of \(\Sigma\). However,
\(h_c{}^d\nabla_dT^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}\) is well defined since for
this quantity, no derivatives in directions pointing out of \(\Sigma\) are taken. This
tensor need not satisfy equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.16}, but we can project its indices
using \(h^a{}_b\) to obtain a tensor field on \(\Sigma\). Then, we have the following
result.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:10.2.1}
Let \((M,g_{ab})\) be a spacetime and let \(\Sigma\) be a smooth spacelike
hypersurface in \(M\). Let \(h_{ab}\) denote the induced metric on \(\Sigma\),
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.10}, and let \(D_a\) denote the derivative operator associated
with \(h_{ab}\) (see theorem~\ref{thm:3.1.1}). Then \(D_a\) is given by the formula
\begin{equation}
  D_cT^{a_1\cdots a_k}{}_{b_1\cdots b_l}
  =h^{a_1}{}_{d_1}\cdots h^{a_k}{}_{d_k}
   h^{e_1}{}_{b_1}\cdots h^{e_l}{}_{b_l}h_c{}^f
   \nabla_fT^{d_1\cdots d_k}{}_{e_1\cdots e_l},
  \tag{10.2.17}\label{eq:10.2.17}
\end{equation}
where \(\nabla_a\) is the derivative operator associated with \(g_{ab}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
It is straightforward to verify that \(D_a\), defined by
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.17}, satisfies the properties (1)--(5) of the definition of
derivative operator given in section~3.1. Furthermore, we have
\begin{equation}
  D_ah_{bc}
  =h_a{}^dh_b{}^eh_c{}^f\nabla_d(g_{ef}+n_en_f)=0
  \tag{10.2.18}\label{eq:10.2.18}
\end{equation}
since \(\nabla_dg_{ef}=0\) and \(h_{ab}n^b=0\). Thus, \(D_a\) is the unique
derivative operator associated with \(h_{ab}\).
\end{proof}

From equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.17} one can derive relations between the curvature
\({}^{(3)}R_{abc}{}^d\) of \(\Sigma\) and the spacetime curvature \(R_{abc}{}^d\).
If \(\omega_a\) is a one-form field on \(\Sigma\), we have
\begin{equation}
  {}^{(3)}R_{abc}{}^d\omega_d=D_aD_b\omega_c-D_bD_a\omega_c.
  \tag{10.2.19}\label{eq:10.2.19}
\end{equation}
However, we have
\begin{align}
  D_aD_b\omega_c
  &=D_a(h_b{}^dh_c{}^e\nabla_d\omega_e) \notag\\
  &=h_a{}^fh_b{}^gh_c{}^e
    \nabla_f(h_g{}^dh_e{}^k\nabla_d\omega_k) \notag\\
  &=h_a{}^fh_b{}^dh_c{}^e\nabla_f\nabla_d\omega_e
    +h_c{}^eK_{ab}n^d\nabla_d\omega_e
    +h_b{}^dK_{ac}n^e\nabla_d\omega_e,
  \tag{10.2.20}\label{eq:10.2.20}
\end{align}
where we have used the fact that
\begin{equation}
  h_a{}^bh_c{}^d\nabla_bh_d{}^e
  =h_a{}^bh_c{}^d\nabla_b(g_d{}^e+n_dn^e)=K_{ac}n^e.
  \tag{10.2.21}\label{eq:10.2.21}
\end{equation}
Now, the middle term on the right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.20}
vanishes when antisymmetrized over \(a\) and \(b\). Furthermore, we have
\begin{equation}
  h_b{}^dn^e\nabla_d\omega_e
  =h_b{}^d\nabla_d(n^e\omega_e)-h_b{}^d\omega_e\nabla_dn^e
  =-K_b{}^e\omega_e.
  \tag{10.2.22}\label{eq:10.2.22}
\end{equation}
Putting together all these results, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  {}^{(3)}R_{abc}{}^d
  =h_a{}^fh_b{}^gh_c{}^eh_j{}^dR_{fge}{}^j
   -K_{ac}K_b{}^d+K_{bc}K_a{}^d.
  \tag{10.2.23}\label{eq:10.2.23}
\end{equation}
A similar calculation (problem~4) shows that
\begin{equation}
  D_aK^a{}_b-D_bK^a{}_a=R_{cd}n^dh^c{}_b.
  \tag{10.2.24}\label{eq:10.2.24}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.23} and \eqref{eq:10.2.24} are known as the
Gauss--Codazzi relations. % SOURCE ERRATUM: printed “Gauss-Codacci.”

We turn, now, to the analysis of the vacuum Einstein's equation. We will give initial
data \((h_{ab},K_{ab})\) on a three-dimensional manifold \(\Sigma\) and attempt to
construct a globally hyperbolic spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) for which \(\Sigma\) is a
Cauchy surface on which the initial data are induced. Our strategy is to write down
Einstein's equation for the metric components, \(g_{\mu\nu}\), in a local coordinate
system \(\{y^\mu\}\) with the time coordinate, \(t\), chosen so that the \(t=0\)
surface corresponds to \(\Sigma\) (or, at least, that portion of \(\Sigma\) covered by
the coordinate system). By casting the equations in the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21}, we
will use theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} to prove local existence of a solution with the
desired properties. Then we shall outline how to ``globalize'' our local results to
obtain the final conclusion, stated as theorem~\ref{thm:10.2.2} below.

The components of the Einstein tensor, \(G_{\mu\nu}\), can be expressed in terms of
coordinate derivatives of the metric tensor components, \(g_{\mu\nu}\), by the methods
of section~3.4a. Einstein's equation in vacuum, \(G_{ab}=0\), yields a system of ten
second-order partial differential equations for the ten unknown metric components.
Furthermore, these equations have a quasilinear form; i.e., they are linear in the
second derivatives of the metric. Explicitly, from equations~\eqref{eq:3.4.5} and
\eqref{eq:3.1.30} we have
\begin{align}
  R_{\mu\nu}
  &=-\frac12\sum_{\alpha,\beta}g^{\alpha\beta}
    \left\{-2\partial_\beta\partial_{(\nu}g_{\mu)\alpha}
      +\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}
      +\partial_\mu\partial_\nu g_{\alpha\beta}\right\}
    +F_{\mu\nu}(g,\partial g).
  \tag{10.2.25}\label{eq:10.2.25}
\end{align}
and thus
\begin{align}
  G_{\mu\nu}
  &=R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12g_{\mu\nu}R \notag\\
  &=-\frac12\sum_{\alpha,\beta}g^{\alpha\beta}
    \left\{-2\partial_\beta\partial_{(\nu}g_{\mu)\alpha}
      +\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}
      +\partial_\mu\partial_\nu g_{\alpha\beta}\right\} \notag\\
  &\quad+\frac12\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\rho,\sigma}
    g_{\mu\nu}g^{\alpha\beta}g^{\rho\sigma}
    \left\{-\partial_\beta\partial_\rho g_{\sigma\alpha}
      +\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\rho\sigma}\right\}
    +\overline F_{\mu\nu}(g,\partial g),
  \tag{10.2.26}\label{eq:10.2.26}
\end{align}
where \(F\) and \(\overline F\) are nonlinear functions of the metric components
\(g_{\alpha\beta}\) and their first derivatives. However, the right-hand side of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.26} is not of the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21}. Indeed, from
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.26} one may show (problem~5) that the equations
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\nu G_{\mu\nu}n^\nu=0
  \tag{10.2.27}\label{eq:10.2.27}
\end{equation}
(where \(n^a\) is the unit normal to the \(t=\text{constant}\) surfaces) contain no
second time derivatives of any of the metric components; i.e., these components of
\(G_{ab}=0\) at \(t=0\) depend only on the initial data. Thus, these equations provide
initial value constraints, in close analogy with equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.2} in the
electromagnetic case. We can express these equations in coordinate invariant form by
using the Gauss--Codazzi equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.23} and \eqref{eq:10.2.24}.
From equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.24}, we obtain the initial value constraint,
\begin{equation}
  0=h^b{}_aG_{bc}n^c=h^b{}_aR_{bc}n^c
   =D_bK^b{}_a-D_aK^b{}_b.
  \tag{10.2.28}\label{eq:10.2.28}
\end{equation}
In addition, we have
\begin{align}
  R_{abcd}h^{ac}h^{bd}
  &=R_{abcd}(g^{ac}+n^an^c)(g^{bd}+n^bn^d) \notag\\
  &=R+2R_{ac}n^an^c \notag\\
  &=2G_{ac}n^an^c.
  \tag{10.2.29}\label{eq:10.2.29}
\end{align}
Thus, from equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.23} we obtain the additional constraint,
\begin{equation}
  0=G_{ab}n^an^b
   =\frac12\left\{{}^{(3)}R+(K^a{}_a)^2-K_{ab}K^{ab}\right\}.
  \tag{10.2.30}\label{eq:10.2.30}
\end{equation}
Thus, equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.28} and \eqref{eq:10.2.30} are the initial value
constraint equations of general relativity expressed in a form analogous to
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.3}. We shall return briefly to discuss some properties of
these equations at the end of this section.

In the electromagnetic case, the identity~\eqref{eq:10.2.6} implied that if the
constraint~\eqref{eq:10.2.3} is satisfied initially and the spatial components of
Maxwell's equations are satisfied everywhere, then the constraint also is satisfied
everywhere. A completely analogous result applies in general relativity. As a
consequence of the Bianchi identity,
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^aG_{ab}=0,
  \tag{10.2.31}\label{eq:10.2.31}
\end{equation}
if the constraints~\eqref{eq:10.2.28} and \eqref{eq:10.2.30} are satisfied initially
and the spatial components of Einstein's equation are satisfied everywhere, then the
constraints also are satisfied. To show this, we note that
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.31} relates the time derivative of the components
\(\sum_\nu G_{\mu\nu}n^\nu\), to non-time differentiated components of
\(G_{\mu\nu}\) and their spatial derivatives. Having solved the purely spatial
components of Einstein's equation and obtained a solution, \(g_{\mu\nu}\), we may set
the spatial components of \(G_{\mu\nu}\) equal to zero in equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.31}
and view the metric components \(g_{\mu\nu}\) as known functions, thus making
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.31} a linear, homogeneous system of four first order equations
for the four unknown components \(\sum_\nu G_{\mu\nu}n^\nu\). It then follows from
the theory of first order partial differential equations (see, e.g., Courant and Hilbert
1962) that if these components vanish initially, they must vanish everywhere.

Thus, Einstein's equation \(G_{ab}=0\), is an underdetermined system of equations for
the metric components \(g_{\mu\nu}\). We have only six evolution equations (namely,
the purely spatial components of \(G_{ab}=0\)) for ten unknown metric components.
However, as in the Maxwell case this underdetermination is not physical. It results
from the redundancy in the description of spacetime geometry by metric components,
\(g_{\mu\nu}\). As discussed in appendix~C, if \(\phi:M\to M\) is a diffeomorphism,
then \((M,g_{ab})\) and \((M,\phi^*g_{ab})\) represent the same physical spacetime.
Since the coordinate basis components of \(g_{ab}\) and \(\phi^*g_{ab}\) are related by
the coordinate transformation associated with \(\phi\), any two solutions of Einstein's
equation whose coordinate basis metric components are related by the tensor
transformation law~\eqref{eq:2.3.8} represent the same physical solution. Since four
arbitrary functions appear in the transformation law, roughly speaking there should
be only six ``nongauge'' functions in the ten metric components \(g_{\mu\nu}\). Thus,
it is plausible that Einstein's equation contains the correct number of evolution
equations, and that a well posed initial value formulation exists. We shall prove that
this is the case by introducing---in close analogy with our treatment of Maxwell's
equations---a convenient choice of ``gauge'' (i.e., coordinates) for which Einstein's
equation has the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21}.

We shall employ \emph{harmonic coordinates}, \(x^\mu\), i.e., coordinates which satisfy
\begin{equation}
  H^\mu\equiv\nabla_a\nabla^ax^\mu=0.
  \tag{10.2.32}\label{eq:10.2.32}
\end{equation}
In a given spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\), we can construct harmonic coordinates in a
neighborhood of that portion of \(\Sigma\) covered by our original set of coordinates
\(\{y^\mu\}\) as follows. For \(\mu=0,1,2,3\) we give \(\{y^\mu\}\) and its normal
derivative on \(\Sigma\) as initial data for equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.32} (which is of
the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} and thus possesses a well posed initial value formulation).
Since the covectors \(\{\nabla_ay^\mu\}\) are linearly independent on \(\Sigma\), the
solutions \(\{x^\mu\}\) will have \(\{\nabla_ax^\mu\}\) linearly independent in a
neighborhood of \(\Sigma\) in \(M\), so \(\{x^\mu\}\) will yield a local coordinate
system. Thus, there is no loss of generality in assuming local existence of harmonic
coordinates.

Writing out the coordinate basis expression for equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.32} using
the formulas of section~3.4a, we find that the harmonic coordinate condition takes the
form
\begin{align}
  0=H^\mu
  &=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac1{\sqrt{-g}}
    \partial_\alpha\!\left[\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\alpha\beta}
      \partial_\beta x^\mu\right] \notag\\
  &=\sum_\alpha\frac1{\sqrt{-g}}
    \partial_\alpha\!\left[\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\alpha\mu}\right] \notag\\
  &=\sum_\alpha\left[\partial_\alpha g^{\alpha\mu}
    +\frac12g^{\alpha\mu}\sum_{\rho,\sigma}
      g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\alpha g_{\rho\sigma}\right].
  \tag{10.2.33}\label{eq:10.2.33}
\end{align}
Using equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.33}, we see that most of the second derivative terms
in equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.25} can be reexpressed in terms of \(H^\mu\) and lower
derivative terms, and thus in harmonic coordinates the vacuum Einstein equation
becomes
\begin{align}
  0=R^H_{\mu\nu}
  &\equiv R_{\mu\nu}+\sum_\alpha g_{\alpha(\mu}\partial_{\nu)}H^\alpha \notag\\
  &=-\frac12\sum_{\alpha,\beta}g^{\alpha\beta}
    \partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}
    +\widehat F_{\mu\nu}(g,\partial g),
  \tag{10.2.34}\label{eq:10.2.34}
\end{align}
where the superscript \(H\) on \(R^H_{\mu\nu}\) denotes that this expression for the
Ricci tensor is valid only for harmonic coordinates. Thus, Einstein's equation is
equivalent to the system~\eqref{eq:10.2.34}, together with the harmonic coordinate
condition~\eqref{eq:10.2.32} or \eqref{eq:10.2.33} (Choquet--Bruhat 1962).
Equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} is known as the ``reduced Einstein equation.'' The key
point is that it is of the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} for which
theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} applies.

We now are in a position to prove local existence of a solution to Einstein's equation
for initial data sufficiently near that of flat spacetime. Let the Riemannian metric
\(h_{ab}\) and the symmetric tensor field \(K_{ab}\) be given on \(\Sigma\) satisfying
the constraint equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.28} and \eqref{eq:10.2.30}. Choose a
coordinate system on (a portion of) \(\Sigma\) and let \(h_{\mu\nu}\) and
\(K_{\mu\nu}\) denote the coordinate basis components of \(h_{ab}\) and \(K_{ab}\).
We prescribe on \(\Sigma\) initial data \((g_{\mu\nu},\partial g_{\mu\nu}/\partial t)\)
such that \(g_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}\) for \(\mu,\nu=1,2,3\), and such that the extrinsic
curvature computed from these initial data using equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.13} is
\(K_{ab}\). A particularly simple choice is to take \(g_{00}=-1\), \(g_{0\mu}=0\) for
\(\mu=1,2,3\), and \(\partial g_{\mu\nu}/\partial t=K_{\mu\nu}\) for
\(\mu,\nu=1,2,3\). Since \(\partial g_{0\mu}/\partial t\) for \(\mu=0,1,2,3\) is
undetermined by these requirements, we may specify \(\partial g_{0\mu}/\partial t\)
such that \(H^\mu=0\) on \(\Sigma\) (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.33}). If this initial
data set is sufficiently near that of flat spacetime, then according to
theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} we can solve equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} in a neighborhood
of the portion of \(\Sigma\) covered by our original coordinates, thereby producing a
globally hyperbolic spacetime with this portion of \(\Sigma\) serving as a Cauchy
surface. This solution of equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} will be a solution of Einstein's
equation if \(H^\mu=0\) in this neighborhood. To prove this is the case, we note that
the Einstein tensor \(G_{\mu\nu}\) in arbitrary coordinates can be expressed in terms
of \(R^H_{\mu\nu}\) (defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.34}) and \(H^\mu\) (given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.33}) via
\begin{equation}
  G_{\mu\nu}=R^H_{\mu\nu}-\frac12R^Hg_{\mu\nu}
  -\sum_\alpha\left\{g_{\alpha(\mu}\partial_{\nu)}H^\alpha
    -\frac12g_{\mu\nu}\partial_\alpha H^\alpha\right\}.
  \tag{10.2.35}\label{eq:10.2.35}
\end{equation}
Since \(\sum_\nu G_{\mu\nu}n^\nu=0\) on \(\Sigma\) and \(H^\mu=0\) on
\(\Sigma\), equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.35} implies that
\(\partial H^\mu/\partial t=0\) on \(\Sigma\) if equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} is
satisfied. Furthermore, when equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} is satisfied, the Bianchi
identity yields
\begin{align}
  0=\sum_\mu\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu}
  &=-\sum_{\rho,\mu,\alpha}g^{\rho\mu}\nabla_\rho
    \left[g_{\alpha(\mu}\partial_{\nu)}H^\alpha
      -\frac12g_{\mu\nu}\partial_\alpha H^\alpha\right] \notag\\
  &=-\sum_{\rho,\mu,\alpha}\frac12g_{\alpha\nu}g^{\rho\mu}
    \partial_\rho\partial_\mu H^\alpha
    +\{\text{lower order terms linear in }H^\alpha\}.
  \tag{10.2.36}\label{eq:10.2.36}
\end{align}
Thus, equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.36} (after multiplication by \(g^{\lambda\nu}\) and
summation over \(\nu\)) takes the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} for which a unique
solution exists according to theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.2}. Since
\(H^\mu=\partial H^\mu/\partial t=0\) initially, this proves that \(H^\mu=0\)
throughout the region where a solution to equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} exists. Thus,
we have established local existence of a solution of Einstein's equation for initial
data sufficiently near that of flat spacetime. Furthermore, theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3}
shows that the solution depends continuously on the initial data and has the desired
domain of dependence property.

The requirement that the initial data be ``sufficiently near'' that of flat spacetime can
be removed by the following trick which uses the idea that any curved geometry
appears ``nearly flat'' when examined on a sufficiently small scale. Suppose initial
data \((g_{\mu\nu},\partial g_{\mu\nu}/\partial t)\) which are not ``sufficiently small''
are given. By a coordinate transformation on \(\Sigma\), we may assume that
\(g_{\mu\nu}=\diag(-1,1,1,1)\) at a point \(p\in\Sigma\) and that \(p\) lies at the
origin of coordinates, \(x^\mu=0\). Let \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\). Suppose we scale
the initial data by
\((g_{\mu\nu},\partial g_{\mu\nu}/\partial t)
\mapsto(\lambda^{-2}g_{\mu\nu},\lambda^{-2}\partial g_{\mu\nu}/\partial t)\), and
then make the coordinate transformation \(x^\mu\mapsto x'^\mu=\lambda^{-1}x^\mu\)
for \(\mu=0,1,2,3\). From the tensor transformation law~\eqref{eq:2.3.8}, we see
that under this combined transformation, the initial data become
\begin{equation*}
  g'_{\mu\nu}(x')=g_{\mu\nu}(\lambda x'),
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial g'_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t'}(x')
  =\lambda\frac{\partial g_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t}(\lambda x'),
\end{equation*}
where \(x'\) denotes the new spatial coordinates on \(\Sigma\). As \(\lambda\to0\),
we see that the new initial data and their derivatives become arbitrarily close to those
of data for flat spacetime. Thus, there exists a (sufficiently small) \(\lambda_0\) such
that we can obtain a solution, \(g^0_{\mu\nu}(x')\), to Einstein's equation in a
neighborhood of \(p\) for the new initial data with \(\lambda=\lambda_0\). The metric
\(g^0_{\mu\nu}(\lambda_0^{-1}x)\) then solves Einstein's equation in a neighborhood
of \(p\) with the original initial data.

Local uniqueness of solutions with given initial data \((h_{ab},K_{ab})\) can be
proven as follows. Let \((O^H,g^H_{ab})\) be the spacetime solution of Einstein's
equation constructed using harmonic coordinates from the initial data
\((g^H_{\mu\nu},\partial g^H_{\mu\nu}/\partial t)\) in the manner described above.
Let \((O,g_{ab})\) be another solution of Einstein's equation (not necessarily in
harmonic coordinates) which covers the same portion of \(\Sigma\) as
\((O^H,g^H_{ab})\) and induces the same initial data \((h_{ab},K_{ab})\) on
\(\Sigma\). We wish to find a diffeomorphism \(\psi\) from a neighborhood of
\(\Sigma\) in \(O\) to a neighborhood of \(\Sigma\) in \(O^H\) such that \(\psi\)
takes \(g_{ab}\) into \(g^H_{ab}\), i.e., \(\psi^*g_{ab}=g^H_{ab}\). We construct
\(\psi\) as follows. Since \(g_{ab}\) and \(g^H_{ab}\) induce the same initial data
on \(\Sigma\), it can be verified that there exists a diffeomorphism \(\phi\) such that
the coordinate components of \(\phi^*g_{ab}\) and its time derivative on \(\Sigma\)
agree with the initial data \((g^H_{\mu\nu},\partial g^H_{\mu\nu}/\partial t)\) of the
solution \(g^H_{ab}\). (In fact, there are many such diffeomorphisms, since \(\phi\)
can be arbitrary away from \(\Sigma\).) We then may use \(\phi^{-1}\) to bring the
coordinates of \(O^H\) into \(O\). We use the initial value and time derivative of
these coordinates on \(\Sigma\) as initial data for a solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.32} in \(O\), thereby constructing harmonic coordinates in
\(O\). Using these harmonic coordinate labels, we define \(\psi\) to be the map which
takes a point in \(O\) to the point of \(O^H\) with the same values of the harmonic
coordinates. (In general, of course, \(\psi\) will be defined only on a neighborhood
of \(\Sigma\) in \(O\) and will map only onto a neighborhood of \(\Sigma\) in
\(O^H\).) Then, in this neighborhood in \(O^H\) the harmonic coordinate components
of \(\psi^*g_{ab}\) will satisfy equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.34} and will have the same
initial data on \(\Sigma\) as \(g^H_{ab}\). Hence, by theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} we
must have \(\psi^*g_{ab}=g^H_{ab}\) in a neighborhood of \(\Sigma\), which is the
desired local uniqueness result.

We outline, now, how to ``globalize'' these local existence and uniqueness results.
We proved above that for \(p\in\Sigma\) there exists a solution \((O,g_{ab})\) of
Einstein's equation into which a neighborhood of \(\Sigma\) containing \(p\) can be
embedded so that the given initial data set is induced on this portion of \(\Sigma\).
To show that there is a solution of Einstein's equation containing all of \(\Sigma\), we
cover \(\Sigma\) by neighborhoods for which local solutions exist. Using the
paracompactness of \(\Sigma\) (see appendix~A), we can ensure that these
neighborhoods have only finite overlap at each point. Using the local uniqueness result
proven above, it follows that for each \(p\in\Sigma\) we can find a globally hyperbolic
spacetime \((\widetilde O,\widetilde g_{ab})\) containing \(p\) (and with a portion of
\(\Sigma\) serving as a Cauchy surface) which can be isometrically mapped into every
local solution in the above family which contains \(p\). By using the embeddings of
the spacetimes \((\widetilde O,\widetilde g_{ab})\) into the local solutions to make
identifications, we can consistently ``patch together'' the
\((\widetilde O,\widetilde g_{ab})\) to construct a spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) which
solves Einstein's equation, which contains all of \(\Sigma\), and which induces on
\(\Sigma\) the given initial data. Furthermore, this spacetime will be globally
hyperbolic with Cauchy surface \(\Sigma\).

The spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) constructed above clearly is not unique since any proper
open subset of \(M\) containing \(\Sigma\) is another solution of Einstein's equation
inducing the same initial data on \(\Sigma\). However, we can consider the set
\(\mathcal S\) of all globally hyperbolic spacetimes modulo diffeomorphisms which are
solutions of Einstein's equation and into which \(\Sigma\) with the given initial data
can be embedded as a Cauchy surface. For two spacetimes
\((M_1,g^1_{ab}),(M_2,g^2_{ab})\) in \(\mathcal S\), we say
\((M_1,g^1_{ab})\geq(M_2,g^2_{ab})\) if \((M_2,g^2_{ab})\) can be isometrically
mapped into \((M_1,g^1_{ab})\) keeping the Cauchy surface fixed. This relation yields
a ``partial order'' on \(\mathcal S\). [Here, a partial order on an arbitrary set \(S\)
is a relation between elements satisfying, for all \(a,b,c\in S\): (i) \(a\geq a\),
(ii) \(a\geq b\) and \(b\geq c\) implies \(a\geq c\), and (iii) \(a\geq b\) and
\(b\geq a\) implies \(a=b\). The word ``partial'' refers to the fact that ``\(\geq\)''
need not be defined for all pairs of elements.] Now, for any partially ordered set \(S\),
a subset \(T\subseteq S\) for which the relation \(\geq\) is defined between all pairs
of elements is said to be \emph{totally ordered}. An \emph{upper bound} for \(T\) is an
element \(b\in S\) such that \(b\geq a\) for all \(a\in T\). Zorn's lemma (which is
equivalent to the axiom of choice) asserts that if every totally ordered subset of \(S\)
has an upper bound, then \(S\) has a \emph{maximal element}, i.e., an element
\(m\in S\) such that, for all \(c\in S\), the relation \(c\geq m\) implies \(c=m\). In
our case, given any totally ordered subset \(\mathcal T\) of \(\mathcal S\), we obtain
an upper bound by taking the union of all spacetimes occurring in \(\mathcal T\) and
then identifying points via the isometric embedding maps. Hence, by Zorn's lemma
there exists a maximal element of \(\mathcal S\), i.e., a spacetime
\((\widetilde M,\widetilde g_{ab})\) which cannot be isometrically mapped into any
other spacetime in \(\mathcal S\). In general, Zorn's lemma implies existence of a
maximal element, but not its uniqueness. However, in our case, if we had a spacetime
in \(\mathcal S\) which could not be isometrically mapped into
\((\widetilde M,\widetilde g_{ab})\), then it can be shown (Choquet--Bruhat and
Geroch 1969) that we could ``patch'' the two spacetimes together to produce a
``larger'' solution, in violation of the maximality of
\((\widetilde M,\widetilde g_{ab})\). This implies that
\((\widetilde M,\widetilde g_{ab})\) is the unique spacetime having the property that
every globally hyperbolic spacetime with the given initial data on the Cauchy surface
\(\Sigma\) can be isometrically mapped into \((\widetilde M,\widetilde g_{ab})\).

Thus, putting together all the results proven or outlined above, we arrive at the
following theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:10.2.2}
Let \(\Sigma\) be a three-dimensional \(C^\infty\) manifold, let \(h_{ab}\) be a
smooth Riemannian metric on \(\Sigma\), and let \(K_{ab}\) be a smooth symmetric
tensor field on \(\Sigma\). Suppose \(h_{ab}\) and \(K_{ab}\) satisfy the constraint
equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.28} and \eqref{eq:10.2.30}. Then there exists a unique
\(C^\infty\) spacetime, \((M,g_{ab})\), called the \emph{maximal Cauchy development}
of \((\Sigma,h_{ab},K_{ab})\), satisfying the following four properties: (i)
\((M,g_{ab})\) is a solution of Einstein's equation. (ii) \((M,g_{ab})\) is globally
hyperbolic with Cauchy surface \(\Sigma\). (iii) The induced metric and extrinsic
curvature of \(\Sigma\) are, respectively, \(h_{ab}\) and \(K_{ab}\). (iv) Every other
spacetime satisfying (i)--(iii) can be mapped isometrically into a subset of
\((M,g_{ab})\). Furthermore, \((M,g_{ab})\) satisfies the desired domain of dependence
property in the following sense. Suppose \((\Sigma,h_{ab},K_{ab})\) and
\((\Sigma',h'_{ab},K'_{ab})\) are initial data sets with maximal developments
\((M,g_{ab})\) and \((M',g'_{ab})\). Suppose there is a diffeomorphism between
\(S\subseteq\Sigma\) and \(S'\subseteq\Sigma'\), which carries \((h_{ab},K_{ab})\)
on \(S\) into \((h'_{ab},K'_{ab})\) on \(S'\). Then \(D(S)\) in the spacetime
\((M,g_{ab})\) is isometric to \(D(S')\) in the spacetime \((M',g'_{ab})\). Finally,
the solution \(g_{ab}\) on \(M\) depends continuously on the initial data
\((h_{ab},K_{ab})\) on \(\Sigma\). (A precise definition of the topologies on initial
data and solutions which makes this map continuous is given in Hawking and Ellis
1973.)
\end{theorem}

Note that it may be possible to extend the ``maximal development'' \((M,g_{ab})\),
i.e., isometrically map it into a proper subset of another spacetime.
Theorem~\ref{thm:10.2.2} asserts only that any such extension cannot have \(\Sigma\)
as a Cauchy surface. It also should be noted that theorem~\ref{thm:10.2.2} gives no
information as to the ``size'' of \((M,g_{ab})\), other than the fact that it is maximal
in the sense of property (iv). Indeed, the singularity theorems of chapter~9 show that
in many cases \((M,g_{ab})\) cannot be geodesically complete. In particular,
theorem~9.5.1 gives a stringent limit on ``how large'' \((M,g_{ab})\) can be for initial
data with \(K^a{}_a\geq C>0\). On the other hand, recently it has been shown
(Christodoulou and O'Murchadha 1981) that for asymptotically flat initial data (see
chapter~11) the maximal development is ``large enough'' to include all ``boosted''
hypersurfaces in the asymptotic region.

Aside from showing that general relativity has the physically desirable property of
possessing a well posed initial value formulation, theorem~\ref{thm:10.2.2} also is
very useful in that it puts globally hyperbolic spacetimes \((M,g_{ab})\) satisfying
Einstein's equation into correspondence with initial data sets \((\Sigma,h_{ab},K_{ab})\)
satisfying the constraint equations. [The association of spacetimes with initial data
sets, of course, is not one-to-one; many distinct initial data sets give rise to the same
spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\), corresponding to the freedom of choosing a spacelike
Cauchy surface in \(M\).] It usually is far easier to solve the constraint equations on
\(\Sigma\) than to solve Einstein's equation on \(M\). Thus, for example, in arguments
involving existence of certain types of solutions of Einstein's equation, great
simplifications usually can be achieved if the question can be posed in terms of initial
data sets. Furthermore, a number of issues in general relativity, such as the positivity
of total energy of isolated systems, are formulated most naturally in terms of initial
data sets (see section~11.2).

A relatively simple method exists for generating solutions of the constraint equations
with \(K^a{}_a=0\) (Lichnerowicz 1944; York 1971). Given \(\Sigma\), one prescribes
an arbitrary Riemannian metric, \(h_{ab}\), on \(\Sigma\) and solves the relatively
simple constraint~\eqref{eq:10.2.28},
\begin{equation}
  D^aK_{ab}=0
  \tag{10.2.37}\label{eq:10.2.37}
\end{equation}
for a trace-free (\(K^a{}_a=0\)) tensor field \(K_{ab}\). Of course,
\((h_{ab},K_{ab})\) will not, in general, satisfy the additional
constraint~\eqref{eq:10.2.30}. However, we let \(\widetilde h_{ab}=\phi^4h_{ab}\)
and let \(\widetilde D_a\) be the derivative operator associated with
\(\widetilde h_{ab}\). As shown in appendix~D, if we define
\(\widetilde K_{ab}=\phi^{-2}K_{ab}\), then equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.37} implies that
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde D^a\widetilde K_{ab}=0.
  \tag{10.2.38}\label{eq:10.2.38}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, using equation~(D.9) of appendix~D, the constraint
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.30} for \((\widetilde h_{ab},\widetilde K_{ab})\) can be
expressed in terms of \(\phi,h_{ab}\), and \(K_{ab}\) as
\begin{equation}
  D^aD_a\phi-\frac18R\phi+\frac18\phi^{-7}K^{ab}K_{ab}=0,
  \tag{10.2.39}\label{eq:10.2.39}
\end{equation}
where the indices here are raised by \(h^{ab}\) and \(R\) is the scalar curvature of
\(h_{ab}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.39} is a nonlinear elliptic equation for \(\phi\).
Locally (i.e., in sufficiently small regions), solutions of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.39} always exist, although global solutions (i.e., solutions
defined over all of \(\Sigma\)) may not exist. (Results on the global existence of
solutions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.39}, as well as of the similar equation which results
when \(K^a{}_a\) is a nonzero constant, are reviewed by Choquet--Bruhat and York
1980.) Thus, in the cases where equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.39} can be solved, an initial
data set \((\Sigma,\widetilde h_{ab},\widetilde K_{ab})\) satisfying the constraint
equations (as well as \(\widetilde K^a{}_a=0\)) is generated from the set
\((\Sigma,h_{ab},K_{ab})\) satisfying merely equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.37} together
with \(K^a{}_a=0\).

A particularly simple choice of \(K_{ab}\) which satisfies
equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.37} and \(K^a{}_a=0\) is \(K_{ab}=0\). [If \(K_{ab}=0\) on
\(\Sigma\), then \(\Sigma\) is referred to as a \emph{moment of time symmetry}. It is
not difficult to see that the maximal development \((M,g_{ab})\) generated by initial
data with \(K_{ab}=0\) will possess a reflection isometry about \(\Sigma\).]
Equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.39} then becomes a linear equation on \(\phi\). If, in
addition, we choose \(h_{ab}\) to be flat, then equation~\eqref{eq:10.2.39} reduces to
Laplace's equation in ordinary three-dimensional space. The monopole solution
\(\phi=1+M/2r\) yields initial data for the Schwarzschild solution. The solution of
Laplace's equation obtained by superimposing two monopoles at different positions can
be interpreted as initial data for two Schwarzschild black holes (Hahn and Lindquist
1964). Thus, the maximal development \((M,g_{ab})\) arising from these initial data
is a spacetime where two black holes are initially at rest, and then, presumably, fall
together and ``collide.'' A portion of this spacetime has been obtained by numerical
solution of Einstein's equation using computers by Smarr (1979).

An interesting issue that can be investigated (at least in a crude way) for a theory
possessing an initial value formulation is how many ``degrees of freedom'' the theory
has, i.e., ``how many'' distinct solutions of the equations exist. In particle mechanics,
we defined above the number of degrees of freedom to be the dimension, \(n\), of the
configuration space. As discussed at the beginning of section~10.1, a proper initial
data set for such a system consists of the \(2n\) initial positions and velocities. Thus,
an equivalent characterization of the number of degrees of freedom in ordinary
particle mechanics is that it is the number of quantities that must be specified as initial
data divided by 2. For the Klein--Gordon field, a proper initial data set consists of the
value of the field and the value of its normal derivative on a Cauchy surface \(\Sigma\),
i.e., two arbitrary functions on \(\Sigma\). Thus, by analogy with particle mechanics,
we may say that the Klein--Gordon field has ``one degree of freedom for each point of
space.''

How many degrees of freedom does the gravitational field have in general relativity?
A proper initial data set for Einstein's equations consists of specifying 12 functions
on \(\Sigma\): the six independent components of \(h_{ab}\), plus the six independent
components of \(K_{ab}\). However, the constraint equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.28} and
\eqref{eq:10.2.30} impose four relations on these 12 quantities, thus effectively
reducing the number of ``freely specifiable'' functions on \(\Sigma\) to eight.
Furthermore, many of the spacetimes generated by the initial data given by these eight
``freely specifiable'' functions are physically equivalent. In particular, if
\(\phi:\Sigma\to\Sigma\) is a diffeomorphism, then the data \(\phi^*h_{ab}\) and
\(\phi^*K_{ab}\) on \(\Sigma\) generates the same physical spacetime as \(h_{ab}\)
and \(K_{ab}\). Thus, three of the eight freely specifiable functions correspond to
diffeomorphisms on \(\Sigma\) and are not physically relevant. Furthermore, as
mentioned above, initial data sets which cannot be taken into each other by a
diffeomorphism still can correspond to different choices of Cauchy surface in the
same spacetime and thus be physically equivalent. Since, roughly speaking, one
arbitrary function is needed to specify a choice of Cauchy surface in a spacetime, the
number of nongauge freely specifiable functions on \(\Sigma\) is reduced to four.
Dividing by 2, we conclude that the gravitational field has two degrees of freedom per
point of space. This is the same number of degrees of freedom as a linear spin-2 field
propagating in flat spacetime, to which general relativity reduces in the weak field
limit (see section~4.4b). Note that the above very crude ``function counting'' argument
in no way singles out precisely which functions (i.e., which of the 12 metric or
extrinsic curvature components or functions of them) can be freely specified, which
functions are determined by the constraints, and which functions correspond to gauge
transformations. Indeed, one of the major obstacles to developing a quantum theory of
gravity (see chapter~14) is the inability to single out the physical degrees of freedom
of the theory.

Finally, we comment briefly on the initial value formulation of Einstein's equation
with matter sources, \(T_{ab}\). First, the initial value constraints for the gravitational
field now take the form
\begin{equation}
  G_{ab}n^b=8\pi T_{ab}n^b.
  \tag{10.2.40}\label{eq:10.2.40}
\end{equation}
From equations~\eqref{eq:10.2.28} and \eqref{eq:10.2.30} we obtain
\begin{equation}
  D^a(K_{ab}-K^c{}_ch_{ab})=-8\pi J_b,
  \tag{10.2.41}\label{eq:10.2.41}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  {}^{(3)}R+(K^a{}_a)^2-K_{ab}K^{ab}=16\pi\rho,
  \tag{10.2.42}\label{eq:10.2.42}
\end{equation}
where \(\rho=T_{ab}n^an^b\) and \(J_b=-h_b{}^cT_{ca}n^a\).

The existence of a well posed initial value formulation for Einstein's equation with
matter depends critically on the dynamical equations satisfied by the matter as well as
on the formula for the stress-energy tensor in terms of the matter and spacetime metric.
If the matter consists of fields \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n\) satisfying an equation of the
form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} (with \(g_{ab}\) the spacetime metric) and if \(T_{ab}\)
depends only on the fields, the metric, and the first derivatives of the fields and metric,
then the combined Einstein--matter field system in harmonic coordinates will be of
the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21}, so a well posed initial value formulation will exist.
Thus, the Einstein--Klein--Gordon equations and the Einstein--Maxwell equations have
well posed initial value formulations. The existence of a well posed initial value
formulation for a few systems not satisfying equations of the
form~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} also has been established. In particular, the Einstein--perfect
fluid system for appropriate choices of equation of state, \(P=P(\rho)\), is known to
possess a well posed initial value formulation (see Hawking and Ellis 1973). The
existence of a well posed initial value formulation does not single out Einstein's
equation from equations which occur in some alternative theories of gravity. In
particular, the Brans--Dicke equations are equivalent to the Einstein--Klein--Gordon
equations (see Dicke 1962) and hence possess a well posed initial value formulation.
Some ``higher derivative'' theories of gravity also have been shown to have a well
posed initial value formulation (Noakes 1983). However, it should be emphasized that
the existence of a well posed initial value formulation is far from an automatic feature
of most theories. In particular, the natural generalization to curved spacetime of the
equations for linear fields of spin greater than 1 (see chapter~13) fail to have a well
posed initial value formulation.
