\subsection{Initial Value Formulation for Particles and Fields}\label{sec:10.1}
\setcounter{theorem}{0}

A fundamental feature of Newton's second law of motion
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}
  \tag{10.1.1}\label{eq:10.1.1}
\end{equation}
in ordinary, nonrelativistic, particle mechanics is that it relates the second time
derivative, \(\mathbf{a}\), of the spatial position of a particle to the force, \(\mathbf{F}\),
which---in usual cases---is a known function of the position and velocity of the
particles in the system. Thus, for a system of particles interacting with themselves
and/or external potentials with forces possibly dependent on positions and velocities
but not on higher time derivatives of the particle positions, the laws of mechanics
take the form
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd^2q_i}{\dd t^2}
  =F_i\left(q_1,\ldots,q_n;
    \frac{\dd q_1}{\dd t},\ldots,\frac{\dd q_n}{\dd t}\right),
  \tag{10.1.2}\label{eq:10.1.2}
\end{equation}
where \(i=1,\ldots,n\) and the number, \(n\), of unknown positions is called the
number of \emph{degrees of freedom} of the system. Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.2} is a
system of \(n\) second order ordinary differential equations for the \(n\) quantities
\(q_1(t),\ldots,q_n(t)\). From the theory of such ordinary differential equations, it
is well known (see, e.g., Coddington and Levinson 1955) that, given arbitrary initial
values for the particle positions \(q_{10},\ldots,q_{n0}\), and velocities
\((\dd q_1/\dd t)_0,\ldots,(\dd q_n/\dd t)_0\) at \(t=t_0\), there always exists a
unique solution of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.2} over a finite time interval about \(t_0\)
with these initial values. Thus, ordinary particle mechanics possesses an initial value
formulation. Furthermore, at a fixed time \(t\) the positions
\(q_1(t),\ldots,q_n(t)\) are continuous functions of the initial positions and velocities
of the particles. Since the causal propagation of changes in the initial data is not an
issue in nonrelativistic mechanics, we conclude that the initial value formulation of
nonrelativistic particle mechanics is well posed.

The above simple example from particle mechanics contains the essential features of
initial value formulations found in all physically reasonable field theories. Consider,
for example, the massive Klein--Gordon field, \(\phi\), propagating in Minkowski
spacetime,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_a\partial^a\phi-m^2\phi=0.
  \tag{10.1.3}\label{eq:10.1.3}
\end{equation}
Choosing global inertial coordinates \(t,x,y,z\), we may write this equation in the form
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2}
  =\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}
   +\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}
   +\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}-m^2\phi.
  \tag{10.1.4}\label{eq:10.1.4}
\end{equation}
The mathematical structure of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} is markedly different from
that of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.2}: Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.2} is a system of
ordinary differential equations while equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} is a single partial
differential equation. Nevertheless, the essential content of these equations is quite
similar. They both tell us how to compute the second time derivative of the unknown
quantity (or quantities) at an instant of time, given the value and first time derivative
of the quantity (or quantities) at that time. (Actually, for
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} one does not even need to know \(\partial\phi/\partial t\) to
compute \(\partial^2\phi/\partial t^2\), but in more general equations or with
non-inertial time slicings, \(\partial\phi/\partial t\) and its first spatial derivatives
would appear on the right side of the equation corresponding to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.1.4}.) Indeed, we may heuristically view
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} as arising from the limit as \(N\to\infty\) of a system
of \(N\) particles coupled by nearest neighbor harmonic oscillator interactions (see,
e.g., Goldstein 1980). In this limit, the discrete index \(i\) goes over to the continuous
label \(\mathbf{x}\) and the finite set of variables \(q_i(t)\) satisfying
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.2} goes over to the field variable \(\phi(\mathbf{x},t)\)
satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4}.

The physical and mathematical analogy between equations~\eqref{eq:10.1.2} and
\eqref{eq:10.1.4} suggests that Klein--Gordon theory should have the following
initial value formulation: We arbitrarily specify the values of \(\phi\) and
\(\partial\phi/\partial t\) on a spatial hypersurface \(\Sigma_0\) of constant inertial
time, \(t=t_0\). Then there should exist a unique solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} having this initial data.

Indeed, it is not difficult to show that Klein--Gordon theory admits this formulation
when one considers only analytic initial data, i.e., when \(\phi\) and
\(\partial\phi/\partial t\) are analytic functions on \(\Sigma_0\). To see this, we
observe that from such initial data, we can compute all spatial derivatives of \(\phi\)
and \(\partial\phi/\partial t\) at \(t=t_0\). Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} then gives
\(\partial^2\phi/\partial t^2\) at \(t=t_0\), and by taking spatial derivatives of
this equation, we may compute all the spatial derivatives of
\(\partial^2\phi/\partial t^2\) at \(t=t_0\) in terms of previously computed
quantities. We then may differentiate equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} with respect to
\(t\), and thereby compute \(\partial^3\phi/\partial t^3\) and all its spatial
derivatives at \(t=t_0\). Continuing in this manner, we obtain \emph{all derivatives}
of \(\phi\) at \(t=t_0\). This enables us to write down a formal power series solution
for \(\phi\). As proven by Cauchy and generalized by Kowalewski, for a wide class of
partial differential equations---or even systems of partial differential equations---this
power series has a finite radius of convergence. We state their theorem for second
order partial differential equations (i.e., equations containing second partial derivatives
of the unknown variables but none of higher order), but it is easily generalized to
equations of arbitrary order. A proof of this theorem can be found in most books
treating partial differential equations (e.g., Courant and Hilbert 1962).

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Kowalewski theorem]\label{thm:10.1.1}
Let \(t,x^1,\ldots,x^{m-1}\) be coordinates of \(\mathbb R^m\). Consider a
system of \(n\) partial differential equations for \(n\) unknown functions
\(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n\) in \(\mathbb R^m\), having the form
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial^2\phi_i}{\partial t^2}
  =F_i\left(t,x^\alpha;\phi_j;
    \frac{\partial\phi_j}{\partial t};
    \frac{\partial\phi_j}{\partial x^\alpha};
    \frac{\partial^2\phi_j}{\partial t\,\partial x^\alpha};
    \frac{\partial^2\phi_j}{\partial x^\alpha\partial x^\beta}\right),
  \tag{10.1.5}\label{eq:10.1.5}
\end{equation}
where each \(F_i\) is an analytic function of its variables. Let \(f_i(x^\alpha)\)
and \(g_i(x^\alpha)\) be analytic functions. Then there is an open neighborhood
\(O\) of the hypersurface \(t=t_0\) such that within \(O\) there exists a unique
analytic solution of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.5} such that
\(\phi_i(t_0,x^\alpha)=f_i(x^\alpha)\) and
\(\partial\phi_i(t_0,x^\alpha)/\partial t=g_i(x^\alpha)\).
\end{theorem}

The Cauchy--Kowalewski theorem shows that Klein--Gordon theory has an initial
value formulation, at least for analytic initial data. It shows that, in analogy to particle
mechanics, the initial value of \(\phi\) and its time derivative may be specified
arbitrarily, and that these initial values determine the subsequent evolution of \(\phi\).
It thereby also shows that there exists a large class of solutions to the Klein--Gordon
equation, since there exist as many analytic solutions of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4}
as there are pairs of arbitrary analytic functions of the three spatial variables
\(x^\alpha\).

However, the Cauchy--Kowalewski analysis is not adequate for showing that the
initial value formulation of Klein--Gordon theory is well posed. First, the analysis
does not establish continuous dependence of solutions on initial data in a suitable
sense. More precisely, we may define a topology on the space of initial data which
makes two functions ``close'' if they and a finite number (or all) of their derivatives
are close. For example, we may define a ``distance'' between two functions \(f_1\)
and \(f_2\) on the \(t=t_0\) initial data surface \(\Sigma_0\), by summing the least
upper bounds (l.u.b.) of the magnitude of \((f_1-f_2)\) and all its derivatives up to
order \(k\),
\begin{align}
  \lVert f_1-f_2\rVert
  &={\mathop{\rm l.u.b.}_{x\in\Sigma_0}}\,|f_1(x)-f_2(x)| \notag\\
  &\quad+\sum_{k_1,k_2,k_3}
  {\mathop{\rm l.u.b.}_{x\in\Sigma_0}}
  \left|
    \frac{\partial^{k_1+k_2+k_3}(f_1-f_2)}
    {\partial x^{k_1}\partial y^{k_2}\partial z^{k_3}}
  \right|,
  \tag{10.1.6}\label{eq:10.1.6}
\end{align}
where \(k_1+k_2+k_3\leq k\). We then may take the open balls in this norm as a
basis of a topology (see appendix~A). (Other reasonable choices of topology also
can be given.) In a compact region of spacetime, we may define a similar topology
on the solutions. The Cauchy--Kowalewski theorem gives no guarantee that---for any
reasonable choices of topology on the space of initial data and the space of
solutions---the map taking analytic initial data to the analytic solutions to which they
give rise is continuous.

Furthermore, the Cauchy--Kowalewski analysis cannot even deal with the issue of
causal propagation of the field. An analytic function is uniquely determined by the
values of it and all its derivatives at one point and, thus, in particular, is uniquely
determined by its value in an arbitrarily small open neighborhood of a point. This
implies that in the analytic case if we alter the initial data in an arbitrary open region
\(U\) of the initial surface \(\Sigma_0\), we must, in fact, alter the initial data over
the entire hypersurface \(\Sigma_0\). Thus, to analyze the issue of causal propagation,
we must consider nonanalytic initial data. However, the Cauchy--Kowalewski analysis
does not even prove existence of a solution for \(C^\infty\), nonanalytic, initial data.

Thus, to show that the initial value formulation of Klein--Gordon theory is well posed,
methods other than the Cauchy--Kowalewski analysis are required. We now shall
outline a proof that the initial value formulation of the massive Klein--Gordon field
in Minkowski spacetime is well posed. Then we shall state some much more general
results obtained by this approach.

Let \(\phi\) be a smooth solution of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.3}. Then the
stress-energy-momentum tensor of \(\phi\),
\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}=\partial_a\phi\,\partial_b\phi
  -\frac12\eta_{ab}\bigl(\partial_c\phi\,\partial^c\phi+m^2\phi^2\bigr),
  \tag{10.1.7}\label{eq:10.1.7}
\end{equation}
(see Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.2.20}), is conserved,
\begin{equation}
  \partial^aT_{ab}=0.
  \tag{10.1.8}\label{eq:10.1.8}
\end{equation}
Consequently, letting \(\xi^a=(\partial/\partial t)^a\) denote the time translation
Killing field orthogonal to the \(t=t_0\) hypersurface \(\Sigma_0\), we have
\begin{equation}
  \partial^a(T_{ab}\xi^b)=0.
  \tag{10.1.9}\label{eq:10.1.9}
\end{equation}
[In fact, for any Killing field \(\xi^a\) and any conserved, symmetric \(T_{ab}\) in
curved spacetime, we have \(\nabla^a(T_{ab}\xi^b)=T_{ab}\nabla^a\xi^b=0\) by
Eq.~(C.3.1).] Let \(S_0\) be a (three-dimensional) closed ball on the initial
hypersurface \(\Sigma_0\). Let \(\Sigma_1\) denote the hypersurface \(t=t_1\)
(with \(t_1>t_0\)), let \(K=D^+(S_0)\cap I^-(\Sigma_1)\), and let
\(S_1=D^+(S_0)\cap\Sigma_1\). Finally, let \(S_2\) denote the ``null portion'' of
the boundary of \(K\) (see Fig.~\ref{fig:10.1}). We integrate
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.9} over \(K\) and apply Gauss's law, thereby obtaining
\begin{equation}
  \int_{S_1}T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b
  +\int_{S_2}T_{ab}l^a\xi^b
  =\int_{S_0}T_{ab}\xi^a\xi^b,
  \tag{10.1.10}\label{eq:10.1.10}
\end{equation}
\input{figures/chapter10/fig10_1.tex}
where \(l^a\) is the future directed normal to \(S_2\). (The natural volume elements
on \(S_0\) and \(S_1\) are understood in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.1.10}; the volume
element on the null surface \(S_2\) is explained in appendix~B.) However, it is not
difficult to verify from equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.7} that \(T_{ab}\) satisfies the
dominant energy condition, i.e., if \(v^a\) is a future directed timelike vector, then
\(-T^a{}_bv^b\) is a future directed timelike or null vector. Consequently, we have
\(T_{ab}l^a\xi^b\geq0\). Hence, the second term on the left-hand side of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.10} is nonnegative. Writing out the other terms explicitly,
we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \int_{S_1}\left[
    \left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)^2
    +|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi|^2+m^2\phi^2\right]
  \leq
  \int_{S_0}\left[
    \left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)^2
    +|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi|^2+m^2\phi^2\right].
  \tag{10.1.11}\label{eq:10.1.11}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.11} is the key equation needed to demonstrate the
existence of a well-posed initial value formulation. First, it shows---without appealing
to analyticity---that there can be at most one solution in \(D^+(S_0)\) with given
initial data \((\phi,\partial\phi/\partial t)\) on \(S_0\). Namely, if \(\phi_1\) and
\(\phi_2\) both are \(C^2\) functions satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.3} with the
same smooth (but not necessarily analytic) initial data, then their difference,
\(\psi=\phi_2-\phi_1\), would be a \(C^2\) solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.3} with vanishing initial data. Hence, for \(\psi\), the
right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.11} vanishes, which implies (assuming
\(m\ne0\)) that \(\psi=0\) on \(S_1\) and hence (since \(\Sigma_1\) is arbitrary)
\(\psi=0\) throughout \(D^+(S_0)\). [If \(m=0\), we have
\(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi=0\) and \(\partial\psi/\partial t=0\) in \(D^+(S_0)\), and since
\(\psi=0\) on \(S_0\), this again implies that \(\psi=0\) throughout
\(D^+(S_0)\).] Similarly, \(\psi\) also vanishes throughout \(D^-(S_0)\). This
result also shows that the second requirement for a well posed initial value formulation
is satisfied: A variation of the initial data outside of \(S_0\) cannot affect the solution
within \(D(S_0)\).

Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.11} also shows that solutions depend continuously on the
initial data. To prove a useful form of this continuous dependence that also allows us
to prove existence of solutions for arbitrary smooth initial data, we proceed as follows.
For simplicity, we shall restrict attention to the massive case, \(m\ne0\). First, by
differentiating equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} with respect to the coordinates \(x^\mu\),
we see that all partial derivatives of \(\phi\) also satisfy the Klein--Gordon equation.
Hence, we obtain inequalities of the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.11} bounding square
integrals of higher space and time derivatives of \(\phi\) on \(S_1\) by the
corresponding square integrals on \(S_0\). Using equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4}, we
can express all terms on \(S_0\) containing more than one time derivative in terms
of the initial data \(\phi,\partial\phi/\partial t\) and their spatial derivatives on
\(S_0\). Thus, we obtain inequalities of the form
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\phi\rVert_{S_1,k}
  \leq C_{1,k}\triplenorm{\phi}_{S_0,k}
  +C_{2,k}\triplenorm{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}}_{S_0,k-1},
  \tag{10.1.12}\label{eq:10.1.12}
\end{equation}
where the norms \(\lVert\phi\rVert_{S_1,k}\) and
\(\triplenorm{\phi}_{S_0,k}\) are defined by
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\phi\rVert_{S_1,k}^2
  =\int_{S_1}\left\{|\phi|^2+\cdots+
    \sum_i|\partial^{k_i}\phi|^2\right\},
  \tag{10.1.13}\label{eq:10.1.13}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \triplenorm{\phi}_{S_0,k}^2
  =\int_{S_0}\left\{|\phi|^2+\cdots+
    \sum_i|D^{k_i}\phi|^2\right\},
  \tag{10.1.14}\label{eq:10.1.14}
\end{equation}
where \(\partial^{k_i}\) denotes a \(k\)th order partial derivative with respect to
space and time coordinates, while \(D^{k_i}\) denotes a \(k\)th order partial
derivative with respect to space coordinates only. (In
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.1.13} and \eqref{eq:10.1.14} we take the sums of all such
partial derivatives of order less than or equal to \(k\). The norms defined by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.1.13} and \eqref{eq:10.1.14} are called \emph{Sobolev norms}.)
By integrating equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.12} over \(t_1\) from \(t_0\) to the
maximum value of \(t\) for which \(D^+(S_0)\cap\Sigma_t\ne\varnothing\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\phi\rVert_{D^+(S_0),k}
  \leq C'_{1,k}\triplenorm{\phi}_{S_0,k}
  +C'_{2,k}\triplenorm{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}}_{S_0,k-1}.
  \tag{10.1.15}\label{eq:10.1.15}
\end{equation}

Now, we apply the following key result. Let \(A\) be any subset of \(\mathbb R^n\)
(with the natural Euclidean metric) which satisfies the \emph{uniform interior cone
condition}, defined as follows: There exists a cone of fixed height \(h\) and fixed
vertex angle \(\theta\) such that for each \(p\in A\) this cone can be mapped
isometrically into \(A\) with vertex at \(p\). Then, for \(k>n/2\) the
\(\lVert\ \rVert_{A,k}\) norm of a smooth function \(f\) (defined by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.1.13} with the integration region taken to be \(A\)) bounds the
numerical values of \(f\) in \(A\), i.e., there exists a constant \(C\) such that
\begin{equation}
  {\mathop{\rm l.u.b.}_{x\in A}}\,|f(x)|\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{A,k}.
  \tag{10.1.16}\label{eq:10.1.16}
\end{equation}
A proof of this result is outlined in problem~1. Thus, taking \(A=D^+(S_0)\) and
\(k=3\), we find using equations~\eqref{eq:10.1.15} and \eqref{eq:10.1.16},
\begin{equation}
  {\mathop{\rm l.u.b.}_{x\in D^+(S_0)}}\,|\phi|
  \leq C''_1\triplenorm{\phi}_{S_0,3}
  +C''_2\triplenorm{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}}_{S_0,2}.
  \tag{10.1.17}\label{eq:10.1.17}
\end{equation}
Similarly, the numerical values of any \(m\)th order partial derivative of \(\phi\)
are bounded in terms of the initial data by
\begin{equation}
  {\mathop{\rm l.u.b.}_{x\in D^+(S_0)}}\,|\partial^m\phi|
  \leq C''_{1,m}\triplenorm{\phi}_{S_0,3+m}
  +C''_{2,m}\triplenorm{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}}_{S_0,2+m}.
  \tag{10.1.18}\label{eq:10.1.18}
\end{equation}
The same type of bounds hold, of course, for \(x\in D^-(S_0)\).

Equations~\eqref{eq:10.1.17} and \eqref{eq:10.1.18} demonstrate the continuous
dependence of \(\phi\) and its derivatives on the initial data in a strong sense. More
precisely, if we define a topology on solutions in \(D(S_0)\) via a norm of the form
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.6} with \(k=m\) and define a topology on the initial data
on \(S_0\) (or on all of \(\Sigma_0\)) via the
\(\triplenorm{\ }_{S_0,3+m}\) norm or the norm
\eqref{eq:10.1.6} with \(k=m+3\), then equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.18}
demonstrates that the linear map from initial data to solutions is bounded and, hence,
is continuous. More generally, the map from initial data on \(\Sigma_0\) to solutions
in any fixed compact region of spacetime is continuous in these topologies.

Finally, we use this continuity to prove existence of a smooth solution \(\phi\) for
arbitrary smooth initial data \((\phi,\partial\phi/\partial t)\) on \(\Sigma_0\). We
proceed by choosing a sequence \(\{(\phi_i^m,\partial\phi_i^m/\partial t)\}\),
\(i=1,2,\ldots\), of analytic initial data on \(\Sigma_0\) such that the functions in
this sequence and their spatial derivatives up to order \((3+m)\) converge uniformly
to \((\phi,\partial\phi/\partial t)\) on \(S_0\). By theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1} there
exists a solution \(\phi_i^m\) with initial data
\((\phi_i^m,\partial\phi_i^m/\partial t)\) on \(\Sigma_0\). (Actually,
theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1} as stated guarantees existence of a solution only in an open
neighborhood of \(\Sigma_0\), but further analysis in the linear case establishes that
a solution exists throughout \(\mathbb R^n\).) However, according to
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.18} [and the corresponding inequality for \(D^-(S_0)\)],
\(\{\phi_i^m\}\) and its first \(m\) derivatives must converge uniformly in
\(D(S_0)\) to a function \(\phi^m\) and its first \(m\) derivatives. Choosing
\(m\geq2\), we easily verify that this limit function \(\phi^m\) must satisfy
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4}. Thus, for all \(m\geq2\) we obtain a \(C^m\) solution
in \(D(S_0)\). However, we proved above that there is at most one \(C^2\) solution
in \(D(S_0)\). Thus, \(\phi^m=\phi^{m'}=\phi\) for all \(m,m'\geq2\). Since
\(\phi\) is \(C^m\) for all \(m\geq2\), we have proven existence of a
\(C^\infty\) solution throughout \(D(S_0)\). Since \(S_0\) is arbitrary, this
solution exists on all of \(\mathbb R^4\).

Thus, we have established that the massive Klein--Gordon field in Minkowski
spacetime has a well posed initial value formulation. Note that, unlike the
Cauchy--Kowalewski analysis, the proof of a well posed initial value formulation
made use of detailed structure of the Klein--Gordon equation. In particular, the
linearity of the equation was used in a number of places, and its ``wave equation''
character was essential to the construction of a conserved \(T_{ab}\) with positive
energy. If we changed the sign of \(\partial^2\phi/\partial t^2\) in
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4}, thereby turning equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.4} into a
four-dimensional ``Laplace type'' equation, we would not be able to construct such
a \(T_{ab}\), and the method of proof would break down even though the
applicability of theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1} would not be affected. Indeed, it is well
known that equations of ``Laplace type'' do not have a well posed initial value
formulation.

The above results for the Klein--Gordon field can be significantly generalized. In
particular, we may replace the Klein--Gordon equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.3} in
\(\mathbb R^4\) by any equation on a manifold \(M\) of the form
\begin{equation}
  g^{ab}\nabla_a\nabla_b\phi+A^a\nabla_a\phi+B\phi+C=0,
  \tag{10.1.19}\label{eq:10.1.19}
\end{equation}
where \(\nabla_a\) is any derivative operator, \(A^a\) is an arbitrary smooth vector
field, \(B\) and \(C\) are arbitrary smooth functions, and \(g^{ab}\) is an arbitrary
smooth Lorentz metric such that the spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) is globally hyperbolic.
(A second order linear partial differential equation is said to be \emph{hyperbolic} if
and only if it can be expressed in the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.19}.) This equation will
have a well posed initial value formulation for initial data
\((\phi,n^a\nabla_a\phi)\) on any smooth, spacelike Cauchy surface \(\Sigma\), where
\(n^a\) is the unit normal to \(\Sigma\). There are only a few significant additional
complications to the proof of this much more general result. Specifically, for
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.19} in general we cannot construct a conserved \(T_{ab}\)
satisfying the dominant energy condition, but we may construct a \(T_{ab}\) satisfying
the dominant energy condition whose failure to be conserved can be bounded, so that
inequalities of the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.12} (with \(C_{1,k}\) and \(C_{2,k}\) now
functions of time) still hold. Also, in the proof of existence, if the coefficients
\(g^{ab},A^a,B\), and \(C\) are nonanalytic, we must also approximate them by
analytic coefficients before we can appeal to theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1}.

These results can be further generalized to systems of equations, resulting in the
following theorem, a complete proof of which can be found in Hawking and Ellis
(1973).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:10.1.2}
Let \((M,g_{ab})\) be a globally hyperbolic spacetime (or a globally hyperbolic region
of an arbitrary spacetime) and let \(\nabla_a\) be any derivative operator. Let
\(\Sigma\) be a smooth, spacelike Cauchy surface. Consider the system of \(n\) linear
equations for \(n\) unknown functions \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n\) of the form
\begin{equation}
  g^{ab}\nabla_a\nabla_b\phi_i
  +\sum_j(A_{ij})^a\nabla_a\phi_j
  +\sum_jB_{ij}\phi_j+C_i=0.
  \tag{10.1.20}\label{eq:10.1.20}
\end{equation}
(Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} is referred to as a linear, diagonal second order
hyperbolic system.) Then equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} has a well posed initial value
formulation on \(\Sigma\). More precisely, given arbitrary smooth initial data,
\((\phi_i,n^a\nabla_a\phi_i)\) for \(i=1,\ldots,n\) on \(\Sigma\), there exists a
unique solution of equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} throughout \(M\). Furthermore, the
solutions depend continuously on the initial data in the sense described above for the
Klein--Gordon equation in flat spacetime. Finally, a variation of the initial data
outside of a closed subset, \(S\), of \(\Sigma\) does not affect the solution in \(D(S)\).
\end{theorem}

We remark also that the differentiability assumptions on the coefficients
\(g^{ab},(A_{ij})^a\), and \(B_{ij}\), as well as on \(\Sigma\) and the initial data
\(\phi_i,n^a\nabla_a\phi_i\) on \(\Sigma\), can be weakened significantly (see
Hawking and Ellis 1973). For results on the initial value formulation of linear partial
differential equations not of second order diagonal form, see, e.g., Bers, John, and
Schechter (1964).

Finally we discuss generalizations of theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.2} to some nonlinear
systems of equations. Very few results other than theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.1} are known
concerning the initial value formulation of general, nonlinear systems of equations.
However, for second order differential equations which are \emph{quasilinear}, i.e.,
linear in the highest derivative terms, many of the results on linear systems apply
locally. More precisely, we call a system of \(n\) second-order partial differential
equations for \(n\) unknown functions \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n\) on a manifold \(M\)
a \emph{quasilinear, diagonal, second order hyperbolic system} if it can be put in the
form
\begin{equation}
  g^{ab}(x;\phi_j;\nabla_c\phi_j)\nabla_a\nabla_b\phi_i
  =F_i(x;\phi_j;\nabla_c\phi_j),
  \tag{10.1.21}\label{eq:10.1.21}
\end{equation}
where \(\nabla_a\) is any derivative operator, \(g^{ab}\) is a smooth Lorentz metric,
and each \(F_i\) is a smooth function of its variables. (Equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.21}
differs from Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} in that \(g^{ab}\) is now permitted to depend on
the unknown variables and their first derivatives, and \(F_i\) now may have nonlinear
dependence on these variables.) For equations of this type, we have the following
theorem due to Leray (1952):

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:10.1.3}
Let \((\phi_0)_1,\ldots,(\phi_0)_n\) be any solution of the quasilinear hyperbolic
system~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} on a manifold \(M\) and let
\((g_0)^{ab}=g^{ab}(x;(\phi_0)_j;\nabla_c(\phi_0)_j)\). Suppose
\((M,(g_0)_{ab})\) is globally hyperbolic (or, alternatively, consider a globally
hyperbolic region of this spacetime). Let \(\Sigma\) be a smooth spacelike Cauchy
surface for \((M,(g_0)_{ab})\). Then, the initial value formulation of
equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} is well posed on \(\Sigma\) in the following sense: For
initial data on \(\Sigma\) sufficiently close to the initial data for
\((\phi_0)_1,\ldots,(\phi_0)_n\), there exists an open neighborhood \(O\) of
\(\Sigma\) such that equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} has a solution,
\(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n\), in \(O\) and
\((O,g_{ab}(x;\phi_j;\nabla_c\phi_j))\) is globally hyperbolic. The solution is
unique in \(O\) and propagates causally in the sense that if the initial data for
\(\phi'_1,\ldots,\phi'_n\) agree with that of \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n\) on a subset,
\(S\), of \(\Sigma\), then the solutions agree on \(O\cap D^+(S)\). Finally, the
solutions depend continuously on the initial data in the sense described above for
the Klein--Gordon field.
\end{theorem}

The basic idea of the proof of theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} is to start by solving the
linear system of the form~\eqref{eq:10.1.20} obtained by replacing \(\phi_i\) and
\(\nabla_a\phi_i\) by \((\phi_0)_i\) and \(\nabla_a(\phi_0)_i\) in \(g^{ab}\) and
\(F_i\) in equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.21}. Then we obtain a solution \((\phi_1)_i\) by
theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.2}. We substitute this solution into the coefficients \(g^{ab}\)
and \(F_i\) and repeat the procedure. In this way, we obtain a sequence \((\phi_n)_i\)
of solutions to linear equations. For initial data sufficiently close to that for
\((\phi_0)_i\), it can be shown that this sequence converges in a neighborhood of
\(\Sigma\) and that the limit satisfies equation~\eqref{eq:10.1.21} and has the above
desired properties. The proof of this convergence and the other properties quoted in
theorem~\ref{thm:10.1.3} is outlined in Hawking and Ellis (1973).
