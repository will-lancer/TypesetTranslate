\subsection{Interior Solutions}\label{sec:6.2}

We turn our attention, now, to the static, spherically symmetric solutions of
Einstein's equation with a perfect fluid stress-energy tensor,
\begin{equation}
  T_{ab}=\rho u_a u_b+P(g_{ab}+u_a u_b).
  \tag{6.2.1}\label{eq:6.2.1}
\end{equation}
In order to be compatible with the static symmetry of the spacetime the fluid
4-velocity, \(u^a\), must point in the same direction as the static Killing vector
field, \(\xi^a\), i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  u^a=-(e_0)^a=-f^{1/2}(\dd t)^a,
  \tag{6.2.2}\label{eq:6.2.2}
\end{equation}
where \(f\) is the function appearing in the general metric form
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.5}. The solutions we seek describe the possible interior fluid
sources of the exterior Schwarzschild metric, and thus our investigation will
yield the equations of structure for static, fluid objects, such as stars.

Einstein's equation with a fluid present is obtained simply by adding the
appropriate stress-energy terms to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.1.35}--\eqref{eq:6.1.37}. We
shall take the three independent equations in the form
\begin{align}
  8\pi T_{00}=8\pi\rho=G_{00}
    &=R_{00}+\frac12(R_0{}^0+R_1{}^1+R_2{}^2+R_3{}^3)\nonumber\\
    &=(rh^2)^{-1}h'+r^{-2}(1-h^{-1}),
  &&\tag{6.2.3}\label{eq:6.2.3}\\
  8\pi T_{11}=8\pi P=G_{11}
    &=R_{11}-\frac12(R_0{}^0+R_1{}^1+R_2{}^2+R_3{}^3)\nonumber\\
    &=(rfh)^{-1}f'-r^{-2}(1-h^{-1}),
  &&\tag{6.2.4}\label{eq:6.2.4}\\
  8\pi T_{22}=8\pi P=G_{22}
    &=\frac12(fh)^{-1/2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[(fh)^{-1/2}f'\right]\nonumber\\
    &\quad+\frac12(rfh)^{-1}f'-\frac12(rh^2)^{-1}h'.
  &&\tag{6.2.5}\label{eq:6.2.5}
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:6.2.3} involves only \(h\). (This could have been predicted
from the general analysis of the structure of Einstein's equation given in
Chapter~10, which shows that the equation \(G_{00}=8\pi T_{00}\) is an initial
value constraint which, in the static case, involves only the geometry of the
spacelike hypersurface \(\Sigma\) orthogonal to \(\xi^a\) and thus cannot involve
\(f\).) It can be rewritten in the form
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[r(1-h^{-1})\right]=8\pi\rho,
  \tag{6.2.6}\label{eq:6.2.6}
\end{equation}
from which it follows immediately that the solution for \(h\) is
\begin{equation}
  h(r)=\left[1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right]^{-1},
  \tag{6.2.7}\label{eq:6.2.7}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  m(r)=4\pi\int_0^r\rho(r')r'^2\,\dd r'+a,
  \tag{6.2.8}\label{eq:6.2.8}
\end{equation}
where \(a\) is a constant. Smoothness of the metric on \(\Sigma\) at \(r=0\)
requires that as \(r\to0\) the area of spheres approach \(4\pi\) times the square
of their proper radius, i.e., that \(h(r)\to1\) as \(r\to0\). Thus, in order to
avoid a conical singularity in the metric at \(r=0\), we must set \(a=0\). Since
\(\Sigma\) must be spacelike for a static configuration, we see that a necessary
condition for staticity is \(h\geq0\), i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  r\geq2m(r).
  \tag{6.2.9}\label{eq:6.2.9}
\end{equation}
If \(\rho=0\) for \(r>R\), our solution for \(h\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.7}, joins on
to the vacuum Schwarzschild solution with total mass
\begin{equation}
  M=m(R)=4\pi\int_0^R\rho(r)r^2\,\dd r.
  \tag{6.2.10}\label{eq:6.2.10}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:6.2.10} is formally identical to the expression for total
mass in Newtonian gravity. Note, however, that this formal analogy is misleading
because the proper volume element on \(\Sigma\) (see appendix B) is
\(\sqrt{{}^{(3)}g}\,\dd^3x=h^{1/2}r^2\sin\theta\,\dd r\,\dd\theta\,\dd\phi\), so the total
\emph{proper mass} is
\begin{equation}
  M_p=4\pi\int_0^R\rho(r)r^2
  \left[1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right]^{-1/2}\,\dd r.
  \tag{6.2.11}\label{eq:6.2.11}
\end{equation}
The difference between \(M\) and \(M_p\) can be interpreted as the gravitational
binding energy, \(E_B\), of the configuration,
\begin{equation}
  E_B=M_p-M,
  \tag{6.2.12}\label{eq:6.2.12}
\end{equation}
which is always positive since \(M_p>M\).

If we write
\begin{equation}
  f=e^{2\phi},
  \tag{6.2.13}\label{eq:6.2.13}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.4} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\phi}{\dd r}=\frac{m(r)+4\pi r^3P}{r[r-2m(r)]}.
  \tag{6.2.14}\label{eq:6.2.14}
\end{equation}
In the Newtonian limit, we have \(r^3P\ll m(r)\) and \(m(r)\ll r\), so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.14} reduces to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\phi}{\dd r}\approx\frac{m(r)}{r^2},
  \tag{6.2.15}\label{eq:6.2.15}
\end{equation}
which is simply the spherically symmetric version of Poisson's equation for the
Newtonian gravitational potential. Thus, in the static spherically symmetric case
we may view \(\phi=\tfrac12\ln f\) as the general relativistic analog of the
% TRANSCRIPTION CORRECTION: the source reads ``nonstationary.''
Newtonian potential. For nonstationary configurations, however, there is no known
analog in general relativity of the Newtonian potential.

If we substitute our results, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.7} and \eqref{eq:6.2.14}, into
our final equation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.5}, it is apparent that we will obtain an
equation for \(\dd P/\dd r\). However, the rather messy algebra required for doing this
can be circumvented by noting that
\begin{align}
  (e_\mu)_b8\pi\nabla_aT^{ab}
    &=(e_\mu)_b\nabla_a(8\pi T^{ab}-G^{ab})\nonumber\\
    &=\nabla_a\left[(e_\mu)_b(8\pi T^{ab}-G^{ab})\right]
      -\omega_{ab\mu}(8\pi T^{ab}-G^{ab}),
  &&\tag{6.2.16}\label{eq:6.2.16}
\end{align}
where \(\omega_{ab\mu}=\nabla_a(e_\mu)_b\). Setting \(\mu=1\), we find the first
term on the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.16} vanishes if
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.4} is satisfied. Thus, since \(\omega_{221}\ne0\), we see that
(given Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.3} and \eqref{eq:6.2.4}) the vanishing of
\((8\pi T^{22}-G^{22})\) is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  (e_1)_b\nabla_aT^{ab}=0,
  \tag{6.2.17}\label{eq:6.2.17}
\end{equation}
and hence we may replace Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.5} by Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.17}. We have
already calculated this component of \(\nabla_aT^{ab}\) for a perfect fluid in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.8}, and thus, without further work, we obtain [using
\((e_1)_b=h^{1/2}(\dd r)_b=h^{-1/2}(\partial/\partial r)_b\)],
\begin{equation}
  h^{-1/2}\frac{\dd P}{\dd r}
  =-(P+\rho)(e_1)_bu^a\nabla_au^b
  =-h^{-1/2}(P+\rho)\frac{\dd\phi}{\dd r},
  \tag{6.2.18}\label{eq:6.2.18}
\end{equation}
where Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.2} and \eqref{eq:6.2.13} were used for the last
equality. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.14}, we may eliminate \(\dd\phi/\dd r\) to obtain
our final result,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd r}=-(P+\rho)\frac{m(r)+4\pi r^3P}{r[r-2m(r)]}.
  \tag{6.2.19}\label{eq:6.2.19}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:6.2.19} is known as the
\emph{Tolman--Oppenheimer--Volkoff equation} of hydrostatic equilibrium. In the
Newtonian limit (\(P\ll\rho\), \(m(r)\ll r\)) it reduces to the Newtonian
hydrostatic equilibrium equation,\footnote{It is interesting to note that
although general relativity has little effect on the equilibrium configurations
of stars with \(P\ll\rho\) and \(m(r)\ll r\), it still can have a significant
influence on the stability of stars with equation of state \(P=C\rho^\lambda\)
with \(\lambda\) near the critical value of \(4/3\); see Chandrasekhar (1964).}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P}{\dd r}\approx-\frac{\rho m(r)}{r^2}.
  \tag{6.2.20}\label{eq:6.2.20}
\end{equation}

In summary, we have found that the spacetime geometry inside a static, spherical
fluid star is
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2=-e^{2\phi}\dd t^2+
  \left(1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right)^{-1}\dd r^2+r^2\dd\Omega^2,
  \tag{6.2.21}\label{eq:6.2.21}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  m(r)=4\pi\int_0^r\rho(r')r'^2\,\dd r',
  \tag{6.2.22}\label{eq:6.2.22}
\end{equation}
and \(\phi\) is determined from Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.14}. The necessary and
sufficient condition for equilibrium is that Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.19} be
satisfied.

Thus, for fluid matter with a given equation of state, \(P=P(\rho)\), equilibrium
configurations can be determined as follows: We arbitrarily prescribe a central
density \(\rho_c\), and hence a central pressure \(P_c=P(\rho_c)\). Then we
integrate Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.19} and \eqref{eq:6.2.22} outward until we reach the
surface of the star, \(P=\rho=0\), at which point we join the solution onto the
vacuum Schwarzschild solution, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.44}. Finally, we solve for
\(\phi\) by integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.14}, subject to the boundary condition
\(\phi\to0\) as \(r\to\infty\) (or, equivalently, by requiring \(\phi\) to match
onto the Schwarzschild value at the surface of the star). This procedure differs
from that used in Newtonian theory only in that Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.19} has replaced
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.20}, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.14} has replaced
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.15}.

The most important difference between equilibrium configurations in general
relativity and Newtonian gravity can be seen from the fact that (assuming
\(P\geq0\)) for a given density profile \(\rho(r)\geq0\), the right-hand side of
the relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.19} is always larger
in magnitude than the right-hand side of the Newtonian Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.20}.
This means that for a given \(\rho(r)\), the central pressure, \(P_c\), required
for equilibrium is always higher in general relativity than in Newtonian theory;
i.e., it is harder to maintain equilibrium in general relativity. This is
dramatically illustrated by consideration of uniform density configurations,
corresponding to an incompressible fluid of density \(\rho_0\),
\begin{equation}
  \rho(r)=
  \begin{cases}
    \rho_0 & (r\leq R),\\
    0 & (r>R),
  \end{cases}
  \tag{6.2.23}\label{eq:6.2.23}
\end{equation}
and hence (in both general relativity and Newtonian theory)
\begin{equation}
  m(r)=\frac43\pi r^3\rho_0\qquad(r\leq R).
  \tag{6.2.24}\label{eq:6.2.24}
\end{equation}

The Newtonian equation of hydrostatic equilibrium, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.20}, is
easily integrated to yield (for \(r\leq R\)),
\begin{equation}
  P(r)=\frac23\pi\rho_0^2(R^2-r^2),
  \tag{6.2.25}\label{eq:6.2.25}
\end{equation}
where the boundary condition \(P(R)=0\) has been imposed. Thus, the central
pressure of a Newtonian uniform density star is
\begin{equation}
  P_c=\frac23\pi\rho_0^2R^2
  =\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)^{1/3}M^{2/3}\rho_0^{4/3},
  \tag{6.2.26}\label{eq:6.2.26}
\end{equation}
which is finite for all values of \(\rho_0\) and \(R\), i.e., equilibrium can be
achieved with sufficiently large pressures for all \(\rho_0\) and \(R\). On the
other hand, the general relativistic equation of hydrostatic equilibrium,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.19}, also can be integrated exactly, as was first done by
Schwarzschild (1916b), yielding
\begin{equation}
  P(r)=\rho_0
  \left[
    \frac{(1-2Mr^2/R^3)^{1/2}-(1-2M/R)^{1/2}}
      {3(1-2M/R)^{1/2}-(1-2Mr^2/R^3)^{1/2}}
  \right].
  \tag{6.2.27}\label{eq:6.2.27}
\end{equation}
Thus, the central pressure required for equilibrium of a uniform density star in
general relativity is
\begin{equation}
  P_c=\rho_0
  \left[
    \frac{1-(1-2M/R)^{1/2}}{3(1-2M/R)^{1/2}-1}
  \right].
  \tag{6.2.28}\label{eq:6.2.28}
\end{equation}
For \(R\gg M\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.28} reduces to the Newtonian value,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.26}. However, now \(P_c\) becomes infinite when
\begin{equation}
  3(1-2M/R)^{1/2}=1,
  \tag{6.2.29}\label{eq:6.2.29}
\end{equation}
i.e., when
\begin{equation}
  R=\frac94M.
  \tag{6.2.30}\label{eq:6.2.30}
\end{equation}
Thus, in general relativity, uniform density stars with \(M>4R/9\) simply cannot
exist. Another way of stating this result is that the maximum possible mass of a
star of uniform density \(\rho_0\) is
\begin{equation}
  M_{\max}=\frac{4}{9(3\pi)^{1/2}\rho_0^{1/2}}.
  \tag{6.2.31}\label{eq:6.2.31}
\end{equation}

The existence of upper mass limits in general relativity is not an artifact of
having restricted consideration to stars of uniform density. In fact, if we
assume\footnote{In fact, the assumption that \(\rho\geq0\) follows from the
monotone decrease assumption, \(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\), since the interior solution must
eventually match onto the exterior Schwarzschild solution with \(\rho=0\).
Conversely, the assumption that \(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\) follows from \(\rho\geq0\) if we
assume, in addition, that the fluid has an equation of state,
\(P=P(\rho)\), with \(P\geq0\) and \(\dd P/\dd\rho\geq0\), since
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.19} implies \(\dd P/\dd r\leq0\).} only that the density,
\(\rho(r)\), is nonnegative and is a monotone decreasing function of \(r\),
\(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\), then we can derive the following two types of upper mass
limits for static, spherical stars in general relativity: (i) For stars of fixed
radius \(R\), the maximum possible mass is given by the uniform density value,
\(M_{\max}=4R/9\). (ii) For a fixed equation of state below a density \(\rho_0\)
(which is physically realistic at low densities), an upper mass limit exists
independent of the equation of state at higher densities than \(\rho_0\). (The
value of this upper mass limit depends, of course, on the value of \(\rho_0\) and
the equation of state assumed to below \(\rho_0\).) We now derive the upper mass
limit (i), and then indicate how the upper mass limit (ii) is obtained.

The existence of an upper mass limit at fixed star radius \(R\) already follows
from the condition \(h\geq0\), which, as seen from Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.9}, implies
that a necessary condition for staticity is
\begin{equation}
  M\leq R/2.
  \tag{6.2.32}\label{eq:6.2.32}
\end{equation}
We can sharpen this limit to \(M\leq4R/9\) using the condition \(f\geq0\) which
states that the Killing field \(\xi^a\) is timelike everywhere. To do
so---assuming only that \(\rho\geq0\) and \(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\) but without any
assumptions whatsoever about \(P\)---we must examine the two independent
equations which do not involve \(P\) from the basic set
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.3}--\eqref{eq:6.2.5}. We already have solved
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.3} for \(h\), so the remaining equation is obtained by taking
the difference of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.4} and \eqref{eq:6.2.5},
\begin{align}
  0=G_{11}-G_{22}
    &=\frac12(rfh)^{-1}f'-r^{-2}(1-h^{-1})+\frac12(rh^2)^{-1}h'\nonumber\\
    &\quad-\frac12(fh)^{-1/2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[(fh)^{-1/2}f'\right].
  &&\tag{6.2.33}\label{eq:6.2.33}
\end{align}
Substituting our solution for \(h\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.7}, in the second and
third terms, and performing a few algebraic manipulations, we find
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[r^{-1}h^{-1/2}\frac{\dd f^{1/2}}{\dd r}\right]
  =(fh)^{1/2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[\frac{m(r)}{r^3}\right].
  \tag{6.2.34}\label{eq:6.2.34}
\end{equation}
Since \(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\), the average density, which is proportional to
\(m(r)/r^3\), also must decrease monotonically with \(r\), so we have
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left[r^{-1}h^{-1/2}\frac{\dd f^{1/2}}{\dd r}\right]\leq0.
  \tag{6.2.35}\label{eq:6.2.35}
\end{equation}
Integrating this inequality \emph{inward} from the surface, \(R\), of the star to
radius \(r\), we obtain
\begin{align}
  \frac{1}{rh^{1/2}(r)}\frac{\dd f^{1/2}}{\dd r}(r)
    &\geq\frac{1}{Rh^{1/2}(R)}\frac{\dd f^{1/2}}{\dd r}(R)\nonumber\\
    &=\frac{(1-2M/R)^{1/2}}{R}
      \left.\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}\right|_{r=R}
      =\frac{M}{R^3},
  &&\tag{6.2.36}\label{eq:6.2.36}
\end{align}
where we have used the fact that our interior solution must join on
smoothly\footnote{As long as \(\rho\) and \(P\) are continuous at \(r=R\),
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.3}--\eqref{eq:6.2.5} imply that \(h\) and \(f\) are at least
\(C^1\) there.} to the Schwarzschild solution, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.44}, and
hence we have calculated the derivative of \(f^{1/2}\) at \(r=R\) using the
exterior Schwarzschild metric. Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.36} by
\(rh^{1/2}\) and integrating inward again from \(R\) to \(0\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
  f^{1/2}(0)\leq(1-2M/R)^{1/2}
    -\frac{M}{R^3}\int_0^R
      \left[1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right]^{-1/2}r\,\dd r.
  \tag{6.2.37}\label{eq:6.2.37}
\end{equation}
Here, again, we have used \(f^{1/2}(R)=(1-2M/R)^{1/2}\) and we have also used
the explicit solution, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.7}, for \(h\). Now, the condition
\(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\) implies that \(m(r)\) cannot be smaller than the value it would
have for a uniform density star (\(\dd\rho/\dd r=0\)),
\begin{equation}
  m(r)\geq Mr^3/R^3.
  \tag{6.2.38}\label{eq:6.2.38}
\end{equation}
Hence, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.37} will be
smallest in magnitude when equality holds in Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.38}. Thus, we
obtain
\begin{align}
  f^{1/2}(0)
    &\leq(1-2M/R)^{1/2}
       -\frac{M}{R^3}\int_0^R
         \left(1-\frac{2Mr^2}{R^3}\right)^{-1/2}r\,\dd r\nonumber\\
    &=\frac32(1-2M/R)^{1/2}-\frac12.
  &&\tag{6.2.39}\label{eq:6.2.39}
\end{align}
Thus, the condition \(f^{1/2}(0)\geq0\) implies that a necessary condition for
staticity is
\begin{equation}
  (1-2M/R)^{1/2}\geq\frac13,
  \tag{6.2.40}\label{eq:6.2.40}
\end{equation}
i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  M\leq4R/9,
  \tag{6.2.41}\label{eq:6.2.41}
\end{equation}
which is the desired result. Again, we emphasize that absolutely no assumptions
concerning the pressure, \(P\), of the star entered into the derivation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.41}.

Given the existence of an upper mass limit at fixed radius, the existence of an
upper mass limit for a given equation of state below density \(\rho_0\) (assuming
this equation of state is physically reasonable and that \(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\))
should not be surprising. If the equation of state does not become very stiff,
i.e., large \(P\), at low densities (which is what we mean by physically
reasonable), an upper mass limit will exist for stars whose density is everywhere
less than \(\rho_0\). Since \(\dd\rho/\dd r\leq0\), stars whose density fails to be
less than \(\rho_0\) must consist of a core of mass \(m_0\) and radius \(r_0\)
where \(\rho\geq\rho_0\), surrounded by an envelope where \(\rho<\rho_0\). Given
the equation of state for \(\rho<\rho_0\), the total mass, \(M\), is determined by
the parameters \(m_0\) and \(r_0\). The function \(M(m_0,r_0)\) will be continuous
in \(m_0\) and \(r_0\). However, since the core density is at least \(\rho_0\)
everywhere, we have the lower mass limit for the core,
\begin{equation}
  m_0\geq\frac43\pi r_0^3\rho_0.
  \tag{6.2.42}\label{eq:6.2.42}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, from the above upper total mass limit,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.41}, we would expect an upper limit on the core mass \(m_0\)
for a given core radius \(r_0\). Indeed, the same derivation as led to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.41}---except that we use the core radius, \(r_0\), rather than
the surface of the star as the boundary, and we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.14} rather
than matching to the Schwarzschild solution to evaluate \(\dd f^{1/2}/\dd r\) at
\(r_0\)---yields the more stringent limit,
\begin{equation}
  m_0\leq\frac29r_0\left[1-6\pi r_0^2P_0+
      (1+6\pi r_0^2P_0)^{1/2}\right],
  \tag{6.2.43}\label{eq:6.2.43}
\end{equation}
where \(P_0=P(\rho_0)\) is the pressure at the core-envelope boundary. But
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.42} and \eqref{eq:6.2.43} restrict \(m_0\) and \(r_0\) to a
compact region of the \(m_0\)-\(r_0\) plane. Thus, \(M(m_0,r_0)\) is a continuous
function defined on a compact set. Therefore, \(M\) is bounded!

It should be pointed out that upper mass limits for a given equation of state at
all densities occur in Newtonian theory also. The important difference which
occurs in general relativity is that one obtains a limit which is independent of
the equation of state at sufficiently high densities. Since there is unavoidable
uncertainty in our knowledge of the physically correct equation of state at very
high densities, this independence is essential to a derivation of a firm upper mass
limit.

For cold matter, the dominant contribution to the pressure at densities much less
than nuclear density (\(\sim10^{14}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)) arises from electron
degeneracy pressure. At low densities
\(n\ll m_e^3c^3/\hbar^3\sim10^{31}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\), where \(n\) is the
number density of electrons and \(m_e\) is the mass of the electron, this source
provides a pressure at \(T=0\) of
\begin{equation}
  P=\frac{\hbar^2(3\pi^2)^{2/3}}{5m_e}n^{5/3},
  \tag{6.2.44}\label{eq:6.2.44}
\end{equation}
whereas at high densities \(n\gg m_e^3c^3/\hbar^3\) we have
\begin{equation}
  P=\frac{\hbar c(3\pi^2)^{1/3}}{4}n^{4/3}.
  \tag{6.2.45}\label{eq:6.2.45}
\end{equation}
At densities approaching nuclear density, cold matter will be mainly in the form
of free neutrons, and a similar degeneracy pressure due to neutrons becomes
important. The precise form obtained for the equation of state depends, of course,
on assumptions concerning the form of matter at the density under consideration
and the microscopic forces acting between the fundamental constituents. While
these factors are well understood at low densities, significant uncertainties arise
at nuclear density and above.

The mass, \(M\), and radius, \(R\), of equilibrium configurations of cold matter
for central densities between \(\sim10^5\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) and
\(\sim10^{17}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:6.1}. Near and
above nuclear density, Figure~\ref{fig:6.1} is only a sketch of the qualitative
features found for physically reasonable equations of state, since the precise
values of \(M\) and \(R\) in this region depend upon many assumptions. (For a
tabulation of exact values in the nuclear density regime for a wide variety of
equations of state, see Arnett and Bowers 1977.) Even well below nuclear density
the predicted equilibrium configurations depend somewhat upon assumptions
involving the composition of the star. Cold bodies on the segment AB of the curve
comprise stable equilibrium configurations supported by electron degeneracy
pressure. These bodies are known as \emph{white dwarfs}. As first shown by
Chandrasekhar (see Chandrasekhar 1939), the maximum mass of a white dwarf is
given by\footnote{We can see why an upper mass limit of this magnitude should
exist by comparing the central pressure needed to hold up a uniform density
Newtonian star, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.26}, with the high density limit of the
electron degeneracy pressure, Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.45}. Taking
\(\rho=\mu_Nm_Nn\), where \(m_N\) denotes the mass of a nucleon, equating the two
pressures, and restoring \(G\) and \(c\), we obtain the order of magnitude
estimate \(M_c\sim(\hbar c/G)^{3/2}(\mu_N^2m_N^2)^{-1}\sim M_\odot/\mu_N^2\).}

\begin{equation}
  M_c\approx1.4\left(\frac{2}{\mu_N}\right)^2M_\odot.
  \tag{6.2.46}\label{eq:6.2.46}
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_N\) is the number of nucleons per electron and \(M_\odot\) denotes
the mass of the Sun (\(\sim2\times10^{33}\,\mathrm{g}\)). In Newtonian theory
with a fixed value of \(\mu_N\), the mass monotonically increases with central
density, approaching the limiting mass \eqref{eq:6.2.46} for a configuration with
\(\rho_c\to\infty\) at radius \(R\to0\). In general relativity with a fixed value
of \(\mu_N\), there is a turnover in the curve, i.e., the mass begins to decrease
with \(\rho_c\), at a finite value of \(\rho_c\) (Chandrasekhar and Tooper 1964),
although the value of \(M_c\) is not significantly changed. However, the turnover
at point B shown in Figure~\ref{fig:6.1} is due to the increase in \(\mu_N\) at
high density caused by the conversion of protons and electrons to neutrons.
General arguments (see Sorkin 1981) show that an instability sets in at the
turnover point B of the curve, making the configurations on the segment BC
unstable. However, configurations on the segment CD are stable. Such bodies,
called \emph{neutron stars}, are composed mainly of neutrons and are supported
largely by neutron degeneracy pressure. Since the density of matter in a neutron
star is comparable to that of atomic nuclei, a neutron star is much like a huge,
highly neutron-enriched nucleus (with atomic weight \(\sim10^{57}\)) except that
it is bound together by gravitation rather than by nuclear forces. On account of
uncertainties in the equation of state, the value of the maximum possible mass,
\(M(D)\), of a neutron star is considerably more uncertain than the white dwarf
limit \eqref{eq:6.2.46}. However, most physically reasonable equations of state
yield \(M(D)\) below \(2M_\odot\). Beyond point D on the curve, the
configurations again become unstable.

If one applies the above upper mass limit argument to the equation of state for
cold matter, choosing \(\rho_0\) of the order of nuclear densities (so that there
are few uncertainties in the equation of state below \(\rho_0\)), a firm upper
mass limit of \(\sim5M_\odot\) is obtained (see Hartle 1978). If the general
features of the equation of state are trusted somewhat beyond nuclear density, the
above limit of \(\sim2M_\odot\) appears to be a realistic upper limit to the
maximum possible mass of a spherical body composed of cold matter. We should
emphasize, however, that our analysis applies only to the spherical case, and
thus, for example, these upper mass limits need not hold for rapidly rotating
bodies.

The existence of an upper mass limit for cold matter has extremely important
consequences for the ultimate fate of stars. Ordinary stars are supported against
collapse under their own weight by ideal gas pressure resulting from high
temperature. This pressure is much higher than the pressure that could be produced
by cold matter at comparable densities, and the above mentioned upper mass limits
for cold stars do not apply. However, a hot star radiates energy from its surface,
and unless this energy is replenished, hydrostatic equilibrium cannot be
maintained. If stars had no source of energy, they would contract---very slowly at
first, but eventually on dynamic time scales when instabilities set in---until such
high densities were reached that the cold matter pressure would dominate the
thermal contribution to the pressure. At that point, if the mass of the star were
sufficiently small that stable equilibrium could be achieved with this cold matter
pressure, the star would simply cool down, remaining in static equilibrium forever.
On the other hand, if the mass of the star were greater than the cold matter upper
mass limit, equilibrium never could be achieved, and the star would have to undergo
complete gravitational collapse.

In fact, stars, of course, do have an internal source of energy: nuclear
reactions. Consequently, they are able to stave off---typically for billions of
years, though for much less time for very massive stars---their ultimate fate of
either support by cold matter pressure or complete collapse. However, this does
not alter the fundamental conclusion that stars with mass greater than the cold
matter upper mass limit must eventually undergo complete gravitational collapse,
unless, of course, they shed enough mass during the course of stellar evolution to
fall below this limit.

When a star forms by condensation of a gas cloud, it contracts and heats up until
the central temperature and density are sufficiently high that nuclear reactions
converting hydrogen to helium occur there. The collapse of the star is then halted,
and a long lasting equilibrium is maintained until finally a large core of helium
is built up. If the star is sufficiently massive, contraction of this core then
will take place until helium reactions begin to occur, resulting in the formation
of heavier elements. This process may then repeat itself until a large core of the
most stable nuclei, nickel and iron, is synthesized.

The crucial issue governing how far along this evolutionary sequence a star
proceeds is whether the electron degeneracy pressure at any stage becomes
sufficient to support the star. If the mass of the star is greater than \(M_c\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.2.46}, electron degeneracy pressure cannot support the star, and
the large nickel and iron core will be built up (unless instabilities caused by
explosive nuclear burning occur prior to this stage and literally blow the star
apart). However, if the mass of the star is less than \(M_c\), the contraction will
be permanently halted at a prior stage. No further nuclear reactions will occur,
and the star will simply cool down forever in a stable, white dwarf configuration.

If \(M\) is greater than \(M_c\), then after a core of nickel and iron of mass
\(\sim M_c\) is built up, this core simply will not be able to support itself:
Electron degeneracy pressure is not sufficient, and no further energy generating
nuclear reactions can occur. Hence, the core will undergo gravitational collapse.
By the time the density of the collapsing core has reached nuclear densities,
neutron degeneracy pressure and nuclear forces provide a significant cold matter
pressure. If the total mass of the collapsing part of the star is below the upper
mass limit for cold matter (\(\sim2M_\odot\)), the collapse may be halted,
resulting in a neutron star. Confirmation of the existence of neutron stars has
been provided by the discovery of pulsars---the astronomically observed objects
which emit exactly reproduced signals with periods of fractions of a second. The
only viable theoretical explanation of pulsars is that they are neutron stars
which possess a hot spot rotating at the signal period which becomes oriented
toward us during each revolution.

When the collapse of the core is halted or slowed down at nuclear densities, a
shock wave will be produced and will propagate outward into the envelope of the
star. It appears likely that this shock wave is responsible, in many cases, for
blowing off the outer envelope of the star with an enormous release of energy, thus
producing a \emph{supernova}. The details of exactly how this occurs are, however,
not well understood at present. Nevertheless, the discovery of pulsars at the
sites of the Crab and Vela supernova remnants has provided strong observational
confirmation of the picture that supernovae are produced in conjunction with the
collapse of the core of a star at the endpoint of stellar evolution.

Thus, in summary, if the mass of a star is sufficiently small, it can attain final
equilibrium as a white dwarf or a neutron star. However, if the mass of the
collapsing portion of a star is greater than the cold matter upper mass limit,
equilibrium can never be achieved, and complete gravitational collapse will occur.
As discussed at the end of Section~\ref{sec:6.4}, the endpoint of such a collapse
in the spherical case will be a Schwarzschild black hole.
