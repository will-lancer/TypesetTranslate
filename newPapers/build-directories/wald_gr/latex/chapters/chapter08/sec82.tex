\subsection{Causality Conditions}\label{sec:8.2}
\setcounter{theorem}{0}

In this section, we shall give a brief discussion of formulations of the notion that
a spacetime be \lq\lq causally well behaved.\rq\rq\ Although according to
theorem~\ref{thm:8.1.2} all spacetimes in general relativity \emph{locally} have the
same qualitative causal structure as in special relativity, globally very significant
differences can occur. For example, we can construct a (flat) spacetime with topology
\(S^1\times\mathbb{R}^3\) by identifying the \(t=0\) and \(t=1\) hyperplanes of
Minkowski spacetime, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:8.6}. The integral curves of
\((\partial/\partial t)^a\) in this spacetime will be closed timelike curves, and it
is not difficult to see that for all \(p\in M\) we have \(I^+(p)=I^-(p)=M\). Thus,
in this spacetime an observer with \lq\lq free will\rq\rq\ should have no difficulty
altering past events.

\input{figures/chapter08/fig86.tex}

In addition, in spacetimes with nontrivial closed causal curves [i.e., closed causal
curves other than the trivial curve \(\lambda(t)=p\) for all \(t\)], severe consistency
conditions may exist on solutions of the equations describing the propagation of
physical fields. Note that the existence of spacetimes with closed causal curves
cannot be blamed entirely on \lq\lq artificial\rq\rq\ topological identifications such as in
Fig.~\ref{fig:8.6}, since examples with topology \(\mathbb{R}^4\) can be constructed
easily by \lq\lq twisting\rq\rq\ the light cones, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:8.7}.

\input{figures/chapter08/fig87.tex}

It is generally believed that spacetimes with nontrivial closed causal curves are not
physically realistic. However, even if a spacetime does not possess closed causal
curves, it can be \lq\lq on the verge\rq\rq\ of violating causality as illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:8.8}. In this example, there exist causal curves which come
\lq\lq arbitrarily close\rq\rq\ to intersecting themselves, although none of them actually
do. Since an arbitrarily small perturbation of the metric in spacetimes such as this
would produce causality violation, these spacetimes also seem physically unreasonable.
The mathematical possibility of spacetimes such as Fig.~\ref{fig:8.8} frequently
arises in proofs of theorems concerning the possible global structures of spacetimes,
so it is very useful to formulate precise conditions which characterize this type of
behavior.

\input{figures/chapter08/fig88.tex}

One such characterization is the strong causality condition. A spacetime
\((M,g_{ab})\) is said to be \emph{strongly causal} if for all \(p\in M\) and every
neighborhood \(O\) of \(p\), there exists a neighborhood \(V\) of \(p\) contained in
\(O\) such that no causal curve intersects \(V\) more than once. Thus, if a spacetime
violates strong causality at \(p\), then near \(p\) there exist causal curves which
come arbitrarily close to intersecting themselves. In such a spacetime closed causal
curves could be produced by a small modification of \(g_{ab}\) in an arbitrarily small
neighborhood of \(p\). In the example of Fig.~\ref{fig:8.8}, strong causality is
violated at the event labeled \(p\).

A useful consequence of strong causality is expressed by the following lemma:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:8.2.1}
Let \((M,g_{ab})\) be strongly causal and let \(K\subseteq M\) be compact. Then
every causal curve \(\lambda\) confined within \(K\) must have past and future
endpoints in \(K\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Without loss of generality, we may assume that the curve parameter, \(t\), of
\(\lambda\) runs from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\). Let \(\{t_i\}\) be an increasing
sequence of numbers which diverges to infinity, and let \(p_i=\lambda(t_i)\). Since
\(\{p_i\}\) is a sequence in \(K\), by theorem A.9 of appendix A it has an
accumulation point \(p\in K\). Suppose one could find an open neighborhood \(O\) of
\(p\) such that no \(t_0\in\mathbb{R}\) exists for which \(\lambda(t)\in O\) for all
\(t>t_0\). Then the same must hold for every open neighborhood \(V\subseteq O\).
This means that \(\lambda\) enters every such \(V\) more than once, since infinitely
many points of the sequence \(\{\lambda(t_i)\}\) enter \(V\) but \(\lambda(t)\) never
remains in \(V\). This contradicts the hypothesis that strong causality holds at
\(p\). Thus, \(p\) is a future endpoint of \(\lambda\). Similarly, a past endpoint
\(q\in K\) of \(\lambda\) also exists.
\end{proof}

Although the imposition of the strong causality condition suffices to rule out the
example of Fig.~\ref{fig:8.8}, one can construct more complicated examples where
strong causality is satisfied, but a modification of \(g_{ab}\) in an arbitrarily small
neighborhood of two or more points produces closed causal curves. Thus, strong
causality does not fully express the condition that one is not on the verge of producing
causality violation. However, this condition is expressed satisfactorily by the
stronger notion of stable causality, defined as follows.

Let \(t^a\) be a timelike vector at point \(p\in M\), and define \(\widetilde g_{ab}\)
at \(p\) by
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde g_{ab}=g_{ab}-t_at_b,
  \tag{8.2.1}\label{eq:8.2.1}
\end{equation}
where \(g_{ab}\) is the spacetime metric. It is easy to see that \(\widetilde g_{ab}\)
is also a Lorentz signature metric at \(p\). Furthermore, the light cone of
\(\widetilde g_{ab}\) is strictly larger than that of \(g_{ab}\); i.e., every timelike
and null vector of \(g_{ab}\) is a timelike vector of \(\widetilde g_{ab}\). If a
spacetime were \lq\lq on the verge\rq\rq\ of having closed causal curves, then if we
\lq\lq open out\rq\rq\ the light cone at each point, we should be able to produce closed
timelike curves. Thus, we define a spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) to be \emph{stably
causal} if there exists a continuous nonvanishing timelike vector field \(t^a\) such
that the spacetime \((M,\widetilde g_{ab})\) (where \(\widetilde g_{ab}\) is defined
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.2.1}) possesses no closed timelike curves. The following theorem
shows that stable causality is equivalent to the existence of a \lq\lq global time
function\rq\rq\ on the spacetime. This greatly strengthens the above suggestion that
the requirement of stable causality should suffice to rule out any causal pathologies.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:8.2.2}
A spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\) is stably causal if and only if there exists a
differentiable function \(f\) on \(M\) such that \(\nabla^af\) is a past directed
timelike vector field.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \lq\lq if.\rq\rq]
Since \(\nabla^af\) is a past directed timelike vector field, along every future
directed timelike curve with tangent \(v^a\), we have
\(g_{ab}v^a\nabla^bf>0\), and thus \(v(f)>0\). Hence, \(f\) strictly increases
along every future directed timelike curve. Clearly, then, there can be no closed
timelike curves in \((M,g_{ab})\) since \(f\) cannot return to its initial value.
Now let \(t^a=\nabla^af\) and define the metric \(\widetilde g_{ab}\) by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.2.1}. It is easy to check that the inverse metric to
\(\widetilde g_{ab}\) is
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde g^{ab}=g^{ab}+\frac{t^at^b}{1-t^ct_c}.
  \tag{8.2.2}\label{eq:8.2.2}
\end{equation}
Thus, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde g^{ab}\nabla_af\nabla_bf
    =t_at^a+\frac{(t^at_a)^2}{1-t^ct_c}
    =\frac{t_at^a}{1-t^ct_c}<0,
  \tag{8.2.3}\label{eq:8.2.3}
\end{equation}
where all index raisings and lowerings on the right-hand sides of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:8.2.2} and \eqref{eq:8.2.3} are done with \(g_{ab}\) and \(g^{ab}\).
Hence \(\widetilde g^{ab}\nabla_bf\) is a timelike vector in the metric
\(\widetilde g_{ab}\). By the same argument as already given above, it follows that
no closed timelike curves exist in the spacetime \((M,\widetilde g_{ab})\). Thus,
\((M,g_{ab})\) is stably causal.
\par\medskip
\noindent\emph{Sketch of proof of \lq\lq only if.\rq\rq}
We are given that \((M,g_{ab})\) is stably causal and wish to construct a global
time function. A promising candidate can be obtained as follows: Using the
paracompactness of \(M\), it is possible to show by arguments similar to those
discussed at the end of appendix A, that one can always define a continuous volume
measure, \(\mu\), on \(M\) such that the total volume of \(M\) is finite,
\(\mu(M)<\infty\). We define
\begin{equation}
  F(p)=\mu(I^-(p)).
  \tag{8.2.4}\label{eq:8.2.4}
\end{equation}
Then \(F\) strictly increases along all future directed causal curves (with
nonvanishing tangent), and thus is a promising candidate for a global time function.
Unfortunately, \(F\) need not be continuous. Nevertheless, for stably causal
spacetimes we can obtain a continuous function with these properties by averaging
\(F\) over nearby spacetimes with \lq\lq opened out\rq\rq\ light cones. More precisely,
let \(t^a\) be a timelike vector field such that \(\widetilde g_{ab}\), defined by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:8.2.1}, has no closed timelike curves. For \(0\leq\alpha\leq1\) we
define \((g_\alpha)_{ab}\) by
\begin{equation}
  (g_\alpha)_{ab}=g_{ab}-\alpha t_at_b
  \tag{8.2.5}\label{eq:8.2.5}
\end{equation}
and define \(F_\alpha(p)\) by
\begin{equation}
  F_\alpha(p)=\mu(I_\alpha^-(p)),
  \tag{8.2.6}\label{eq:8.2.6}
\end{equation}
where \(I_\alpha^-(p)\) denotes the chronological past of \(p\) in the metric
\((g_\alpha)_{ab}\). By averaging \(F_\alpha\) over \(\alpha\), it can be shown that
we produce a continuous function which strictly increases along causal curves.
Further \lq\lq smoothing out\rq\rq\ of this function produces a differentiable function
with these properties. Details of this proof can be found in Hawking and Ellis
(1973).
\end{proof}

As a corollary, we have:

\medskip
\noindent\textsc{Corollary.}\quad Stable causality implies strong causality.
\medskip
\begin{proof}
Let \(f\) be a global time function on \(M\). Given any \(p\in M\) and any open
neighborhood \(O\) of \(p\), we can choose an open neighborhood \(V\subseteq O\)
of \(p\) shaped so that the limiting value of \(f\) along every future directed
causal curve leaving \(V\) is greater than the limiting value of \(f\) on every
future directed causal curve entering \(V\) (see Fig.~\ref{fig:8.9}). Thus, since
\(f\) increases along every future directed causal curve, no causal curve can enter
\(V\) twice.
\end{proof}

In conclusion, stable causality appears to be the appropriate notion which expresses
the idea that a spacetime is not \lq\lq on the verge\rq\rq\ of displaying bad causal
behavior.
