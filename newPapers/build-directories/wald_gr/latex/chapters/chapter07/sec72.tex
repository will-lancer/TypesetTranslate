\subsection{Spatially Homogeneous Cosmologies}\label{sec:7.2}

In chapter 5 we studied in detail the solutions of Einstein's equation which are
spatially homogeneous and isotropic. These models successfully account for many
of the observed properties of our universe. However, our universe certainly is not
\emph{exactly} homogeneous and isotropic, and one would like to have a better
understanding of the possible dynamical behavior of the universe in the absence of
these symmetries. We already showed in chapter 5 that spatial isotropy at each point
implies spatial homogeneity, so the simplest first step toward generalizing the
Robertson--Walker models would be to obtain the spatially homogeneous but
anisotropic solutions. We would expect this to be a tractable problem, since on
account of the spatial symmetry, only the time variations should be nontrivial. Thus,
Einstein's equation should reduce to a system of ordinary differential equations. The
main purpose of this section is to demonstrate that this is indeed the case by outlining
the derivation of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41}--\eqref{eq:7.2.43} below. The solutions
to these equations provide interesting models for the possible behavior of the
universe near an initial singularity (Collins and Ellis 1979).

We already defined a spatially homogeneous spacetime at the beginning of chapter 5
as one which possesses a group of isometries whose orbits are spacelike
hypersurfaces which foliate (i.e., pass through every point of) the spacetime. Our first
task is to define more precisely the nature of the group of isometries we seek and to
develop some important properties of such groups. The notion of a Lie group which
we are about to define generalizes to “\(m\) parameters” the notion of a
one-parameter group of transformations discussed in section 2.2.

First, we remind the reader that a group \(\mathcal G\) is simply a set together with
a map \(\mathcal G\times\mathcal G\longrightarrow\mathcal G\) (called
“multiplication”) and a preferred element \(e\) (called the identity element) such
that: (1) the multiplication law is associative,
\(g_1(g_2g_3)=(g_1g_2)g_3\); (2) for all \(g\in\mathcal G\), we have
\(eg=ge=g\); and (3) for every \(g\in\mathcal G\) there is an element of
\(\mathcal G\)---denoted \(g^{-1}\) and called the inverse of \(g\)---such that
\(gg^{-1}=g^{-1}g=e\).

Many sets consisting of a finite number of elements provide examples of groups. A
good example of a group with infinitely many elements is the collection of
diffeomorphisms of a manifold \(M\) with “multiplication” given by composition,
\(\psi\phi=\psi\circ\phi\), and \(e\) taken as the identity map,
\(e(p)=p\) for all \(p\in M\). Similarly, the collection of isometries of a
manifold \(M\) with metric \(g_{ab}\) also forms a group (which is a subgroup of the
group of diffeomorphisms), since the composition of two isometries is an isometry,
and the inverse of any isometry is an isometry.

A \emph{Lie group}, \(G\), of dimension \(m\) is a group which is also an
\(m\)-dimensional manifold such that the inverse map \(i(g)=g^{-1}\) and the
multiplication map \(f(g_1,g_2)=g_1g_2\) are smooth
\(\bigl(C^\infty\bigr)\). Thus, the elements of a Lie group can be characterized
locally by \(m\) parameters, and the multiplication and inverse operations depend
smoothly on these parameters. The group of diffeomorphisms of a manifold \(M\)
does not yield a (finite-dimensional) Lie group, since this group is “too big” to be
characterized by \(m\) parameters. However, it is possible to show that the group of
isometries always yields a (possibly zero-dimensional and possibly disconnected)
Lie group. In fact, an argument given in section C.3 of appendix C shows that on a
manifold, \(M\), of dimension \(n\), the Lie group of isometries cannot have
dimension greater than \(n(n+1)/2\). We establish, now, a number of properties of
Lie groups which will be used below in our analysis of homogeneous cosmologies.

Let \(G\) be a Lie group of dimension \(m\). It follows from the smoothness of \(i\)
and \(f\) that for each \(h\in G\) the map
\begin{equation}
  \psi_h(g)=hg
  \tag{7.2.1}\label{eq:7.2.1}
\end{equation}
called \emph{left translation} by \(h\), is a diffeomorphism. Let \(\psi_h^*\)
denote the map on tensors induced by \(\psi_h\) (see appendix C). If a vector field
\(v^a\) on \(G\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \psi_h^*v^a=v^a
  \tag{7.2.2}\label{eq:7.2.2}
\end{equation}
for all \(h\in G\), then \(v^a\) is called \emph{left invariant}. More generally, a
tensor field invariant under \(\psi_h^*\) for all \(h\in G\) is said to be left
invariant. It is easy to see that the left invariant vector fields form a vector space
since sums and scalar multiples of left invariant vector fields are also left invariant.
Clearly, a left invariant vector field is determined by its value at \(T_eG\), the
tangent space at the identity element, \(e\), since if \(v^a\) is left invariant, we
have
\begin{equation}
  \left.v^a\right|_h
    =\psi_h^*\!\left[\left.v^a\right|_e\right] .
  \tag{7.2.3}\label{eq:7.2.3}
\end{equation}
Conversely, if we define a vector field in terms of its value at \(e\) by
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.3}, we produce a left invariant vector field. Thus, the left
invariant vector fields on \(G\) form an \(m\)-dimensional vector space.

Now, if \(\phi\) is any diffeomorphism on any manifold and \(v^a\) and \(w^a\)
are any two vector fields, we have
\begin{equation}
  \phi^*([v,w])=[\phi^*(v),\phi^*(w)] ,
  \tag{7.2.4}\label{eq:7.2.4}
\end{equation}
where \([\, ,\,]\) denotes the commutator, defined in section 2.2 above. Thus, if
\(v^a\) and \(w^a\) are left invariant vector fields on a Lie group, the
commutator \([v,w]^a\) also will be a left invariant vector field. Since the
commutator depends linearly on \(v^a\) and \(w^a\), this implies that there exists
a left invariant tensor field \(c^a{}_{bc}\) such that
\begin{equation}
  [v,w]^a=c^a{}_{bc}v^bw^c .
  \tag{7.2.5}\label{eq:7.2.5}
\end{equation}
The tensor field \(c^a{}_{bc}\) is called the \emph{structure constant tensor} of the
Lie group. It follows immediately from its definition that
\begin{equation}
  c^a{}_{bc}=-c^a{}_{cb} .
  \tag{7.2.6}\label{eq:7.2.6}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, the Jacobi identity for commutators,
\begin{equation}
  [u,[v,w]]+[v,[w,u]]+[w,[u,v]]=0 ,
  \tag{7.2.7}\label{eq:7.2.7}
\end{equation}
implies that
\begin{equation}
  c^e{}_{d[a}c^d{}_{bc]}=0 .
  \tag{7.2.8}\label{eq:7.2.8}
\end{equation}

A finite-dimensional vector space with a tensor \(c^a{}_{bc}\) of type \((1,2)\)
satisfying equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.6} and \eqref{eq:7.2.8} is called a
\emph{Lie algebra}. We have shown above that the left invariant vector fields of a
Lie group comprise a Lie algebra. Thus, every Lie group gives rise to a Lie algebra.
Conversely, it is possible to show that every Lie algebra gives rise to a Lie group in
a unique way up to global topological structure. More precisely, given any Lie
algebra, there exists a unique connected, simply connected Lie group whose Lie
algebra coincides with the given Lie algebra.\footnote{Existence of a Lie group
associated with a Lie algebra follows from Ado's theorem (see, e.g., Jacobson 1962).
Existence of a simply connected Lie group follows from the universal covering group
construction described in chapter 13. Uniqueness is proven, e.g., in Warner (1971).}
This fact tremendously simplifies the analysis of Lie groups, since Lie algebras are
much simpler objects to work with than Lie groups.

Let \(\alpha_a\) be a left invariant one-form. If \(v^a\) is a left invariant vector
field, then \(\alpha_av^a\) is constant, so
\begin{equation}
  0=\nabla_b(\alpha_av^a)
   =v^a\nabla_b\alpha_a+\alpha_a\nabla_bv^a ,
  \tag{7.2.9}\label{eq:7.2.9}
\end{equation}
where \(\nabla_a\) is any derivative operator on \(G\). If \(v^a\) and \(w^a\)
are both left invariant vector fields, we have
\begin{align}
  2v^aw^b\nabla_{[a}\alpha_{b]}
    &=(v^aw^b-v^bw^a)\nabla_a\alpha_b \notag\\
    &=-v^a\alpha_b\nabla_aw^b+\alpha_bw^a\nabla_av^b \notag\\
    &=-\alpha_b[v,w]^b \notag\\
    &=-\alpha_bc^b{}_{cd}v^cw^d .
  \tag{7.2.10}\label{eq:7.2.10}
\end{align}
Thus, we find that every left invariant one-form \(\alpha_b\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
  2\nabla_{[a}\alpha_{b]}=-\alpha_cc^c{}_{ab} .
  \tag{7.2.11}\label{eq:7.2.11}
\end{equation}

The \emph{right translation} map \(\chi_h\), defined by
\(\chi_h(g)=gh\), also is a diffeomorphism. We define right invariant tensor
fields analogously to left invariant tensor fields. As discussed in section 2.2, any
vector field generates a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms. Let \(x^a\) be a
right invariant vector field, and let \(\phi_t\) be its one-parameter group of
diffeomorphisms. Since \(x^a\) is right invariant, we have
\begin{equation}
  \chi_h\circ\phi_t=\phi_t\circ\chi_h
  \tag{7.2.12}\label{eq:7.2.12}
\end{equation}
for all \(h\in G\). Consequently, defining \(h(t)=\phi_t(e)\), we have, for all
\(g\in G\),
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t(g)=\phi_t\circ\chi_g(e)
    =\chi_g\circ\phi_t(e)
    =\chi_g[h(t)]
    =h(t)g
    =\psi_{h(t)}(g) .
  \tag{7.2.13}\label{eq:7.2.13}
\end{equation}
Thus, we find
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t=\psi_{h(t)} ;
  \tag{7.2.14}\label{eq:7.2.14}
\end{equation}
i.e., the right invariant vector fields are infinitesimal generators of left
translations.

This immediately implies that if \(x^a\) is a right invariant vector field and
\(v^a\) is a left invariant vector field, we have
\begin{equation}
  0=\mathcal L_xv^a=[x,v]^a .
  \tag{7.2.15}\label{eq:7.2.15}
\end{equation}
Similarly, if \(x^a\) is a right invariant vector field and \(\alpha_a\) is a left
invariant one-form, we have
\begin{equation}
  0=\mathcal L_x\alpha_b
    =x^a\nabla_a\alpha_b+\alpha_a\nabla_bx^a ,
  \tag{7.2.16}\label{eq:7.2.16}
\end{equation}
where \(\nabla_a\) is any derivative operator. Consequently, if \(x^a\) and
\(y^a\) are both right invariant but \(\alpha_b\) is left invariant, we find, by a
calculation similar to that of equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.10},
\begin{align}
  2x^ay^b\nabla_{[a}\alpha_{b]}
    &=y^bx^a\nabla_a\alpha_b-x^by^a\nabla_a\alpha_b \notag\\
    &=-y^b\alpha_a\nabla_bx^a+x^b\alpha_a\nabla_by^a \notag\\
    &=\alpha_a[x,y]^a .
  \tag{7.2.17}\label{eq:7.2.17}
\end{align}
On the other hand, by equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.11}, we have
\begin{equation}
  2x^ay^b\nabla_{[a}\alpha_{b]}
    =x^ay^b(-\alpha_cc^c{}_{ab}) .
  \tag{7.2.18}\label{eq:7.2.18}
\end{equation}
Thus, we find
\begin{equation}
  [x,y]^c=-c^c{}_{ab}x^ay^b ;
  \tag{7.2.19}\label{eq:7.2.19}
\end{equation}
i.e., except for a change in sign, the right invariant vector fields satisfy the same
commutation relations as the left invariant vector fields. Note that
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.19} shows that \(c^c{}_{ab}\) is also right
invariant.\footnote{The tensor \(c^c{}_{da}c^d{}_{cb}\) is symmetric in \(a\)
and \(b\). For semisimple Lie groups it also is nondegenerate. Thus, semisimple
Lie groups possess a natural bi-invariant (i.e., left and right invariant) metric
\(g_{ab}=c^c{}_{da}c^d{}_{cb}\).}

Let us apply the above facts about Lie groups to homogeneous cosmological models.
By definition, in a spatially homogeneous spacetime \((M,g_{ab})\), there exists
a family of spacelike hypersurfaces \(\Sigma_t\) such that for any two points
\(p,q\in\Sigma_t\), there exists an element \(g\colon M\longrightarrow M\) of the
Lie group, \(G\), of isometries such that \(g(p)=q\). (\(G\) is said to act
\emph{transitively} on each \(\Sigma_t\).) We will restrict attention to the case
where for all \(\Sigma_t\) and for all \(p,q\in\Sigma_t\) there is a
\emph{unique} element \(g\in G\) such that \(g(p)=q\), in which case \(G\) is
said to act \emph{simply transitively} on each \(\Sigma_t\). This implies
\(\dim G=\dim\Sigma_t=3\). In fact, almost no loss of generality results from
restricting consideration to a simply transitive action because it turns out (see,
e.g., MacCallum 1979) that the only case where \(G\) does not act simply
transitively or does not possess a subgroup with simply transitive
action\footnote{In particular, the isometry groups of the homogeneous, isotropic
models of chapter 5 all possess subgroups which have a simply transitive action on
the homogeneous surfaces.} is the group
\(\operatorname{SO}(3)\times\mathbb R\) acting on the “cylinder”
\(S^2\times\mathbb R\) [with the orbits of \(\operatorname{SO}(3)\) being the
two-dimensional spheres]; spatially homogeneous models with this isometry group
(called Kantowski--Sachs models) can be treated separately by similar techniques
(see, e.g., Ryan and Shepley 1975).

The advantage of considering simply transitive action is that if we (arbitrarily)
choose a point \(p\in\Sigma_t\), we can put the elements of \(G\) into
correspondence with the points of \(\Sigma_t\) by the association
\(g\longmapsto g(p)\). (A simply transitive action is needed to ensure that this
correspondence is one-to-one.) Under this identification of \(G\) and \(\Sigma_t\),
the action on \(\Sigma_t\) of the isometry \(g\) corresponds to left
multiplication by \(g\) on \(G\). Thus, the tensor fields on \(\Sigma_t\) which
are preserved under the isometries---in particular, the spatial metric, \(h_{ab}\),
on \(\Sigma_t\)---correspond precisely to the left invariant tensor fields on
\(G\). In particular, this means that the vector fields on \(\Sigma_t\) which are
preserved under the isometries satisfy the commutation
relations~\eqref{eq:7.2.5}, and, similarly, the invariant one-forms satisfy
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.11}. Furthermore, the infinitesimal generators of the
isometries on \(\Sigma_t\)---i.e., the Killing vector fields of
\(\Sigma_t\)---correspond to the right invariant vector fields of \(G\). Thus,
the Killing vector fields of \(\Sigma_t\) satisfy the commutation
relations~\eqref{eq:7.2.19}.

Our next task is to put the metric of a spatially homogeneous spacetime with simply
transitive group action into a useful canonical form. Consider a single homogeneous
hypersurface \(\Sigma_0\). Ideally, one would like to choose spatial coordinates
adapted to the Killing vector fields so that the coordinate components of the
spacetime metric would be independent of the spatial coordinates. However, except
for the simple case of the translation group, \(G=\mathbb R^3\), this cannot be
done, since coordinate vector fields must commute, but according to
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.19} the Killing vector fields do not commute unless
\(c^c{}_{ab}=0\). Thus, in general, only one Killing vector field can be employed
as a coordinate vector field, and this is not particularly useful. Thus, instead, we
choose a basis of one-forms
\((\sigma^1)_a,(\sigma^2)_a,(\sigma^3)_a\) which are preserved under the
isometries (which we can do by choosing an arbitrary basis at \(p\in\Sigma_0\)
and defining the basis elsewhere by “left translation”). Since the spatial metric,
\(h_{ab}\), on \(\Sigma_0\) (obtained by restriction of the spacetime metric
\(g_{ab}\) to vectors tangent to \(\Sigma_0\)) is left invariant, we have
\begin{equation}
  h_{ab}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{3}
    h_{\alpha\beta}(\sigma^\alpha)_a(\sigma^\beta)_b ,
  \tag{7.2.20}\label{eq:7.2.20}
\end{equation}
where the components, \(h_{\alpha\beta}\), are constant on \(\Sigma_0\), i.e.,
independent of spatial position.

Let \(p\in\Sigma_0\), let \(t^a\) denote the unit normal to \(\Sigma_0\) at
\(p\), and let \(\gamma\) be the geodesic determined by \((p,t^a)\). Then
\(\gamma\) will be orthogonal to all the spatial hypersurfaces it intersects because,
by proposition C.3.1, the tangent to \(\gamma\) must always remain orthogonal to all
the spatial Killing vector fields since it is initially orthogonal to them. We label the
other spatially homogeneous hypersurfaces by the proper time \(t\) of the
intersection of the geodesic \(\gamma\) with the hypersurface. Then, the vector field
\(t^a=-\nabla^at\) will be everywhere orthogonal to each \(\Sigma_t\) (since
\(t\) is constant on \(\Sigma_t\)), and the integral curves of \(t^a\) are all
geodesics (with \(t^at_a=-1\)), since this is true along \(\gamma\) by
construction and hence is true everywhere on each \(\Sigma_t\) by spatial
homogeneity. We define the one-forms \((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) throughout the
spacetime in terms of their values on the initial surface \(\Sigma_0\) by “Lie
transport” along \(t^a\), i.e., by setting
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_t(\sigma^\alpha)_a=0 ,
  \tag{7.2.21}\label{eq:7.2.21}
\end{equation}
or, equivalently, by defining \((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) on the hypersurface
\(\Sigma_t\) by
\begin{equation}
  (\sigma^\alpha)_a(t)=\phi_t^*[\sigma^\alpha(0)]_a ,
  \tag{7.2.22}\label{eq:7.2.22}
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_t\) denotes the one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms generated by
\(t^a\). It follows directly from equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.21} that
\((\sigma^\alpha)_at^a=0\) everywhere.

Since \(\phi_t\) is constructed in an entirely geometrical manner, it is not difficult
to see that it must commute with any isometry \(g\),
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t\circ g=g\circ\phi_t .
  \tag{7.2.23}\label{eq:7.2.23}
\end{equation}
It follows immediately from this fact that on each \(\Sigma_t\) (i.e., not only on
\(\Sigma_0\)), the basis one-forms \((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) are invariant under the
spatial isometries. Note also that
\begin{align}
  2t^a\nabla_{[a}(\sigma^\alpha)_{b]}
    &=t^a\nabla_a(\sigma^\alpha)_b
      -t^a\nabla_b(\sigma^\alpha)_a \notag\\
    &=t^a\nabla_a(\sigma^\alpha)_b
      +(\sigma^\alpha)_a\nabla_bt^a \notag\\
    &=\mathcal L_t(\sigma^\alpha)_b \notag\\
    &=0 .
  \tag{7.2.24}\label{eq:7.2.24}
\end{align}
Thus, \(\nabla_{[a}(\sigma^\alpha)_{b]}\) has no component perpendicular to
\(\Sigma_t\). Since the part of \(\nabla_{[a}(\sigma^\alpha)_{b]}\) projected
into \(\Sigma_t\) satisfies equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.11} since
\((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) is invariant under the spatial isometries, we have
\begin{equation}
  2\nabla_{[a}(\sigma^\alpha)_{b]}
    =-c^c{}_{ab}(\sigma^\alpha)_c .
  \tag{7.2.25}\label{eq:7.2.25}
\end{equation}

The conclusion of all of the above discussion is the following: For a spatially
homogeneous spacetime on which the spatial isometry group \(G\) acts in a simply
transitive manner, the manifold structure is \(M=\mathbb R\times G\). By
defining the function \(t\) and the left invariant one-forms
\((\sigma^1)_a,(\sigma^2)_a,\) and \((\sigma^3)_a\) on \(M\) in the manner
described above, we can express the spacetime metric \(g_{ab}\) in the form
\begin{equation}
  g_{ab}=-\nabla_at\nabla_bt
    +\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{3}
      h_{\alpha\beta}(t)(\sigma^\alpha)_a(\sigma^\beta)_b ,
  \tag{7.2.26}\label{eq:7.2.26}
\end{equation}
where the one-forms \((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{[a}(\sigma^\alpha)_{b]}
    =-\frac12c^c{}_{ab}(\sigma^\alpha)_c .
  \tag{7.2.27}\label{eq:7.2.27}
\end{equation}

Thus, to construct spatially homogeneous cosmological models, we simply choose a
three-dimensional Lie group \(G\), choose a basis of left invariant one-forms on
\(G\), and choose the functions \(h_{\alpha\beta}(t)\) [or, equivalently, choose a
time-dependent left invariant metric \(h_{ab}(t)\) on \(G\)]. We then define the
spacetime metric on \(\mathbb R\times G\) by equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.26}. All
homogeneous cosmologies with simply transitive action can be constructed in this
manner.

Thus, apart from the exceptional case mentioned above where the Lie group action
is not simply transitive, the program for obtaining all general relativistic spatially
homogeneous cosmologies will be completed by (1) obtaining all three-dimensional
Lie groups \(G\) and (2) writing down and solving Einstein's equation for the
metric~\eqref{eq:7.2.26}.

The first task was accomplished by Bianchi (1897), who obtained all
three-dimensional Lie algebras and classified them into nine types. (As mentioned
above, these Lie algebras are in one-to-one correspondence with all connected,
simply connected three-dimensional Lie groups.) We shall derive here a slightly
modified version of the Bianchi classification (see Ellis and MacCallum 1969). We
seek all possible tensors \(c^c{}_{ab}\) on a three-dimensional vector space \(V\)
satisfying \(c^c{}_{ab}=c^c{}_{[ab]}\) and the Jacobi identity,
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.8}. Let \(\epsilon_{abc}\) be a fixed 3-form on \(V\),
i.e., a totally antisymmetric tensor of type \((0,3)\). Given \(c^c{}_{ab}\), we
define \(A_a\) and \(M^{ab}\) by
\begin{align}
  A_a&=c^b{}_{ba} ,
  \tag{7.2.28}\label{eq:7.2.28}\\
  M^{ab}&=\frac12\epsilon^{acd}
    \left(c^b{}_{cd}-\delta^b{}_cA_d\right) ,
  \tag{7.2.29}\label{eq:7.2.29}
\end{align}
where \(\epsilon^{abc}\) is the unique totally antisymmetric tensor of type
\((3,0)\) satisfying \(\epsilon^{abc}\epsilon_{abc}=3!=6\). Contracting
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.29} with \(\epsilon_{aef}\) and using
equation~\eqref{eq:B.2.13}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{aef}M^{ab}
    =c^b{}_{ef}-\delta^b{}_{[e}A_{f]} .
  \tag{7.2.30}\label{eq:7.2.30}
\end{equation}
Contracting this equation over \(e\) and \(b\) and using the
definition~\eqref{eq:7.2.28} of \(A_a\), we find that \(M^{ab}\) is symmetric,
\begin{equation}
  M^{[ab]}=0 .
  \tag{7.2.31}\label{eq:7.2.31}
\end{equation}
Thus, we have shown that on a three-dimensional vector space, any \(c^c{}_{ab}\)
satisfying \(c^c{}_{ab}=c^c{}_{[ab]}\) can be written in the form
\begin{equation}
  c^c{}_{ab}
    =M^{cd}\epsilon_{dab}+\delta^c{}_{[a}A_{b]} ,
  \tag{7.2.32}\label{eq:7.2.32}
\end{equation}
where \(M^{cd}=M^{dc}\). Substitution of this expression into the Jacobi
identity~\eqref{eq:7.2.8} yields the remarkably simple result,
\begin{equation}
  M^{ab}A_b=0 .
  \tag{7.2.33}\label{eq:7.2.33}
\end{equation}
Thus, a three-dimensional Lie algebra is determined by a covector \(A_a\) and a
% SOURCE ERRATUM: printed ``satisying''; corrected to ``satisfying.''
symmetric tensor \(M^{ab}\) satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.33}.

If \(A_a=0\), then equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.33} is trivially satisfied. In this case
(referred to as “class A”), the resulting Lie algebras are classified (i.e., uniquely
determined up to isomorphisms) by the rank and signature (up to overall sign) of
\(M^{ab}\). Hence, there exist precisely six distinct Lie algebras in this case:
(i) \(M^{ab}=0\), (ii) \(\operatorname{rank}(M^{ab})=1\),
(iii) \(\operatorname{rank}(M^{ab})=2\), signature \(+-\),
(iv) \(\operatorname{rank}(M^{ab})=2\), signature \(++\),
(v) \(\operatorname{rank}(M^{ab})=3\), signature \(++-\), and
(vi) \(\operatorname{rank}(M^{ab})=3\), signature \(+++\).
If \(A_b\ne0\) (referred to as “class B”), then by
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.33} the rank of \(M^{ab}\) cannot be greater than two.
Hence, in this case there exist four possibilities for the rank and signature (up to
overall sign) of \(M^{ab}\). However, in the two cases where the rank of \(M^{ab}\)
is equal to two, a scalar \(\alpha\) is determined by the formula
\begin{equation}
  A_eA_f
    =\alpha M^{ac}M^{bd}\epsilon_{cde}\epsilon_{abf} ,
  \tag{7.2.34}\label{eq:7.2.34}
\end{equation}
since the tensors appearing on both sides of this equation are nonvanishing and must
be proportional to each other. As a result, in class B there exist two one-parameter
families of Lie algebras classified by the signature of the rank-two tensor \(M^{ab}\)
and the value of \(\alpha\), as well as two further Lie algebras corresponding to the
cases where the rank of \(M^{ab}\) is zero (i.e., \(M^{ab}=0\)) or one. Tables
giving explicit formulas for the components of \(c^c{}_{ab}\) in conveniently
chosen bases for all the above Lie algebras can be found in Taub (1951) and Ryan
and Shepley (1975).

To write down Einstein's equation, we need to compute the curvature of the
metric~\eqref{eq:7.2.26}. The most straightforward procedure to do so is to define
a left invariant orthonormal basis \((e_\mu)_a\) (which, however, will not satisfy
\eqref{eq:7.2.21}) by
\begin{align}
  (e_0)_a&=\nabla_at ,
  \tag{7.2.35}\label{eq:7.2.35}\\
  (e_\alpha)_a&=\sum_{\beta=1}^{3}
    B_{\alpha\beta}(t)(\sigma^\beta)_a
    \qquad(\alpha=1,2,3) ,
  \tag{7.2.36}\label{eq:7.2.36}
\end{align}
where, in order for the \((e_\mu)_a\) to be orthonormal, \(B_{\alpha\beta}\)
must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\alpha\beta}
    =\sum_{\gamma,\delta}
      B_{\alpha\gamma}B_{\beta\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}
    \qquad(\alpha,\beta=1,2,3) ,
  \tag{7.2.37}\label{eq:7.2.37}
\end{equation}
where \(h^{\gamma\delta}\) are the components of the inverse metric, \(h^{ab}\),
in the basis dual to \((\sigma^\alpha)_a\). (There is, of course, considerable
freedom involved in the choice of \(B_{\alpha\beta}\); additional conditions, such
as \(B_{\alpha\beta}=B_{\beta\alpha}\) [Ryan and Shepley 1975] may be imposed in
order to uniquely specify \(B_{\alpha\beta}\).) For this orthonormal basis, we have
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{[a}(e_0)_{b]}=\nabla_{[a}\nabla_{b]}t=0 ;
  \tag{7.2.38}\label{eq:7.2.38}
\end{equation}
and, for \(\alpha=1,2,3\),
\begin{align}
  \nabla_{[a}(e_\alpha)_{b]}
    &=\sum_{\beta=1}^{3}
      \left\{
        \frac{dB_{\alpha\beta}}{dt}
          \nabla_{[a}t(\sigma^\beta)_{b]}
        -\frac12B_{\alpha\beta}c^c{}_{ab}(\sigma^\beta)_c
      \right\} \notag\\
    &=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{3}
      A_\alpha{}^\gamma\nabla_{[a}t(e_\gamma)_{b]}
      -\frac12c^c{}_{ab}(e_\alpha)_c ,
  \tag{7.2.39}\label{eq:7.2.39}
\end{align}
where
\(A_\alpha{}^\gamma
  =\sum_\beta(dB_{\alpha\beta}/dt)(B^{-1})^{\beta\gamma}\)
and equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.27} was used.
Equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.38} and \eqref{eq:7.2.39} determine the connection
one-forms via equation~\eqref{eq:3.4.24}, and, thus, the curvature can be
calculated by the tetrad method of section 3.4b. This procedure will yield a formula
for the Riemann and Ricci curvature tensors in terms of the matrix
\(B_{\alpha\beta}\) and the structure constant tensor \(c^c{}_{ab}\), and such
formulas for the Ricci curvature are given by Ryan and Shepley (1975). However,
using equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.37}, we can eliminate \(B_{\alpha\beta}\) in terms
of \(h_{\alpha\beta}\) (as must be possible since the choices of Lie group and
\(h_{\alpha\beta}\) completely determine the metric~\eqref{eq:7.2.26} and thus
the curvature must be expressible in terms of them). We then can write the
component formulas thus obtained as tensor equations for the curvature in terms of
the metric, \(h_{ab}(t)\), on \(\Sigma_t\) and the structure constant tensor
\(c^c{}_{ab}\). We shall not present the details of these rather lengthy calculations
here but merely present the final result. Defining \(K_{ab}\) by
\begin{equation}
  K_{ab}=\frac12\mathcal L_th_{ab}
  \tag{7.2.40}\label{eq:7.2.40}
\end{equation}
[so that, as discussed more generally in chapters 9 and 10, \(K_{ab}(t)\) is the
extrinsic curvature of \(\Sigma_t\)], we obtain the following formulas for the
components \(G_{ab}(e_0)^a(e_0)^b\), \(G_{bc}h^b{}_a(e_0)^c\), and
\(h^c{}_ah^d{}_bR_{cd}\) of the Einstein and Ricci curvature tensors:
\begin{align}
  2G_{ab}(e_0)^a(e_0)^b
    &=(K^a{}_a)^2-K_{ab}K^{ab}
      -c^a{}_{ab}c^c{}_c{}^b
      -\frac12c^a{}_{cb}c^c{}_a{}^b
      -\frac14c_{abc}c^{abc} ,
  \tag{7.2.41}\label{eq:7.2.41}\\
  G_{bc}h^b{}_a(e_0)^c
    &=R_{bc}h^b{}_a(e_0)^c
      =K^b{}_cc^c{}_{ba}+K^b{}_ac^c{}_{bc} ,
  \tag{7.2.42}\label{eq:7.2.42}\\
  h^c{}_ah^d{}_bR_{cd}
    &=\mathcal L_tK_{ab}+K^c{}_cK_{ab}-2K_{ac}K^c{}_b
      +\frac14c_{acd}c_b{}^{cd} \notag\\
    &\quad-c^c{}_{cd}c_{(ab)}{}^d-c_{cda}c^{(cd)}{}_b .
  \tag{7.2.43}\label{eq:7.2.43}
\end{align}
In the right-hand sides of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41}--\eqref{eq:7.2.43} all
indices are lowered and raised by the spatial metric, \(h_{ab}\), and its inverse,
\(h^{ab}\).

Now, the vacuum Einstein equation, \(R_{ab}=0\), is equivalent to the vanishing of
the left-hand sides of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41}--\eqref{eq:7.2.43}. (The
non-vacuum Einstein equation, of course, is obtained by setting the left-hand sides
of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41}--\eqref{eq:7.2.43} equal to appropriate
stress-energy terms.) Using the definition of \(K_{ab}\),
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.40}, it may be verified that if
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41} and \eqref{eq:7.2.42} with the left-hand sides set
equal to zero hold “initially,” i.e., on the hypersurface \(\Sigma_0\), then they
hold everywhere if equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.43} is satisfied (with the left-hand
side set equal to zero); in other words, the time derivatives of
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41} and \eqref{eq:7.2.42} vanish automatically by virtue
of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40} and \eqref{eq:7.2.43}. The reason this occurs can
be traced directly to the Bianchi identity, \(\nabla_aG^{ab}=0\), and will be
discussed more generally in chapter 10. Thus, equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41} and
\eqref{eq:7.2.42} act as constraint equations on the initial values of \(K_{ab}\)
and \(h_{ab}\). (The right-hand sides of
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.41}--\eqref{eq:7.2.43}, of course, contain \(h_{ab}\)
implicitly in the index raisings and lowerings.) On the other hand,
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40} and \eqref{eq:7.2.43} act as evolution equations for
\(h_{ab}\) and \(K_{ab}\). If we take the components of
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40} and \eqref{eq:7.2.43} in our original basis
\((\sigma^\alpha)_a\), we obtain directly a system of ordinary differential
equations which express \(dh_{\alpha\beta}/dt\) and \(dK_{\alpha\beta}/dt\) as
functions of \(h_{\alpha\beta}\), \(K_{\alpha\beta}\), and the group structure
constants \(c^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}\). From the theory of ordinary differential
equations, it is well known that a unique solution of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40}
and \eqref{eq:7.2.43} always exists for given initial values of \(h_{ab}\) and
\(K_{ab}\).

Thus, to obtain a spatially homogeneous solution of the vacuum Einstein equation,
we need only solve the algebraic constraint equations, \eqref{eq:7.2.41} and
\eqref{eq:7.2.42}, for the initial data \(h_{ab}\), \(K_{ab}\) at \(t=0\). A
unique solution then is generated by the evolution equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40}
and \eqref{eq:7.2.43}. Although in most cases it may not be possible to integrate
these equations analytically, numerical integration of such a system of ordinary
differential equations always can be carried out in a straightforward manner.
Similar conclusions hold for the non-vacuum Einstein equation with appropriate
matter sources.

As an example of the above procedure, we shall determine all the spatially
homogeneous vacuum solutions for the simplest case of the Lie group
\(G=\mathbb R^3\) with group “multiplication” given by addition. (This case is
called “type I” in the Bianchi classification of Lie groups.) Since the group
multiplication is commutative, the vector field generators of left translations must
commute, so we have \(c^c{}_{ab}=0\) by equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.19}. Hence any
basis of left invariant vector fields forms a coordinate basis, and for any choice of
\((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) constructed in the manner described above, we can find
coordinates \(x,y,z\) on \(\mathbb R^3\) so that
\((\sigma^1)_a=(dx)_a\), \((\sigma^2)_a=(dy)_a\),
\((\sigma^3)_a=(dz)_a\). (The simple case \(c^c{}_{ab}=0\), of course, is the
only case for which this can be done.) Without loss of generality, we may choose
\((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) to be orthonormal on the initial surface \(\Sigma_0\), and
we may rotate this basis to diagonalize \(K_{\alpha\beta}\) on \(\Sigma_0\). The
evolution equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40} and \eqref{eq:7.2.43} with
\(c^c{}_{ab}=0\) then imply that in the basis \((\sigma^\alpha)_a\) (which is
defined off the initial surface by equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.21}) both
\(h_{\alpha\beta}\) and \(K_{\alpha\beta}\) must remain diagonal for all time.
Thus, we have
\begin{align}
  K_{\alpha\beta}&=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,k_2,k_3) ,
  \tag{7.2.44}\label{eq:7.2.44}\\
  h_{\alpha\beta}&=\operatorname{diag}(f_1,f_2,f_3) ,
  \tag{7.2.45}\label{eq:7.2.45}\\
  h^{\alpha\beta}&=\operatorname{diag}(f_1^{-1},f_2^{-1},f_3^{-1}) .
  \tag{7.2.46}\label{eq:7.2.46}
\end{align}
By construction, on the initial surface, we have \(f_\alpha=1\). The initial value
constraint~\eqref{eq:7.2.42} is trivially satisfied, so the only requirement on the
initial values of \(k_\alpha\) comes from equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.41} which yields
(using \(f_\alpha=1\)),
\begin{equation}
  (k_1+k_2+k_3)^2=k_1^2+k_2^2+k_3^2 .
  \tag{7.2.47}\label{eq:7.2.47}
\end{equation}
The evolution equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.40} and \eqref{eq:7.2.43} yield
\begin{align}
  k_\alpha&=\frac12\frac{df_\alpha}{dt} ,
  \tag{7.2.48}\label{eq:7.2.48}\\
  \frac{dk_\alpha}{dt}
    &=-\left(\sum_\beta f_\beta^{-1}k_\beta\right)k_\alpha
      +2f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha^2 .
  \tag{7.2.49}\label{eq:7.2.49}
\end{align}
Hence, we find
\begin{align}
  \frac{d}{dt}(f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha)
    &=f_\alpha^{-1}
      \left\{
        -\left(\sum_\beta f_\beta^{-1}k_\beta\right)k_\alpha
        +2f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha^2
      \right\}
      -k_\alpha f_\alpha^{-2}(2k_\alpha) \notag\\
    &=-\left(\sum_\beta f_\beta^{-1}k_\beta\right)
      f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha .
  \tag{7.2.50}\label{eq:7.2.50}
\end{align}
% SOURCE ERRATUM: the printed second line has a plus sign; the preceding line
% and equation (7.2.51) require the minus sign used above.
Summing equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.50} over \(\alpha\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dK}{dt}=-K^2 ,
  \tag{7.2.51}\label{eq:7.2.51}
\end{equation}
where \(K=\sum_\alpha f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha=K^a{}_a\). If we exclude the
trivial solution \(K=0\) (which leads uniquely to the Minkowski solution), the
general solution of equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.51} is
\begin{equation}
  K=\frac1t ,
  \tag{7.2.52}\label{eq:7.2.52}
\end{equation}
where the constant of integration has been absorbed in the definition of the zero of
\(t\). Substitution of our solution~\eqref{eq:7.2.52} into
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.50} yields
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dt}(f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha)
    =-\frac1t(f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha) ,
  \tag{7.2.53}\label{eq:7.2.53}
\end{equation}
which has the general solution
\begin{equation}
  f_\alpha^{-1}k_\alpha=\frac{p_\alpha}{t} ,
  \tag{7.2.54}\label{eq:7.2.54}
\end{equation}
where \(p_\alpha\) is a constant. Hence, by equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.48}, we have
\begin{equation}
  \frac12f_\alpha^{-1}\frac{df_\alpha}{dt}
    =\frac{p_\alpha}{t} ,
  \tag{7.2.55}\label{eq:7.2.55}
\end{equation}
which has the general solution
\begin{equation}
  f_\alpha=C_\alpha t^{2p_\alpha}
  \tag{7.2.56}\label{eq:7.2.56}
\end{equation}
where \(C_\alpha\) is a constant. Equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.48} (or
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.54}) then yields
\begin{equation}
  k_\alpha=C_\alpha p_\alpha t^{2p_\alpha-1} .
  \tag{7.2.57}\label{eq:7.2.57}
\end{equation}
Substitution of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.56} and \eqref{eq:7.2.57} in
equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.52} yields
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\alpha p_\alpha=1 .
  \tag{7.2.58}\label{eq:7.2.58}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.56} and \eqref{eq:7.2.57}, subject to the
condition~\eqref{eq:7.2.58}, are the general solutions (aside from Minkowski
spacetime) of the evolution equations. Taking \(t=1\) as our initial surface on
which to impose the requirement \(f_\alpha=1\) and \(k_\alpha\) subject to the
constraint~\eqref{eq:7.2.47}, we obtain
\begin{align}
  C_\alpha&=1 ,
  \tag{7.2.59}\label{eq:7.2.59}\\
  \left(\sum_\alpha p_\alpha\right)^2
    &=\sum_\alpha p_\alpha^2 .
  \tag{7.2.60}\label{eq:7.2.60}
\end{align}
Thus, the general spatially homogeneous vacuum solution (aside from Minkowski
spacetime) of Einstein's equation with simply transitive Lie group
\(G=\mathbb R^3\) is
\begin{equation}
  ds^2=-dt^2+t^{2p_1}dx^2+t^{2p_2}dy^2+t^{2p_3}dz^2 ,
  \tag{7.2.61}\label{eq:7.2.61}
\end{equation}
where \(p_1,p_2,p_3\) are constants subject to equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.58} and
\eqref{eq:7.2.60}. Equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.61} is known as the Kasner solution
(Kasner 1925).

One solution of equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.58} and \eqref{eq:7.2.60} is
\(p_1=1\), \(p_2=p_3=0\). The Kasner solution with this choice of \(p_\alpha\)
can be recognized to be simply the Rindler spacetime of section 6.4 (crossed with
\(\mathbb R^2\)), with \(t\) and \(x\) interchanged. Thus, in this case, the
apparent singularity at \(t=0\) is only a coordinate singularity, and the Kasner
solution~\eqref{eq:7.2.61} is just the wedge of Minkowski spacetime labeled as
region II in Figure 6.8. However, all solutions other than the trivial solutions where
two of the \(p_\alpha\) vanish yield nonflat spacetimes with a physical singularity
at \(t=0\). Note that with the exception of the trivial solutions, all solutions of
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.58} and \eqref{eq:7.2.60} must have two of the
\(p_\alpha\) positive and the other negative. Thus, the Kasner solution describes
a homogeneous universe which expands in two directions but contracts in the other
direction. Interestingly, the analysis of the dynamics of homogeneous vacuum
solutions with \(G=\operatorname{SU}(2)\) (Bianchi type IX) shows that near the
initial singularity the evolution can be described as a series of “Kasner epochs”
connected by “transition regimes” where the values of \(p_\alpha\) change (see
Belinskii et al.\ 1970).
