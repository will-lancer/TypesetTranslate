\subsection{Number Counts}\label{sec:14.7}

Since the Hubble program has not yet succeeded in telling us very much about
the cosmic scale factor \(a(t)\), it is natural to widen our scope, and
consider numbers of optical or radio sources as functions of apparent
luminosity and/or red shift.

The study of number counts offers two potential advantages over the Hubble
program:

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(A)}
The development of radio telescopes of very large aperture and sensitivity
has led to the detection and resolution of thousands of faint radio sources,
most of which are presumably at very great distances. The majority of these
sources have not yet been identified with optical objects, so their red
shifts are as yet unknown. (No radio lines have been observed from resolved
radio sources, and their red shifts can therefore only be measured
optically.) Not knowing \(z\), the best use to which cosmologists can put
these sources is to plot their number as a function of their strength.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(B)}
The quasi-stellar objects discussed in the last section have measured red
shifts ranging up to \(z\simeq2\), but have too wide a spread in absolute
luminosity to allow determination of the luminosity distance \(d_L\). By
plotting the number of quasi-stellar objects as a function of \(z\) alone,
or \(z\) and \(l\), we can eliminate some of the problems caused by the
spread in \(L\).

To begin in a very general way, let us assume that at time \(t_1\) there are
\(n(L,t_1)\dd L\) sources per unit volume with absolute luminosity between
\(L\) and \(L+\dd L\). The proper volume element is
\[
  \dd V
  =
  \sqrt{\gamma}\,\dd r_1\,\dd\theta_1\,\dd\phi_1
  =
  a^3(t_1)(1-kr_1^2)^{-1/2}r_1^2
  \dd r_1\sin\theta_1\,\dd\theta_1\,\dd\phi_1,
\]
so the number of sources between \(r_1\) and \(r_1+\dd r_1\), with absolute
luminosity between \(L\) and \(L+\dd L\), is
\begin{equation}
  \dd N
  =
  4\pi a^3(t_1)(1-kr_1^2)^{-1/2}r_1^2
  n(t_1,L)\,\dd r_1\,\dd L.
  \tag{14.7.1}\label{eq:14.7.1}
\end{equation}
The coordinates \(r_1\) and \(t_1\) are related according to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.3.1}, which we can write as
\begin{equation}
  r_1=r(t_1),
  \tag{14.7.2}\label{eq:14.7.2}
\end{equation}
where \(r(t)\) is a function defined by the formula
\begin{equation}
  \int_t^{t_0}\frac{\dd t'}{a(t')}
  =
  \int_0^{r(t)}(1-kr^2)^{-1/2}\,\dd r.
  \tag{14.7.3}\label{eq:14.7.3}
\end{equation}
Differentiation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.3} gives
\[
  \dd r_1
  =
  -(1-kr_1^2)^{1/2}\frac{\dd t_1}{a(t_1)},
\]
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.1} can therefore be written
\begin{equation}
  \dd N
  =
  4\pi a^2(t_1)r^2(t_1)n(t_1,L)\,
  \lvert\dd t_1\rvert\,\dd L.
  \tag{14.7.4}\label{eq:14.7.4}
\end{equation}

The red shift and apparent luminosity of a source of absolute luminosity
\(L\) at \(r_1,t_1\) are given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.3.6} and
\eqref{eq:14.4.12}:
\begin{equation}
  z=\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}-1,
  \tag{14.7.5}\label{eq:14.7.5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  l
  =
  \frac{La^2(t_1)}{4\pi r_1^2a^4(t_0)}.
  \tag{14.7.6}\label{eq:14.7.6}
\end{equation}
Hence the number of sources with red shift less than \(z\) and apparent
luminosity greater than \(l\) is given by the integral of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.4} over all \(L\) and a finite range of \(t_1\):
\begin{equation}
  N(<z,>l)
  =
  \int_0^\infty\dd L
  \int_{\max\{t_z,t_l(L)\}}^{t_0}
  4\pi r^2(t_1)a^2(t_1)n(t_1,L)\,\dd t_1,
  \tag{14.7.7}\label{eq:14.7.7}
\end{equation}
where the lower limits, set by the conditions on red shift and apparent
luminosity, are defined by
\begin{equation}
  a(t_z)=\frac{a(t_0)}{1+z},
  \tag{14.7.8}\label{eq:14.7.8}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{r^2(t_l)}{a^2(t_l)}
  =
  \frac{L}{4\pi l\,a^4(t_0)}.
  \tag{14.7.9}\label{eq:14.7.9}
\end{equation}
If red shifts are not observed, then the quantity of interest is the number
\(N(>l)\) of sources with apparent luminosity greater than \(l\), which can
be calculated by taking the lower limit in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.7} to be just
\(t_l(L)\). If apparent luminosities are not observed, then the quantity of
interest is the number \(N(<z)\) of sources with red shift less than \(z\),
which can be calculated by taking the lower limit in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.7} to be just \(t_z\). (However, the observed number
counts can only be used to put a lower bound on \(N(<z)\), not to measure
it, because any given optical or radio telescope will only detect sources
above some minimum brightness.)

Radio telescopes do not measure total apparent luminosities, but instead
measure the \emph{flux density} \(S\), the power per unit antenna area and
per unit frequency interval, at a fixed frequency. The flux density of a
source at \(r_1,t_1\) is
\begin{equation}
  S(\nu)
  =
  \frac{
    P\!\left(\nu a(t_0)/a(t_1)\right)a(t_1)
  }{
    a^3(t_0)r_1^2
  },
  \tag{14.7.10}\label{eq:14.7.10}
\end{equation}
where \(P\) is the \emph{intrinsic power}, the power emitted per unit solid
angle and per unit frequency interval. (See Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.4.35}, with
\(S\equiv l'\), \(P\equiv L'/4\pi\).)

Following the same derivation that led to Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.7}, we find
that the number of sources with red shift less than \(z\) and flux density
at frequency \(\nu\) greater than \(S\) is given by
\begin{align}
  N(<z,>S;\nu)
  =
  \int_0^\infty\dd P
  \int_{\max\{t_z,t_S(P)\}}^{t_0}
  4\pi r^2(t_1)a^2(t_1)\,
  n\!\left(
    t_1,P,\nu\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}
  \right)
  \dd t_1.
  \tag{14.7.11}\label{eq:14.7.11}
\end{align}
Here \(t_S(P)\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{r^2(t_S)}{a(t_S)}
  =
  \frac{P}{S\,a^3(t_0)}.
  \tag{14.7.12}\label{eq:14.7.12}
\end{equation}

The analysis of radio source counts is very much simplified by the
observation\hyperref[ch14-ref-62]{\textsuperscript{62}} that such radio
sources generally have ``straight'' spectra,
\begin{equation}
  P\propto\nu^{-\alpha},
  \tag{14.7.13}\label{eq:14.7.13}
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha\), the spectral index, is about 0.7 to 0.8. In this case, the
number of sources with intrinsic power at frequency \(\nu\) between \(P\)
and \(P+\dd P\) is of the form
\[
  n(t,P,\nu)\,\dd P
  =
  n\!\left(
    t,P\left[\frac{\nu}{\nu_0}\right]^\alpha,\nu_0
  \right)
  \dd\!\left(
    P\left[\frac{\nu}{\nu_0}\right]^\alpha
  \right),
\]
where \(\nu_0\) is any arbitrary fixed frequency. The number density then
obeys the scaling rule
\begin{equation}
  n(t,P,\nu)
  =
  \left(\frac{\nu}{\nu_0}\right)^\alpha
  n\!\left(
    t,P\left[\frac{\nu}{\nu_0}\right]^\alpha,\nu_0
  \right).
  \tag{14.7.14}\label{eq:14.7.14}
\end{equation}
By changing the variable of integration in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.11} from
\(P\) to \(P[a(t_0)/a(t_1)]^\alpha\), we can refer the number density within
the integral to the fixed frequency \(\nu\), and find
\begin{equation}
  N(<z,>S;\nu)
  =
  \int_0^\infty\dd P
  \int_{\max\{t_z,t_{S\alpha}(P)\}}^{t_0}
  4\pi r^2(t_1)a^2(t_1)n(t_1,P,\nu)\,\dd t_1,
  \tag{14.7.15}\label{eq:14.7.15}
\end{equation}
where \(t_{S\alpha}(P)\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
  r^2(t_{S\alpha})
  \left[
    \frac{a(t_{S\alpha})}{a(t_0)}
  \right]^{1-\alpha}
  =
  \frac{P}{S\,a^2(t_0)}.
  \tag{14.7.16}\label{eq:14.7.16}
\end{equation}
The number counts will now obey the scaling rule
\begin{equation}
  N(<z,>S;\nu)
  =
  N\!\left(
    <z,>S\left[\frac{\nu}{\nu_0}\right]^\alpha;\nu_0
  \right).
  \tag{14.7.17}\label{eq:14.7.17}
\end{equation}
To the extent that Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.17} is verified by observation, we
can conclude that all sources do have the ``straight'' spectrum
\eqref{eq:14.7.13}, with the same spectral index \(\alpha\).

If there were no creation, destruction, or evolution of the sources during
the time it takes for light to reach us from the furthest observed source,
then \(n(t,L)\) and \(n(t,P,\nu)\) would have the simple time dependence
\eqref{eq:14.2.17}:
\begin{equation}
  n(t,L)
  =
  \left[\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t)}\right]^3n(t_0,L),
  \tag{14.7.18}\label{eq:14.7.18}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  n(t,P,\nu)
  =
  \left[\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t)}\right]^3n(t_0,P,\nu).
  \tag{14.7.19}\label{eq:14.7.19}
\end{equation}
In this case, the observed number counts could be used to gain information
about \(k\) and \(a(t)\). Alternatively, if we had a cosmological model for
\(k\) and \(a(t)\), we could use the observed number counts to deduce the
functional dependence of the number density \(n\) on \(t\) and \(L\) or
\(P\).

A good deal of insight into the results to be expected from these two
different modes of analysis can be gained by concentrating on the special
cases where \(z\) is small or \(l\) or \(S\) is large. The lower limits on
the \(t_1\)-integral in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.7.7} and \eqref{eq:14.7.11} are
then close to \(t_0\), so we can use the general expansions
\eqref{eq:14.6.1} and \eqref{eq:14.6.6}:
\[
  a(t_1)
  =
  a(t_0)
  \left[
    1-H_0(t_0-t_1)+\cdots
  \right],
\]
\[
  r(t_1)
  =
  a^{-1}(t_0)(t_0-t_1)
  \left[
    1+\frac12H_0(t_0-t_1)+\cdots
  \right].
\]
We will also express the number densities as expansions in \(t_0-t_1\):
\begin{equation}
  n(t_1,L)
  =
  n(t_0,L)
  \left[
    1-\beta_0(L)H_0(t_0-t_1)+\cdots
  \right],
  \tag{14.7.20}\label{eq:14.7.20}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
  n\!\left(
    t_1,P,\nu\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}
  \right)
  =
  n(t_0,P,\nu)
  \bigl\{
    1
    -[\beta_0(P,\nu)+2\alpha_0(P,\nu)]
    H_0(t_0-t_1)
    +\cdots
  \bigr\},
  \tag{14.7.21}\label{eq:14.7.21}
\end{align}
where \(\beta_0\) measures the rate of change of source density,
\begin{equation}
  \beta_0(L)
  \equiv
  H_0^{-1}
  \left.
    \partial_t\ln n(t,L)
  \right|_{t=t_0},
  \tag{14.7.22}\label{eq:14.7.22}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \beta_0(P,\nu)
  \equiv
  H_0^{-1}
  \left.
    \partial_t\ln n(t,P,\nu)
  \right|_{t=t_0},
  \tag{14.7.23}\label{eq:14.7.23}
\end{equation}
and \(\alpha_0\) is an effective spectral index,
\begin{equation}
  2\alpha_0(P,\nu)
  \equiv
  -\nu\,\partial_\nu\ln n(t_0,P,\nu).
  \tag{14.7.24}\label{eq:14.7.24}
\end{equation}
(The motivation for this last definition will be made clear below.) Then,
for \(t\) close to \(t_0\),
\begin{align}
  \int_t^{t_0}
  4\pi r^2(t_1)a^2(t_1)n(t_1,L)\,\dd t_1
  =
  \frac{4\pi}{3}n(t_0,L)(t_0-t)^3
  \biggl\{
    1
    -\frac34[\beta_0(L)+1]H_0(t_0-t)
    +\cdots
  \biggr\},
  \tag{14.7.25}\label{eq:14.7.25}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  \int_t^{t_0}
  &4\pi r^2(t_1)a^2(t_1)
  n\!\left(
    t_1,P,\nu\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_1)}
  \right)
  \dd t_1
  \notag\\
  &=
  \frac{4\pi}{3}n(t_0,P,\nu)(t_0-t)^3
  \biggl\{
    1
    -\frac34[
      \beta_0(P,\nu)+2\alpha_0(P,\nu)+1
    ]H_0(t_0-t)
    +\cdots
  \biggr\}.
  \tag{14.7.26}\label{eq:14.7.26}
\end{align}

If \(z\) is small, then the lower limit on the integral
\eqref{eq:14.7.25} is determined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.8}, which gives
\[
  H_0(t_0-t_z)
  =
  z-\left(1+\frac{q_0}{2}\right)z^2+\cdots.
\]
Hence Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.7} gives the number of sources with red shift less
than \(z\) as
\begin{equation}
  N(<z)
  =
  \frac{4\pi}{3}H_0^{-3}z^3
  \int_0^\infty
  n(t_0,L)
  \left\{
    1-\frac34[\beta_0(L)+2q_0+5]z+\cdots
  \right\}
  \dd L.
  \tag{14.7.27}\label{eq:14.7.27}
\end{equation}

If \(l\) is large, then the lower limit on the integral
\eqref{eq:14.7.25} is determined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.9}, which gives
\[
  t_0-t_l(L)
  =
  \left(\frac{L}{4\pi l}\right)^{1/2}
  \left\{
    1
    -\frac32
    \left(\frac{LH_0^2}{4\pi l}\right)^{1/2}
    +\cdots
  \right\}.
\]
Now Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.7} gives the number of sources with apparent
luminosity greater than \(l\) as
\begin{align}
  N(>l)
  =
  \frac{4\pi}{3}(4\pi l)^{-3/2}
  \int_0^\infty
  n(t_0,L)
  \left\{
    1
    -\frac34[\beta_0(L)+7]
    \left(\frac{LH_0^2}{4\pi l}\right)^{1/2}
    +\cdots
  \right\}
  L^{3/2}\,\dd L.
  \tag{14.7.28}\label{eq:14.7.28}
\end{align}

Finally, if \(S\) is large, then the lower limit on the integral
\eqref{eq:14.7.26} is determined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.12}, which gives
\[
  t_0-t_S(P)
  =
  \left(\frac{P}{S}\right)^{1/2}
  \left\{
    1
    -\left(\frac{PH_0^2}{S}\right)^{1/2}
    +\cdots
  \right\}.
\]
Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.11} gives the numbers of sources with flux density
at frequency \(\nu\) greater than \(S\) as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  N(>S,\nu)
  &=
  \frac{4\pi}{3}S^{-3/2}
  \int_0^\infty
  n(t_0,P,\nu)P^{3/2}
  \\[-2pt]
  &\quad{}\times
  \biggl\{
    1
    -\frac34[
      \beta_0(P,\nu)+2\alpha_0(P,\nu)+5
    ]
  \\[-2pt]
  &\hspace{7.5em}{}
    \left(\frac{PH_0^2}{S}\right)^{1/2}
    +\cdots
  \biggr\}
  \dd P.
\end{aligned}
\tag{14.7.29}\label{eq:14.7.29}
\end{equation}
If all sources have the ``straight'' spectrum \eqref{eq:14.7.13}, then
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.7.14} and \eqref{eq:14.7.24} give
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_0(P,\nu)
  =
  \frac{\alpha}{2}
  \left[
    1+P\,\partial_P\ln n(t_0,P,\nu)
  \right],
  \tag{14.7.30}\label{eq:14.7.30}
\end{equation}
so integration by parts allows us to make the substitution
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_0(P,\nu)\longrightarrow\alpha.
  \tag{14.7.31}\label{eq:14.7.31}
\end{equation}
That is, the ``effective spectral index'' \(\alpha_0\) may be replaced with
\(\alpha\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.29}, provided that sources have the
spectrum \(P\propto\nu^{-\alpha}\).

Inspection of these results shows that measured values of \(N(<z)\) for
\(z\ll1\) could be used to deduce the deceleration parameter \(q_0\) if we
knew the evolutionary parameter \(\beta_0\), while, in contrast,
measurement of \(N(>l)\) or \(N(>S,\nu)\) for large \(l\) or \(S\) cannot
tell us anything about \(q_0\), whatever we assume for \(\beta_0\).

If we assume no evolution, then \(n\) has a time dependence given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.18} or \eqref{eq:14.7.19} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.6.1}, so Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.7.22} and
\eqref{eq:14.7.23} give
\begin{equation}
  \beta_0(L)=\beta_0(P,\nu)=-3
  \qquad\text{(no evolution).}
  \tag{14.7.32}\label{eq:14.7.32}
\end{equation}
In this case, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.7.27}--\eqref{eq:14.7.29} give
\begin{equation}
  N(<z)
  =
  \frac{4\pi}{3}H_0^{-3}z^3
  \int_0^\infty n(t_0,L)\,\dd L
  \left\{
    1-\frac32(q_0+1)z+\cdots
  \right\},
  \tag{14.7.33}\label{eq:14.7.33}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  N(>l)
  =
  \frac{4\pi}{3}(4\pi l)^{-3/2}
  \int_0^\infty
  n(t_0,L)
  \left\{
    1
    -3\left(\frac{LH_0^2}{4\pi l}\right)^{1/2}
    +\cdots
  \right\}
  L^{3/2}\,\dd L,
  \tag{14.7.34}\label{eq:14.7.34}
\end{equation}
and, for straight spectra,
\begin{align}
  N(>S,\nu)
  =
  \frac{4\pi}{3}S^{-3/2}
  \int_0^\infty
  n(t_0,P,\nu)
  \left\{
    1
    -\frac32(\alpha+1)
    \left(\frac{PH_0^2}{S}\right)^{1/2}
    +\cdots
  \right\}
  P^{3/2}\,\dd P.
  \tag{14.7.35}\label{eq:14.7.35}
\end{align}
Thus the neglect of evolution leads to the definite predictions that
\(N(>l)\) must decrease with \(l\) more slowly than \(l^{-3/2}\), and,
since \(\alpha\) is positive, \(N(>S,\nu)\) must decrease with \(S\) more
slowly than \(S^{-3/2}\).

However, this result appears to be contradicted by
observation.\hyperref[ch14-ref-69]{\textsuperscript{69}} The relevant radio
source surveys are listed in Table~\ref{tab:14.1}. Jointly and severally,
they yield a number-count
function\hyperref[ch14-ref-63]{\textsuperscript{63}} \(N(>S,\nu)\) that
decreases with \(S\) (for
\(S>5\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,Hz^{-1}}\)) roughly like
\(S^{-1.8}\), and definitely more rapidly than \(S^{-3/2}\). We are forced
to the conclusion that evolution is important. According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.7.29}, the decrease of \(S^{3/2}N(>S,\nu)\) with \(S\)
requires that
\begin{equation}
  \beta_0<-2\alpha_0-5\simeq-6.5,
  \tag{14.7.36}\label{eq:14.7.36}
\end{equation}
so that the number density of sources must be decreasing faster than
\(a(t)^{-6.5}\).

A similar conclusion is reached from the study of number counts of radio
sources as a function of their angular diameters. From a study of the
distribution of the sizes of the radio sources in the 3C catalogue, Longair
and Pooley\hyperref[ch14-ref-64]{\textsuperscript{64}} calculated the
distribution in angular diameters to be expected for the fainter sources in
the 5C catalogue if no evolutionary effects are important. Their result does
not agree with observation for any \(q_0\), indicating an evolutionary
decrease of the proper source density.

If evolution is as substantial as indicated by these source counts, we
evidently cannot use the source counts to learn much about \(a(t)\). We
shall return to the program of using source counts to learn about source
evolution in the next chapter, when we have a dynamical model for \(a(t)\).
