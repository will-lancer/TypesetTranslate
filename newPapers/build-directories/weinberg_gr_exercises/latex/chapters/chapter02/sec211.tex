\subsection{Relativistic Imperfect Fluids\optionalreading}\label{sec:2.11}
\begingroup
\renewcommand\thefootnote{*}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of
development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}
\endgroup

The last section dealt with a perfect fluid, in which mean free paths and times
are so short that perfect isotropy is maintained about any point moving with the
fluid. In practice, one often has to deal with somewhat imperfect fluids, in
which the pressure, density, or velocity vary appreciably over distances of the
order of a mean free path, or over times of the order of a mean free time, or
both. In such fluids, thermal equilibrium is not strictly maintained, and the
fluid kinetic energy is dissipated as heat.

The correct treatment of dissipative effects for relativistic fluids raises
certain delicate questions of principle, which do not arise in the
nonrelativistic case. For this reason, and also because dissipation plays an
increasingly important role in theories of the early universe (see
Sections~15.8, 15.10, 15.11), it will be worth our while here to develop the
outlines of the general theory of relativistic imperfect fluids.

We suppose that the presence of weak space-time gradients in an imperfect fluid
has the effect of modifying the energy-momentum tensor and particle-current
vector by terms \(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\) and \(\Delta N^\mu\), which are of first
order in these gradients. Instead of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.10.7} and
\eqref{eq:2.10.13}, we then have
\begin{equation}
  T^{\mu\nu}=p\eta^{\mu\nu}
  +(p+\rho)U^\mu U^\nu+\Delta T^{\mu\nu},
  \tag{2.11.1}\label{eq:2.11.1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  N^\mu=nU^\mu+\Delta N^\mu .
  \tag{2.11.2}\label{eq:2.11.2}
\end{equation}
Once we allow such correction terms, the definitions of the pressure \(p\),
energy density \(\rho\), particle density \(n\), and fluid velocity \(U^\mu\)
become somewhat ambiguous. The general practice is to define \(\rho\) and
\(n\) as the total energy density and particle number density in a comoving
frame:
\begin{equation}
  T^{00}\equiv\rho ,
  \tag{2.11.3}\label{eq:2.11.3}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  N^0\equiv n ,
  \tag{2.11.4}\label{eq:2.11.4}
\end{equation}
a comoving frame being characterized by the condition that at a given point,
the velocity four-vector is
\begin{equation}
  U^i=0,\qquad U^0=1 .
  \tag{2.11.5}\label{eq:2.11.5}
\end{equation}
In addition, the pressure \(p\) is generally defined to be the same function of
\(\rho\) and \(n\) [e.g., Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.10.27}] as in the case where all
fluid gradients are negligible and dissipation is absent. Finally, it is
necessary in a relativistic fluid to specify whether \(U^\mu\) is the velocity
of energy transport or particle transport. In the approach of Landau and
Lifshitz,\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-5]{5}} \(U^\mu\) is taken to be
the velocity of energy transport, so that \(T^{i0}\) vanishes in a comoving
frame. In the approach of Eckart,\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-6]{6}}
\(U^\mu\) is taken to be the velocity of particle transport, so that it is
\(N^i\) that vanishes in a comoving frame. The two approaches are perfectly
equivalent, but Eckart's seems to me to be slightly more convenient, and will be
adopted here. With this definition of \(U^\mu\), we then have in a comoving
frame
\begin{equation}
  N^i=0 .
  \tag{2.11.6}\label{eq:2.11.6}
\end{equation}
A comparison of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.11.3}--\eqref{eq:2.11.6} with
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.11.1} and \eqref{eq:2.11.2} shows that in a comoving frame,
the dissipative terms \(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\) and \(\Delta N^\mu\) are subject
to the constraints
\begin{equation}
  \Delta T^{00}=\Delta N^0=\Delta N^i=0 ,
  \tag{2.11.7}\label{eq:2.11.7}
\end{equation}
and therefore, in a general Lorentz frame,
\begin{equation}
  U_\mu U_\nu\Delta T^{\mu\nu}=0 ,
  \tag{2.11.8}\label{eq:2.11.8}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Delta N^\mu=0 .
  \tag{2.11.9}\label{eq:2.11.9}
\end{equation}
All effects of dissipation thus show up as contributions to
\(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\). Our task is now to construct the most general possible
dissipative tensor \(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\) allowed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.8}
and by the second law of thermodynamics.

To this end, let us calculate the entropy produced by fluid motions. As in the
last sections, we start by contracting the conservation law
\eqref{eq:2.8.7} with \(U_\mu\):
\begin{equation}
  0=U_\mu\partial_\nu T^{\mu\nu}.
  \tag{2.11.10}\label{eq:2.11.10}
\end{equation}
By following the same reasoning that was used to derive
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.10.19} for a perfect fluid, one sees that in general
\[
  U_\mu\partial_\nu
  \left[p\eta^{\mu\nu}+(p+\rho)U^\mu U^\nu\right]
  =-kT\,\partial_\mu(n\sigma U^\mu),
\]
where \(T\) and \(\sigma k\) are the temperature and entropy per particle,
defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.10.18}. Hence Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.10} now reads
\[
  \partial_\mu(n\sigma U^\mu)
  =\frac1{kT}U_\mu\partial_\nu\Delta T^{\mu\nu},
\]
or equivalently
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\mu S^\mu
  =-\frac1T(\partial_\nu U_\mu)\Delta T^{\mu\nu}
   +\frac1{T^2}(\partial_\nu T)U_\mu\Delta T^{\mu\nu},
  \tag{2.11.11}\label{eq:2.11.11}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  S^\mu\equiv nk\sigma U^\mu
  -T^{-1}U_\nu\Delta T^{\mu\nu}.
  \tag{2.11.12}\label{eq:2.11.12}
\end{equation}
The entropy density in a comoving frame is \(nk\sigma=S^0\), so we may
interpret \(S^\mu\) as the entropy-current four-vector, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.11} thus gives the rate of entropy production per unit
volume. The second law of thermodynamics then requires that
\(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\) be a linear combination of velocity and temperature
gradients, such that the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.11} is
\emph{positive} for all possible fluid configurations. Note that this is only
possible because we have included the second term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.12};
without this term, \(\partial_\mu S^\mu\) would not be simply quadratic in
first derivatives, and hence could not be positive for all fluid
configurations. Note also that \(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\) is not allowed to involve
gradients of \(p,\rho,n\), and so on, because if it did then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.11} would contain products of pressure or density gradients
with velocity or temperature gradients, and, again, these products would not be
positive for all fluid configurations.

It is convenient at this point to go over to a comoving frame, in which
\(U^\mu\) has the form \eqref{eq:2.11.5} at a given space-time point \(P\).
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.10.17}, it follows that in this frame, all gradients of
\(U^0\) vanish at \(P\). Setting \(U^i,\partial_\mu U^0\), and
\(\Delta T^{00}\) equal to zero in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.11}, we find that in a
Lorentz frame comoving at \(P\), the rate of entropy production per unit volume
at \(P\) is
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\mu S^\mu
  =-\left(\frac{\dot U_i}{T}
    +\frac1{T^2}\partial_iT\right)\Delta T^{i0}
   -\frac1T(\partial_jU_i)\Delta T^{ij}.
  \tag{2.11.13}\label{eq:2.11.13}
\end{equation}
In order for this to be positive for all possible fluid configurations, we must
have
\begin{equation}
  \Delta T^{i0}
  =-\chi\left(\partial_iT+T\dot U_i\right),
  \tag{2.11.14}\label{eq:2.11.14}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Delta T^{ij}
  =-\eta\left(
      \partial_jU_i+\partial_iU_j
      -\frac23\delta_{ij}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U
    \right)
   -\zeta\,\delta_{ij}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U ,
  \tag{2.11.15}\label{eq:2.11.15}
\end{equation}
with positive coefficients
\begin{equation}
  \chi\geq0,\qquad\eta\geq0,\qquad\zeta\geq0 ,
  \tag{2.11.16}\label{eq:2.11.16}
\end{equation}
so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.13} reads
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \partial_\mu S^\mu
  ={}&\frac{\chi}{T^2}
      \bigl(\boldsymbol{\nabla}T+T\dot{\mathbf U}\bigr)^2\\
    &+\frac{\eta}{2T}
      \left(
        \partial_jU_i+\partial_iU_j
        -\frac23\delta_{ij}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U
      \right)
      \left(
        \partial_jU_i+\partial_iU_j
        -\frac23\delta_{ij}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U
      \right)\\
    &+\frac{\zeta}{T}
      \bigl(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U\bigr)^2
  \geq0 .
\end{aligned}
  \tag{2.11.17}\label{eq:2.11.17}
\end{equation}
Except for the relativistic correction \(T\dot{\mathbf U}\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.14}, the form of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.11.14} and
\eqref{eq:2.11.15} is the same as in the nonrelativistic theory of imperfect
fluids,\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-5]{5}} and we therefore may identify
\(\chi,\eta,\zeta\) as the coefficients of \emph{heat conduction},
\emph{shear viscosity}, and \emph{bulk viscosity}.

It now only remains to translate our results from the forms
\eqref{eq:2.11.5}, \eqref{eq:2.11.7}, \eqref{eq:2.11.14}, and
\eqref{eq:2.11.15}, which are valid only in comoving frames, to forms valid
in general Lorentz frames. Let us define a \emph{shear tensor},
\begin{equation}
  W_{\mu\nu}\equiv
  \partial_\mu U_\nu+\partial_\nu U_\mu
  -\frac23\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_\rho U^\rho ,
  \tag{2.11.18}\label{eq:2.11.18}
\end{equation}
a \emph{heat-flow vector},
\begin{equation}
  Q_\mu\equiv\partial_\mu T
  +T(\partial_\nu U_\mu)U^\nu ,
  \tag{2.11.19}\label{eq:2.11.19}
\end{equation}
and a \emph{projection tensor} on the hyperplane normal to \(U^\mu\):
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mu\nu}\equiv\eta_{\mu\nu}+U_\mu U_\nu .
  \tag{2.11.20}\label{eq:2.11.20}
\end{equation}
It is straightforward to check that in a comoving Lorentz frame, our formulas
\eqref{eq:2.11.7}, \eqref{eq:2.11.14}, and \eqref{eq:2.11.15} for
\(\Delta T^{\mu\nu}\) are satisfied by the tensor
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \Delta T^{\mu\nu}={}&
  -\eta H^{\mu\rho}H^{\nu\sigma}W_{\rho\sigma}\\
  &-\chi\left(H^{\mu\rho}U^\nu+H^{\nu\rho}U^\mu\right)Q_\rho
  -\zeta H^{\mu\nu}\partial_\rho U^\rho .
\end{aligned}
  \tag{2.11.21}\label{eq:2.11.21}
\end{equation}
Since this formula is Lorentz-invariant and valid in a comoving Lorentz frame,
it is valid in all Lorentz frames.

In general, the coefficients \(\chi T,\eta,\zeta\) might be expected on
dimensional grounds to be of the order of the pressure, or the thermal energy
density, times some sort of mean free time. However, there are important special
cases\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-8]{8}} in which the bulk viscosity
\(\zeta\) is much smaller than \(\eta\) or \(\chi T\). To see when this
applies, note that Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.11.1} and \eqref{eq:2.11.21} give the
trace of the total energy-momentum tensor as
\begin{equation}
  T^\mu{}_\mu=3p-\rho-3\zeta\,\partial_\mu U^\mu .
  \tag{2.11.22}\label{eq:2.11.22}
\end{equation}
Suppose that we are dealing with a medium for which this trace can be expressed
as a function of \(\rho\) and \(n\) alone:
\begin{equation}
  T^\mu{}_\mu=f(\rho,n).
  \tag{2.11.23}\label{eq:2.11.23}
\end{equation}
For instance, for the simple gas characterized by Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.10.20},
this trace is
\[
  T^\mu{}_\mu
  =-\sum_N\frac{m^2}{E_N}\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_N).
\]
In the extreme relativistic case, we have \(E_N\gg m\), so in this case
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.23} is satisfied, with
\[
  f(\rho,n)\simeq0 .
\]
In the nonrelativistic case, we have
\[
  \frac1{E_N}\simeq\frac1m-\frac{E_N-m}{m^2},
\]
so in this case Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.23} is again satisfied, with
\[
  f(\rho,n)\simeq-mn+(\rho-mn).
\]
In the absence of velocity gradients, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.11.22} and
\eqref{eq:2.11.23} would give a formula for the pressure
\begin{equation}
  p=\frac13\bigl[\rho+f(\rho,n)\bigr].
  \tag{2.11.24}\label{eq:2.11.24}
\end{equation}
But we have agreed to define \(p\) in general as the same function of
\(\rho\) and \(n\) as in the absence of dissipation, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.24} must hold even in the presence of velocity gradients,
and therefore Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.11.22}, \eqref{eq:2.11.23}, and
\eqref{eq:2.11.24} give
\begin{equation}
  \zeta=0 .
  \tag{2.11.25}\label{eq:2.11.25}
\end{equation}
It would be wrong, however, to conclude that \(\zeta\) is generally
negligible. As we have seen, the trace of the energy-momentum tensor for a
simple gas is a function of \(\rho\) and \(n\) only in the extreme relativistic
or extreme nonrelativistic limit; for \(kT\) of order \(m\),
\(T^\mu{}_\mu\) cannot be expressed in the form \eqref{eq:2.11.23}, and the
bulk viscosity is of the same order as the shear
viscosity.\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-7]{7}} The bulk viscosity is also
important\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-8]{8}} in a fluid that allows an
easy exchange of energy between translational and internal degrees of freedom,
as in the case of a gas of rough
spheres.\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-9]{9}} Another case, of particular
importance to cosmology, is that of a material medium with very short mean free
times, interacting with radiation quanta with a finite mean free time \(\tau\).
In this case, the coefficients of heat conduction, shear viscosity, and bulk
viscosity are calculated to be\textsuperscript{\hyperref[ch2-ref-10]{10}}
\begin{equation}
  \chi=\frac43aT^3\tau ,
  \tag{2.11.26}\label{eq:2.11.26}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \eta=\frac4{15}aT^4\tau ,
  \tag{2.11.27}\label{eq:2.11.27}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \zeta=4aT^4\tau
  \left[
    \frac13-\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_n
  \right]^2,
  \tag{2.11.28}\label{eq:2.11.28}
\end{equation}
where \(a\) is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, defined so that the radiation
energy density is \(aT^4\), and \(p\) and \(\rho\) are the total pressure and
energy density of the matter and radiation. Note that, in general,
\(\chi T,\eta,\zeta\) are comparable, but if the pressure and thermal energy
are dominated by radiation, then
\((\partial p/\partial\rho)_n\simeq\tfrac13\) and, as expected, the bulk
viscosity will be small.
