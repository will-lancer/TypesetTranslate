\subsection{The Formation of Galaxies}\label{sec:15.8}

In the last two sections we have considered two constituents of the present
universe---helium and the microwave background---which may be relics of an
earlier era of cosmic history. Looking at the night sky, we see one other
possible relic---the clumping of stars into clusters, galaxies, and clusters
of galaxies. It is natural to interpret this clumping as the effect of
gravitational attraction acting on initially uniform diffuse matter, as first
suggested by Newton in a famous letter to Dr.\ Richard Bentley.%
\hyperref[ch15-ref-143]{\textsuperscript{143}} Unfortunately, we still do
not have even a tentative quantitative theory of the formation of galaxies,
anywhere near so complete and plausible as our theories of the origin of the
cosmic abundance of helium or the microwave background.

The first serious theory of galaxy formation was proposed by Sir James Jeans
early in this century.\hyperref[ch15-ref-144]{\textsuperscript{144}} Jeans
supposed the universe to be filled with a nonrelativistic fluid, with mass
density \(\rho\), pressure \(p\), velocity \(\mathbf v\), and gravitational
field \(\mathbf g\), governed by the equation of continuity
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t\rho
  +
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)
  =
  0,
  \tag{15.8.1}\label{eq:15.8.1}
\end{equation}
the Euler equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t\mathbf v
  +
  (\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla})\mathbf v
  =
  -\frac1\rho\boldsymbol{\nabla}p+\mathbf g,
  \tag{15.8.2}\label{eq:15.8.2}
\end{equation}
and the gravitational field equations
\begin{align}
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf g
  &=0,
  \tag{15.8.3}\label{eq:15.8.3}\\
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf g
  &=-4\pi G\rho.
  \tag{15.8.4}\label{eq:15.8.4}
\end{align}
The effects of gravitation were ignored in the unperturbed ``solution,''
taken to be that for a static uniform fluid:
\[
  \rho=\text{constant},
  \qquad
  p=\text{constant},
  \qquad
  \mathbf v=0.
\]
If we add small perturbations
\(\rho_1,p_1,\mathbf v_1,\mathbf g_1\), then to first order
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.8.1}--\eqref{eq:15.8.4} become
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \partial_t\rho_1
    +\rho\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf v_1
    &=0,
    \\
    \partial_t\mathbf v_1
    &=
    -\frac{v_s^2}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\rho_1+\mathbf g_1,
    \\
    \boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf g_1
    &=0,
    \\
    \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf g_1
    &=-4\pi G\rho_1,
  \end{aligned}
\]
where \(v_s^2\) is the speed of sound,
\[
  v_s^2
  \simeq
  \frac{p_1}{\rho_1}
  =
  \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{adiabatic}},
\]
and all quantities that do not carry a subscript ``1'' are now understood to
refer to the unperturbed ``solution.'' Combining these equations gives a
differential equation for \(\rho_1\):
\[
  \partial_t^2\rho_1
  =
  v_s^2\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\rho_1
  +
  4\pi G\rho\rho_1.
\]
The solution takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \rho_1
  \propto
  \exp\{\ii\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\ii\omega t\},
  \tag{15.8.5}\label{eq:15.8.5}
\end{equation}
with \(\omega\) and \(\mathbf k\) related by the ``dispersion relation''
\begin{equation}
  \omega^2=k^2v_s^2-4\pi G\rho.
  \tag{15.8.6}\label{eq:15.8.6}
\end{equation}
This result bears a very close resemblance to the dispersion relation for
longitudinal electrostatic oscillations in a plasma,%
\hyperref[ch15-ref-49]{\textsuperscript{49}}
\begin{equation}
  \omega^2
  =
  k^2v_s^2+\frac{4\pi n_e e^2}{m_e},
  \tag{15.8.7}\label{eq:15.8.7}
\end{equation}
where \(e,m_e\), and \(n_e\) are the (unrationalized) charge, mass, and number
density of electrons. The difference between \eqref{eq:15.8.6} and
\eqref{eq:15.8.7} is that in \eqref{eq:15.8.6}, \(n_e\) is replaced with
the number density \(\rho/m\), \(m_e\) is replaced with \(m\), \(e^2\) is
replaced with the Newtonian ``coupling constant'' \(Gm^2\), and an extra
minus sign is inserted to take account of the attractive nature of
gravitation. Because of the minus sign in \eqref{eq:15.8.6}, the
``gravitostatic'' waves exhibit an instability that is not present in plasma
waves: \(\omega\) is imaginary for wave numbers below the critical value
\begin{equation}
  k_J
  =
  \left(\frac{4\pi G\rho}{v_s^2}\right)^{1/2},
  \tag{15.8.8}\label{eq:15.8.8}
\end{equation}
so that \(\rho_1\) can grow (or decay) exponentially, with an e-folding rate
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Im}\omega
  =
  v_s(k_J^2-k^2)^{1/2},
  \qquad
  \text{for \(k^2<k_J^2\)}.
  \tag{15.8.9}\label{eq:15.8.9}
\end{equation}

Unfortunately, Jeans's theory is not applicable to the formation of galaxies
in an expanding universe, because Jeans assumed a static medium, whereas the
rate of expansion of the universe is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.1.20} in all
cases of interest as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dot a}{a}
  \simeq
  \left(\frac{8\pi\rho G}{3}\right)^{1/2}
  =
  \left(\frac23\right)^{1/2}k_Jv_s,
  \tag{15.8.10}\label{eq:15.8.10}
\end{equation}
which is of the same order as the maximum value of the growth rate
\eqref{eq:15.8.9}. The first satisfactory theory of the instabilities of an
expanding universe was given in 1946 by Lifshitz.%
\hyperref[ch15-ref-145]{\textsuperscript{145}} He showed that disturbances
at wave numbers below \(k_J\) grow, not exponentially, but like a power of
\(t\) or of \(a(t)\). This result will be derived and discussed in detail
below, using both the nonrelativistic treatment suggested in 1957 by
Bonner\hyperref[ch15-ref-146]{\textsuperscript{146}} (Section~\ref{sec:15.9})
and a simplified version of Lifshitz's relativistic theory
(Section~\ref{sec:15.10}).

Although we are not yet in a position to determine the rates with which
disturbances actually grow, we can rather easily decide which disturbances
can grow and which cannot. For sufficiently large wave numbers, the waves
described by Jeans's theory become ordinary sound waves, with
\begin{equation}
  \omega^2=k^2v_s^2.
  \tag{15.8.11}\label{eq:15.8.11}
\end{equation}
What are the conditions for this simple dispersion relation to be valid?
Gravitational forces will be negligible if the gravitational energy of a
sphere of radius \(\lvert\mathbf k\rvert^{-1}\) is much smaller than its
thermal energy:
\[
  \frac{G(\rho\lvert\mathbf k\rvert^{-3})^2}
       {\lvert\mathbf k\rvert^{-1}}
  \ll
  \rho v_s^2\lvert\mathbf k\rvert^{-3}.
\]
Also, the expansion of the universe will have negligible effect if the
expansion rate is much less than the frequency:
\[
  \sqrt{G\rho}\ll\lvert\omega\rvert.
\]
Both of these conditions will be satisfied by the relation
\eqref{eq:15.8.11}, as long as the wave number satisfies the condition
\[
  \lvert\mathbf k\rvert\gg k_J,
\]
just as in Jeans's theory. Thus, even when the expansion of the universe is
taken into account, we expect there to be a critical wave number, of order
\(k_J\), above which disturbances cannot grow, but only oscillate like sound
waves.

Since the expansion of the universe causes \(\mathbf k\) to decrease as
\(1/a\), it is convenient to characterize disturbances by a constant, the
rest mass within a sphere of radius \(2\pi/\lvert\mathbf k\rvert\):
\begin{equation}
  M
  =
  \frac{4\pi nm_H}{3}
  \left(
    \frac{2\pi}{\lvert\mathbf k\rvert}
  \right)^3,
  \tag{15.8.12}\label{eq:15.8.12}
\end{equation}
where \(n\) is the hydrogen number density. According to the above analysis,
the only growing disturbances are those with wave number less than \(k_J\),
and hence with mass \(M\) greater than the Jeans mass
\begin{equation}
  M_J
  \equiv
  \frac{4\pi nm_H}{3}
  \left(\frac{2\pi}{k_J}\right)^3
  =
  \frac{4\pi nm_H}{3}
  \left[
    \frac{\pi v_s^2}{G(\rho+p)}
  \right]^{3/2}.
  \tag{15.8.13}\label{eq:15.8.13}
\end{equation}
(It proves convenient here to replace \(\rho\) with \(\rho+p\); this is
permissible, because \(M_J\) is used only in order-of-magnitude arguments,
and \(p\) is never more than \(\rho/3\).)

We can gain a good deal of insight into the history of a protogalactic
fluctuation by following the variations in \(M_J\) caused by the expansion
of the universe. (See Figure~\ref{fig:15.6}.)

From the time of \(e^+-e^-\) annihilation
\((T\simeq10^{10\,\circ}\mathrm K)\) until the time of recombination of
hydrogen \((T\simeq4000^\circ\mathrm K)\), it is a good approximation to
take the contents of the universe as nonrelativistic ionized hydrogen plus
black-body electromagnetic radiation, both in thermal equilibrium at
temperature \(T\). Also, since the photon entropy \(\sigma k\) is so large,
we may neglect the pressure, thermal energy, and entropy of the matter. The
total energy density, pressure, and specific entropy are then (aside from the
uncoupled neutrinos)
\begin{align}
  \rho
  &=
  nm_H+a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4,
  \tag{15.8.14}\label{eq:15.8.14}\\
  p
  &=
  \frac13a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4,
  \tag{15.8.15}\label{eq:15.8.15}\\
  \sigma
  &=
  \frac{4a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^3}{3nk}.
  \tag{15.8.16}\label{eq:15.8.16}
\end{align}

In an adiabatic disturbance \(\sigma\) is constant, so \(n\) varies as
\(T^3\), and hence
\[
  \delta\rho
  =
  [3nm_H+4a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4]\frac{\delta T}{T},
  \qquad
  \delta p
  =
  \left[\frac43a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4\right]\frac{\delta T}{T}.
\]
The speed of sound is therefore
\begin{equation}
  v_s^2
  =
  \left(\frac{\delta p}{\delta\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{adiabatic}}
  =
  \frac13
  \left(
    \frac{kT\sigma}{m_H+kT\sigma}
  \right),
  \tag{15.8.17}\label{eq:15.8.17}
\end{equation}
and the Jeans mass \eqref{eq:15.8.13} has the value
\begin{equation}
  M_J
  =
  \frac{
    2\pi^{5/2}k^2\sigma^2
  }{
    9a_{\mathrm{rad}}^{1/2}m_H^2G^{3/2}
    (1+\sigma kT/m_H)^3
  },
  \tag{15.8.18}\label{eq:15.8.18}
\end{equation}
or, in terms of the solar mass,
\begin{equation}
  M_J
  =
  906M_\odot\sigma^2
  \left(
    1+\frac{\sigma kT}{m_H}
  \right)^{-3}.
  \tag{15.8.19}\label{eq:15.8.19}
\end{equation}
Once the hydrogen recombines at \(T_R\simeq4000^\circ\mathrm K\), the
radiation pressure becomes ineffective, and the equations of state are those
for a monatomic ideal gas with \(\gamma=5/3\):
\begin{align}
  \rho
  &=
  nm_H+\frac32nkT,
  \tag{15.8.20}\label{eq:15.8.20}\\
  p
  &=
  nkT.
  \tag{15.8.21}\label{eq:15.8.21}
\end{align}
The speed of sound here has the familiar value
\begin{equation}
  v_s^2=\frac{5kT}{3m_H},
  \tag{15.8.22}\label{eq:15.8.22}
\end{equation}
and the Jeans mass \eqref{eq:15.8.13} has the value
\begin{equation}
  M_J
  =
  4\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)^{5/2}
  \left(\frac{5kT}{G}\right)^{3/2}
  n^{-1/2}m_H^{-2}.
  \tag{15.8.23}\label{eq:15.8.23}
\end{equation}
Just after recombination, the matter temperature \(T\) is the same as the
radiation temperature, so \(T\) can be expressed in terms of \(n\) and the
specific photon entropy \eqref{eq:15.8.16}; this gives
\begin{equation}
  M_J
  =
  \frac{
    2\pi^{5/2}5^{3/2}k^2\sigma^{1/2}
  }{
    9a_{\mathrm{rad}}^{1/2}m_H^2G^{3/2}
  }
  =
  102M_\odot\sigma^{1/2}.
  \tag{15.8.24}\label{eq:15.8.24}
\end{equation}
As long as no additional heat is put into the gas, its temperature will drop
as \(a^{-2}\) [see Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.16}], and since \(n\) drops as
\(a^{-3}\), the Jeans mass \eqref{eq:15.8.23} will decrease as
\(a^{-3/2}\).

We can now see how profound an effect the black-body radiation has on the
growth of fluctuations.\hyperref[ch15-ref-147]{\textsuperscript{147}} As
emphasized in Section~\ref{sec:15.5}, the present microwave temperature
\(2.7^\circ\mathrm K\) indicates that \(\sigma\) is very large, of order
\(10^8\) to \(10^9\). In consequence, the Jeans mass \eqref{eq:15.8.19}
starts at \(T=10^{9\,\circ}\mathrm K\) at a very low value
\(10^{13}M_\odot/\sigma\), of order \(10^5M_\odot\) to \(10^4M_\odot\);
then rises like \(T^{-3}\) until \(T\) reaches a temperature
\(m_H/k\sigma\), of order \(10^{5\,\circ}\mathrm K\) to
\(10^{4\,\circ}\mathrm K\); then levels off at a very high value
\(9\sigma^2M_\odot\), of order \(10^{17}M_\odot\) to \(10^{19}M_\odot\)
until the recombination of hydrogen; following this, \(M_J\) drops
precipitously to the value \eqref{eq:15.8.24}, of order \(10^6M_\odot\) to
\(3\times10^6M_\odot\); and it continues to drop as \(a^{-3/2}\)
thereafter. If we fix our attention on a particular fluctuation whose mass
\(M\) has the value \(M_G\simeq10^{11}M_\odot\) of a typical good-sized
present galaxy, we may distinguish three distinct phases in its growth:

\emph{(A)} The Jeans mass \eqref{eq:15.8.19} will be less than the galactic
mass until the temperature drops to the value
\begin{equation}
  T_A
  =
  \left(
    \frac{9M_\odot}{\sigma M_G}
  \right)^{1/3}
  \frac{m_H}{k}
  \simeq
  10^{7\,\circ}\mathrm K.
  \tag{15.8.25}\label{eq:15.8.25}
\end{equation}
During this period, the amplitude of the fluctuation will have a chance to
grow under the influence of its own gravitation. Since the total energy
density is dominated in this early phase by radiation, this is a relativistic
problem, and the growth rate must be calculated in a general-relativistic
formalism. It is shown in Section~\ref{sec:15.10} that the fastest-growing
normal modes have a density contrast \(\delta\rho/\rho\) that grows as \(t\).

\emph{(B)} From the time when \(T\) drops below the value
\eqref{eq:15.8.25} until the recombination of hydrogen at
\(T\simeq4000^\circ\mathrm K\), the Jeans mass will be larger than the
galactic mass, so the protogalactic disturbance will behave like a packet of
ordinary sound waves. No appreciable growth is possible during this phase.
For a relatively high present density, say of order
\(3\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), there is a long period before
recombination when \(\sigma kT<m_H\), so that the total energy density is
dominated by the hydrogen rest mass, and the protogalactic sound waves can be
treated by Newtonian mechanics (see Section~\ref{sec:15.9}). For a relatively
low present density, say of order \(10^{-30}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), we have
\(\sigma kT>m_H\) throughout virtually all of Phase B, so that a relativistic
treatment is required. (See Section~\ref{sec:15.10}.)

\emph{(C)} From the time of recombination until the present, the Jeans mass
will be much less than the galactic mass, so the fluctuation amplitude can
again grow. The total energy density in this phase is dominated by hydrogen
rest mass, so this is a nonrelativistic problem, and the growth rate can be
calculated by Newtonian methods. The density contrast \(\delta\rho/\rho\) is
shown in Section~\ref{sec:15.9} to grow roughly as \(t^{2/3}\).

There is one disappointing aspect of this general picture: Nothing so far
gives any clue to the reason for the observed galactic mass distribution.
(The Jeans mass just before recombination is very much larger than any
galactic mass, whereas the Jeans mass just after recombination appears to be
related to the mass of a globular
cluster\hyperref[ch15-ref-147a]{\textsuperscript{147a}} rather than a
galaxy.) Such a clue has recently appeared in
calculations\hyperref[ch15-ref-148]{\textsuperscript{148}} of the damping of
protogalactic fluctuations while they are undergoing essentially acoustic
oscillations in Phase B. Dissipation will be important whenever some
particle's mean free time is too long to maintain perfect thermal equilibrium.
For photons, the chief collision mechanism during Phase B is scattering by
nonrelativistic electrons, and the mean free time here is
\begin{equation}
  \tau_\gamma=\frac{1}{n\sigma_T},
  \tag{15.8.26}\label{eq:15.8.26}
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_T\) is the Thomson cross-section
\[
  \sigma_T
  \equiv
  \frac{8\pi e^4}{3m_e^2}
  =
  0.6652\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{cm^2}.
\]
In contrast, the mean free time of an electron or a proton, taking into
account only Coulomb collisions, will be of order
\[
  \tau_e
  \simeq
  \left[
    n\left(\frac{kT}{m_e}\right)^{1/2}
    \frac{e^4}{(kT)^2}
  \right]^{-1},
\]
which is shorter than \(\tau_\gamma\) by a factor of order
\((kT/m_e)^{3/2}\). Thus the dominant dissipative effects in Phase B will
arise from the failure of perfect thermal equilibrium between matter and
radiation, rather than from any dissipation in the matter itself. Also, for
any reasonable value of the present baryon number density, a fluctuation of
mass \(10^{11}M_\odot\) will have a radius
\(2\pi/\lvert\mathbf k\rvert\) much larger than the photon mean free path
\(\tau_\gamma\) throughout virtually all of Phase B, so the interaction
between matter and radiation can be treated to first order in
\(\tau_\gamma\). In this approximation, the medium of protons, electrons,
and photons behaves like an imperfect fluid (see Section~\ref{sec:2.11}),
with coefficients of shear viscosity, bulk viscosity, and heat conduction
given by\hyperref[ch15-ref-149]{\textsuperscript{149}}
\begin{align}
  \eta
  &=
  \frac4{15}a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4\tau_\gamma,
  \tag{15.8.27}\label{eq:15.8.27}\\
  \zeta
  &=
  4a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4\tau_\gamma
  \left[
    \frac13-
    \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_n
  \right]^2,
  \tag{15.8.28}\label{eq:15.8.28}\\
  \chi
  &=
  \frac43a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^3\tau_\gamma.
  \tag{15.8.29}\label{eq:15.8.29}
\end{align}
In general, a sound wave in an imperfect fluid will be damped at a
rate\hyperref[ch15-ref-149]{\textsuperscript{149}}
\begin{align}
  \Gamma
  =
  \frac{k^2}{2(\rho+p)}
  \Biggl\{
    \zeta+\frac43\eta
    &+
    \chi
    \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_n^{-2}
    \Biggl[
      \rho+p
      -2T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_n
      \notag\\[-2pt]
      &\quad
      +v_s^2T\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_n
      -\frac{n}{v_s^2}
       \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}\right)_T
    \Biggr]
  \Biggr\}.
  \tag{15.8.30}\label{eq:15.8.30}
\end{align}
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.8.14}, \eqref{eq:15.8.15}, and
\eqref{eq:15.8.26}--\eqref{eq:15.8.29} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.30} gives the
damping rate here as
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma
  =
  \frac{
    k^2a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4
  }{
    6n\sigma_T[nm_H+\tfrac43a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4]
  }
  \left\{
    \frac{16}{15}
    +
    \frac{
      n^2m_H^2
    }{
      a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4
      [nm_H+\tfrac43a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4]
    }
  \right\},
  \tag{15.8.31}\label{eq:15.8.31}
\end{equation}
the two terms in the brackets representing the effects of shear viscosity and
heat conduction, respectively. (The bulk viscosity \eqref{eq:15.8.28}
vanishes here, because with the matter pressure and thermal energy neglected,
\((\partial p/\partial\rho)_n\) is just \(1/3\).) Since
\(k^2\propto M^{-2/3}\), the protogalactic sound wave will be damped in
amplitude by a factor of form
\begin{equation}
  D
  \equiv
  \exp\left\{-\int_{t_A}^{t_R}\Gamma\dd t\right\}
  =
  \exp\left[
    -\left(\frac{M_c}{M}\right)^{2/3}
  \right],
  \tag{15.8.32}\label{eq:15.8.32}
\end{equation}
where \(M_c\) is some critical mass (and a subscript \(R\) denotes the time
of hydrogen recombination). For a relatively high present density, there was
a long period before recombination when the energy density was dominated by
hydrogen rest mass, so that
\[
  t\simeq(6\pi nm_HG)^{-1/2},
  \qquad
  \Gamma\simeq\frac{k^2}{6\sigma_Tn}\propto t^{2/3},
\]
and the critical mass in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.32} is
\begin{equation}
  M_c
  \simeq
  \frac{32\pi^4}{3}
  \left(\frac{m_H}{10\sigma_T}\right)^{3/2}
  (6\pi G)^{-3/4}
  (n_Rm_H)^{-5/4}.
  \tag{15.8.33}\label{eq:15.8.33}
\end{equation}
For instance, for a present mass density
\(3\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), the critical mass is
\(5\times10^{12}M_\odot\). For a relatively low present density, the energy
density during virtually all of Phase B was dominated by radiation (including
neutrinos), so that
\[
  t\simeq(15.5\pi a_{\mathrm{rad}}T^4G)^{-1/2},
  \qquad
  \Gamma\simeq\frac{2k^2}{15\sigma_Tn}\propto t^{1/2},
\]
and the critical mass in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.32} is
\begin{equation}
  M_c
  \simeq
  \frac{32\pi^4}{3}
  \left(\frac{4m_H}{45\sigma_T}\right)^{3/2}
  (15.5\pi a_{\mathrm{rad}}T_R^4G)^{-3/4}
  (n_Rm_H)^{-1/2}.
  \tag{15.8.34}\label{eq:15.8.34}
\end{equation}
For instance, for a present mass density
\(10^{-30}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), the critical mass is
\(2\times10^{14}M_\odot\). A fluctuation will presumably be unable to
survive the damping in Phase B if the exponent \((M_c/M)^{2/3}\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.32} is greater than about 10, so we can conclude that the
fluctuations surviving at the time of recombination have a minimum mass
between \(1.6\times10^{11}M_\odot\) and \(6\times10^{12}M_\odot\), just
about the mass of a large galaxy.

So far, we have learned that any small fluctuation of mass between about
\(10^{11}M_\odot\) and \(10^{17}M_\odot\) will grow in Phase A, undergo
damped oscillations in Phase B, but will survive to trigger new growth in
Phase C. One may still wonder why there is a fairly definite upper limit on
galactic mass, of order \(10^{12}M_\odot\) to \(10^{13}M_\odot\), rather
than a smooth distribution of masses extending from \(10^{11}M_\odot\) up
to much higher values. One possible answer lies in the well-known property of
nonlinear effects,\hyperref[ch15-ref-150]{\textsuperscript{150}} of
transferring energy from long wavelengths to the shortest wavelengths that
can survive the effects of dissipation. Unfortunately, the application of
the theory of turbulence to problems of galactic growth has just barely
begun.\hyperref[ch15-ref-151]{\textsuperscript{151}}

The theory of the origins of galaxies is of more than academic interest,
because the value \((\delta n/n)_R\) of the fractional change in density at
the time of recombination may become accessible to observation in the near
future.\hyperref[ch15-ref-152]{\textsuperscript{152}} For fluctuations that
are approximately adiabatic, the number density is proportional to the cube
of the temperature, so the temperature fluctuations at the onset of
recombination will be given by
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\delta T_\gamma}{T_\gamma}\right)_R
  =
  \frac13\left(\frac{\delta n}{n}\right)_R.
  \tag{15.8.35}\label{eq:15.8.35}
\end{equation}
If the universe has remained optically thin since this time, then these
fluctuations will show up in the cosmic microwave background, as fluctuations
of the observed cosmic radiation temperature with angle. (Note, however,
that Thomson scattering could wash out such inhomogeneities%
\hyperref[ch15-ref-152a]{\textsuperscript{152a}} without affecting the Planck
shape of the distribution function; see Section~\ref{sec:15.4}.) According
to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.5.35}--\eqref{eq:15.5.37} and \eqref{eq:15.8.12}, a
fluctuation of mass \(M\) will appear to have angular scale
\[
  \frac{\theta}{2}
  \simeq
  q_0H_0(1+z_R)
  \left(
    \frac{2\pi}{\lvert\mathbf k\rvert_R}
  \right)
  \simeq
  q_0H_0(1+z_R)
  \left(
    \frac{3M}{4\pi n_Rm_H}
  \right)^{1/3},
\]
or, since \(n\propto a^{-3}\),
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\theta}{2}
  \simeq
  q_0H_0
  \left(
    \frac{3M}{4\pi n_0m_H}
  \right)^{1/3}.
  \tag{15.8.36}\label{eq:15.8.36}
\end{equation}
For instance, for \(q_0=\tfrac12\),
\(H_0^{-1}=13\times10^9\) years, and a present density
\(n_0m_H\simeq1.1\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), the fluctuation
corresponding to a nascent galaxy of mass \(10^{11}M_\odot\) should have an
angular scale \(\theta=30''\). As indicated in Section~\ref{sec:15.5}, the
measurement of even small fluctuations with this angular scale is well within
the reach of present techniques. It is therefore a matter of some importance
to calculate how strong a fluctuation has to be at the time of recombination
to grow into a galaxy by the present time. This problem will be addressed in
the next section.
