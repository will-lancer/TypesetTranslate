\subsection{General-Relativistic Theory of Small Fluctuations}\label{sec:15.10}

The nonrelativistic analysis presented in the last section is adequate for the
study of compressional and rotational perturbations during the matter-dominated
era, when \(p\ll\rho\). However, a relativistic treatment is needed to deal
with the radiation- or lepton-dominated eras, when \(p\) is of the same order
as \(\rho\), or to treat the propagation of gravitational radiation in any
era.

The relativistic theory of small disturbances in an expanding universe is
rather complicated, so the theory presented here will deal only with the
simplest case, when the unperturbed Robertson--Walker metric has curvature
\(k=0\). This is not too severe a restriction, because the results for
\(k=+1\) or \(k=-1\) are essentially the same as for \(k=0\), provided that
we keep to the early universe when \(\dot a^2\gg\lvert k\rvert\), and
provided that we keep to perturbations of wavelength much less than \(a\).
These are the most interesting cases in any event, particularly since the
growth of condensations in the recent past can be treated for any value of
\(k\) by the nonrelativistic theory of the last section.

It proves convenient here to include the effects of dissipation from the
beginning. The medium will be characterized by a coefficient of shear
viscosity \(\eta\) and a heat-conduction coefficient \(\chi\); as mentioned
in Section~\ref{sec:15.8}, the bulk viscosity \(\zeta\) will be negligible
once the pressure and kinetic energy density of matter drop below those of
radiation. The effects of dissipation can be taken into account by adding
suitable terms to the energy--momentum tensor. These terms were calculated in
Section~\ref{sec:2.11} for a relativistic imperfect fluid in the absence of
gravitation; the correct energy--momentum tensor in the presence of gravitation
can be immediately obtained by writing Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.11.21} in a generally
covariant form:
\begin{equation}
  T^{\mu\nu}
  =
  pg^{\mu\nu}
  +(p+\rho)U^\mu U^\nu
  -\eta H^{\mu\rho}H^{\nu\sigma}W_{\rho\sigma}
  -\chi\bigl(H^{\mu\rho}U^\nu+H^{\nu\rho}U^\mu\bigr)Q_\rho,
  \tag{15.10.1}\label{eq:15.10.1}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
  W_{\mu\nu}
  &\equiv
  \nabla_\nu U_\mu+\nabla_\mu U_\nu
  -\frac23g_{\mu\nu}\nabla_\gamma U^\gamma,
  \tag{15.10.2}\label{eq:15.10.2}\\
  Q_\mu
  &\equiv
  \nabla_\mu T+T U^\nu\nabla_\nu U_\mu,
  \tag{15.10.3}\label{eq:15.10.3}\\
  H_{\mu\nu}
  &\equiv
  g_{\mu\nu}+U_\mu U_\nu.
  \tag{15.10.4}\label{eq:15.10.4}
\end{align}
It is easy to check that the dissipative terms in \(T^{\mu\nu}\) vanish for
a Robertson--Walker metric (with any \(k\)), so the Friedmann solutions still
provide our starting point. In particular, for \(k=0\) the Einstein equations
have the familiar unperturbed solution
\begin{align}
  g_{tt}&=-1,
  &g_{ti}&=0,
  &g_{ij}&=a^2(t)\delta_{ij},
  \tag{15.10.5}\label{eq:15.10.5}\\
  U^t&=1,
  &U^i&=0,
  \tag{15.10.6}\label{eq:15.10.6}
\end{align}
where \(x^i\) (with \(i=1,2,3\)) are a set of quasi-Euclidean comoving
coordinates, and
\begin{equation}
  \dot a^2=\frac{8\pi G\rho a^2}{3}.
  \tag{15.10.7}\label{eq:15.10.7}
\end{equation}
The only independent nonvanishing components of the unperturbed affine
connection are then given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.1.3}--\eqref{eq:15.1.5} as
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^t{}_{ij}=a\dot a\,\delta_{ij},
  \qquad
  \Gamma^i{}_{tj}=H\delta^i{}_j.
  \tag{15.10.8}\label{eq:15.10.8}
\end{equation}

We shall consider a disturbance, in which the metric is
\(g_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}\), with \(h_{\mu\nu}\) small. Before we write down
the field equations for \(h_{\mu\nu}\), it is useful to recall the remark of
Section~\ref{sec:10.9}, that by performing a coordinate transformation
\eqref{eq:10.9.6}, we can convert the solution \(h_{\mu\nu}\) into an
equivalent solution
\[
  h_{\mu\nu}^*
  =
  h_{\mu\nu}+\nabla_\nu\xi_\mu+\nabla_\mu\xi_\nu
\]
with \(\xi_\mu\) an arbitrary small vector field. Using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.8}, this new solution takes the form
\begin{align}
  h_{ij}^*
  &=
  h_{ij}
  +\partial_j\xi_i+\partial_i\xi_j
  -2a\dot a\,\delta_{ij}\xi_t,
  \tag{15.10.9}\label{eq:15.10.9}\\
  h_{it}^*
  &=
  h_{it}
  +\dot\xi_i+\partial_i\xi_t-2H\xi_i,
  \tag{15.10.10}\label{eq:15.10.10}\\
  h_{tt}^*
  &=
  h_{tt}+2\dot\xi_t.
  \tag{15.10.11}\label{eq:15.10.11}
\end{align}
It is extremely convenient to choose \(\xi_\mu\) so that
\[
  h_{it}^*=h_{tt}^*=0,
\]
thus maintaining to the greatest possible extent the form of the unperturbed
metric in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.5}. This can be accomplished by constructing
\(\xi_\mu\) according to the prescriptions
\[
  \xi_t=-\frac12\int h_{tt}\,\dd t,
  \qquad
  \xi_i
  =
  -a^2\int
  \bigl(h_{it}+\partial_i\xi_t\bigr)a^{-2}\,\dd t.
\]
Dropping the asterisk, it will simply be assumed from now on that the
coordinate system is chosen so that
\begin{equation}
  h_{it}=h_{tt}=0.
  \tag{15.10.12}\label{eq:15.10.12}
\end{equation}

The perturbation \(\delta g_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}\) produces in the affine
connection a perturbation \eqref{eq:10.9.1}, which here has the components
\begin{align}
  \delta\Gamma^i{}_{jk}
  &=
  \frac1{2a^2}
  \bigl(
    \partial_kh_{ij}+\partial_jh_{ik}-\partial_ih_{jk}
  \bigr),
  \tag{15.10.13}\label{eq:15.10.13}\\
  \delta\Gamma^t{}_{jk}
  &=
  \frac12\dot h_{jk},
  \tag{15.10.14}\label{eq:15.10.14}\\
  \delta\Gamma^i{}_{tj}
  &=
  \frac1{2a^2}
  \bigl(\dot h_{ij}-2Hh_{ij}\bigr),
  \tag{15.10.15}\label{eq:15.10.15}\\
  \delta\Gamma^t{}_{ti}
  &=
  \delta\Gamma^i{}_{tt}
  =
  \delta\Gamma^t{}_{tt}
  =0.
  \tag{15.10.16}\label{eq:15.10.16}
\end{align}
Contracting, we find also that
\begin{equation}
  \delta\Gamma_\mu
  \equiv
  \delta\Gamma^\nu{}_{\nu\mu}
  =
  \delta\Gamma^i{}_{i\mu}
  =
  \partial_\mu\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{2a^2}\right),
  \tag{15.10.17}\label{eq:15.10.17}
\end{equation}
it being understood that repeated Latin indices are summed over the values
\(1,2,3\). The perturbation in the Ricci tensor is then given, in the
curvature convention of this edition, by the negatives of Weinberg's printed
components. More explicitly,
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: each right-hand side in (15.10.18)--(15.10.20) is
% the negative of the source expression at fixed tensor slots.
\begin{align}
  \delta R_{ij}
  ={}&
  -\frac1{2a^2}
  \left(
    \nabla^2h_{ij}
    -\partial_j\partial_kh_{ik}
    -\partial_i\partial_kh_{jk}
    +\partial_i\partial_jh_{kk}
  \right)
  +\frac12\ddot h_{ij}
  \notag\\
  &-\frac H2\bigl(\dot h_{ij}-\delta_{ij}\dot h_{kk}\bigr)
  +H^2\bigl(2h_{ij}-\delta_{ij}h_{kk}\bigr),
  \tag{15.10.18}\label{eq:15.10.18}\\
  \delta R_{ti}
  ={}&
  -\frac12\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \left[
    a^{-2}\bigl(\partial_i h_{kk}-\partial_kh_{ki}\bigr)
  \right],
  \tag{15.10.19}\label{eq:15.10.19}\\
  \delta R_{tt}
  ={}&
  -\frac1{2a^2}
  \left[
    \ddot h_{kk}
    -2H\dot h_{kk}
    +2\left(H^2-\frac{\ddot a}{a}\right)h_{kk}
  \right].
  \tag{15.10.20}\label{eq:15.10.20}
\end{align}

According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.1}, the source term on the right-hand side
of Einstein's field equations is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  S_{\mu\nu}
  &\equiv
  T_{\mu\nu}-\frac12g_{\mu\nu}T^\lambda{}_\lambda\\
  &=
  \frac12(\rho-p)g_{\mu\nu}
  +(\rho+p)U_\mu U_\nu
  -\eta H_{\mu\rho}H_{\nu\sigma}W^{\rho\sigma}\\
  &\quad
  -\chi\bigl(H_{\mu\rho}U_\nu+H_{\nu\rho}U_\mu\bigr)Q^\rho.
  \end{split}
  \tag{15.10.21}\label{eq:15.10.21}
\end{equation}
In order to preserve the normalization of the velocity \(U\), we must have
\[
  0=\delta(g_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu)=-2U_1^t.
\]
The perturbations \(h_{ij}\), \(U_1^i\), \(\rho_1\), \(p_1\), and \(T_1\)
then produce in \(S_{\mu\nu}\) the perturbations
\begin{align}
  \delta S_{ij}
  &=
  \frac12(\rho-p)h_{ij}
  +\frac{a^2}{2}\delta_{ij}(\rho_1-p_1)
  -\eta a^4\delta W^{ij},
  \tag{15.10.22}\label{eq:15.10.22}\\
  \delta S_{it}
  &=
  -a^2(\rho+p)U_1^i
  -\chi\dot T\,\delta H_{it}
  +\chi a^2\delta Q^i,
  \tag{15.10.23}\label{eq:15.10.23}\\
  \delta S_{tt}
  &=
  \frac12(\rho_1+3p_1)-2\chi\dot T\,\delta H_{tt},
  \tag{15.10.24}\label{eq:15.10.24}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  \delta H_{it}
  &=-a^2U_1^i,
  &\delta H_{tt}&=0,
  \tag{15.10.25}\label{eq:15.10.25}\\
  \delta W^{ij}
  &=
  a^{-2}
  \left(
    \partial_jU_1^i+\partial_iU_1^j
    -\frac23\delta_{ij}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
  \right)
  \notag\\
  &\quad
  +a^2\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \left[
    a^{-2}
    \left(h_{ij}-\frac13\delta_{ij}h_{kk}\right)
  \right],
  \tag{15.10.26}\label{eq:15.10.26}\\
  \delta Q^i
  &=
  a^{-2}
  \left[
    \partial_iT_1
    +T\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(a^2U_1^i\bigr)
  \right].
  \tag{15.10.27}\label{eq:15.10.27}
\end{align}
Finally, the perturbed Einstein field equations here take the form
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: the source prints a minus sign in (15.10.28);
% it becomes a plus in the binding curvature convention.
\begin{equation}
  \delta R_{\mu\nu}=8\pi G\,\delta S_{\mu\nu}.
  \tag{15.10.28}\label{eq:15.10.28}
\end{equation}
Putting together Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.18}--\eqref{eq:15.10.20} and
\eqref{eq:15.10.22}--\eqref{eq:15.10.28}, we find
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \nabla^2h_{ij}
  ={}&
  \partial_j\partial_kh_{ik}
  -\partial_i\partial_jh_{kk}
  +\partial_i\partial_kh_{jk}
  -a^2\ddot h_{ij}\\
  &+a\dot a\bigl(\dot h_{ij}-\delta_{ij}\dot h_{kk}\bigr)
  +2\dot a^2\bigl(-2h_{ij}+\delta_{ij}h_{kk}\bigr)\\
  ={}&
  -8\pi G(\rho-p)a^2h_{ij}
  -8\pi Ga^4\delta_{ij}(\rho_1-p_1)\\
  &+16\pi G\eta a^4
  \left(
    \partial_jU_1^i+\partial_iU_1^j
    -\frac23\delta_{ij}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
  \right)\\
  &+16\pi G\eta a^2\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \left\{
    a^{-2}
    \left[h_{ij}-\frac13\delta_{ij}h_{kk}\right]
  \right\}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{15.10.29}\label{eq:15.10.29}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \left\{
    a^{-2}
    \bigl(\partial_i h_{kk}-\partial_kh_{ki}\bigr)
  \right\}
  ={}&
  16\pi Ga^2(\rho+p)U_1^i
  -16\pi G\chi\dot T\,a^2U_1^i\\
  &-16\pi G\chi
  \left[
    \partial_iT_1
    +T\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(a^2U_1^i\bigr)
  \right].
  \end{split}
  \tag{15.10.30}\label{eq:15.10.30}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \ddot h_{kk}
  -2H\dot h_{kk}
  +2\left(H^2-\frac{\ddot a}{a}\right)h_{kk}
  =
  -8\pi G(\rho_1+3p_1)a^2.
  \tag{15.10.31}\label{eq:15.10.31}
\end{equation}

The equations of motion for the fluid can be obtained either from the
conservation equations \(\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0\), or directly from the
field equations. By applying the operators \(\partial_i\) and
\(\dd/\dd t+3H\) to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.29} and
\eqref{eq:15.10.30}, and using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.30},
\eqref{eq:15.10.31}, and the trace of \eqref{eq:15.10.29} to simplify the
result, we obtain the momentum-conservation equation
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \left(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}+16\pi G\eta\right)
  &\left[
    \begin{aligned}
      &a^5U_1^i\bigl(\rho+p-\chi\dot T\bigr)\\[-0.2em]
      &\quad-\chi a^3
      \left\{
        \partial_iT_1
        +T\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(a^2U_1^i\bigr)
      \right\}
    \end{aligned}
  \right]\\
  &=
  -a^3\partial_i p_1
  +\eta a^3
  \left\{
    \nabla^2U_1^i
    +\frac13\partial_i
      \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
    +\frac23\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
      \left(a^{-2}\partial_i h_{kk}\right)
  \right\}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{15.10.32}\label{eq:15.10.32}
\end{equation}
(The vector \(\mathbf U_1\) is understood to have components \(U_1^i\),
not \(U_{1i}\).) Also, by taking the divergence of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.30} and using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.31} and the trace
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.29}, we obtain the energy-conservation equation
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \dot\rho_1+3H(\rho_1+p_1)
  ={}&
  -(\rho+p)
  \left\{
    \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{2a^2}\right)
    +\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
  \right\}\\
  &+\chi
  \left\{
    \dot T\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
    +a^{-2}\nabla^2T_1
    +Ta^{-2}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
      \left(a^2\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1\right)
  \right\}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{15.10.33}\label{eq:15.10.33}
\end{equation}
As usual in dealing with dissipative processes, we must also make use of the
conservation law for the particle current \(nU^\mu\):
\[
  0=\nabla_\mu(nU^\mu)
  =U^\mu\partial_\mu n+n\nabla_\mu U^\mu.
\]
(Strictly speaking, \(n\) should be taken as the density of baryon number or
lepton numbers.) For the unperturbed solution, this gives the familiar result
\[
  n\propto a^{-3},
\]
and to first order in the perturbations \(n_1\), \(\mathbf U_1\), and
\(h_{ij}\), we have
\[
  0
  =
  \dot n_1+3Hn_1
  +n\bigl(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
  +\delta\Gamma^\nu{}_{t\nu}\bigr),
\]
or, using Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.17},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{n_1}{n}\right)
  =
  -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf U_1
  -\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{2a^2}\right).
  \tag{15.10.34}\label{eq:15.10.34}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:15.10.29}--\eqref{eq:15.10.34} provide a convenient
set of fundamental equations, but it should be kept in mind that these
equations are not all independent, as shown by the way they were derived.

One solution of these equations can be found immediately:
\begin{align}
  h_{ij}(\mathbf x,t)
  &=
  a^2(t)
  \bigl[\partial_jf_i(\mathbf x)+\partial_if_j(\mathbf x)\bigr],
  \notag\\
  \rho_1&=p_1=\mathbf U_1=n_1=T_1=0.
  \tag{15.10.35}\label{eq:15.10.35}
\end{align}
Here \(f_i\) is an arbitrary function of position. [To verify
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.29}, use Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.1.20}.] However, reference
to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.9}--\eqref{eq:15.10.11} shows that this is
\emph{not} a physical disturbance, but represents the effects of an
infinitesimal coordinate transformation of the form \eqref{eq:10.9.6}:
\begin{equation}
  x^\mu\longrightarrow x^\mu-\xi^\mu(\mathbf x),
  \qquad
  \xi^t=0,
  \qquad
  \xi_i(\mathbf x,t)=a^2(t)f_i(\mathbf x),
  \tag{15.10.36}\label{eq:15.10.36}
\end{equation}
whose structure is such as to preserve the vanishing of \(h_{it}\) and
\(h_{tt}\). We are interested only in physical disturbances, whose form
necessarily differs from \eqref{eq:15.10.35}.

The manifest spatial homogeneity of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.29}--\eqref{eq:15.10.34} allows us to find solutions
with the spatial dependence
\begin{equation}
  h_{ij},\ \rho_1,\ p_1,\ \mathbf U_1,\ n_1,\ T_1
  \propto
  \exp(\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x),
  \tag{15.10.37}\label{eq:15.10.37}
\end{equation}
with \(\mathbf q\) a constant wave number. Just as in the nonrelativistic
case, it is convenient to analyze the general solution into normal modes.
Now there are modes of three different kinds:

\subsubsection*{Radiative Modes}

There is a simple class of solutions with
\begin{equation}
  0
  =
  h_{kk}
  =
  q_i h_{ij}
  =
  \rho_1
  =
  p_1
  =
  \mathbf U_1
  =
  n_1
  =
  T_1.
  \tag{15.10.38}\label{eq:15.10.38}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:15.10.30}--\eqref{eq:15.10.34} are here trivially
satisfied, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.29}, together with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.1.20}, gives
\begin{equation}
  \ddot h_{ij}
  +\bigl(-H+16\pi G\eta\bigr)\dot h_{ij}
  +\left(
    \frac{q^2}{a^2}
    -2\frac{\ddot a}{a}
    -32\pi G\eta H
  \right)h_{ij}
  =0.
  \tag{15.10.39}\label{eq:15.10.39}
\end{equation}
For very large wave numbers, we can find a general second-order WKB solution
\begin{equation}
  h_{ij}
  \propto
  a\exp\left\{
    \int
    \left[
      \frac{\pm\ii\lvert\mathbf q\rvert}{a}
      -8\pi G\eta
    \right]\dd t
  \right\}.
  \tag{15.10.40}\label{eq:15.10.40}
\end{equation}
With \(a\) and \(\eta\) slowly varying, this result may be converted from
the comoving spatial coordinate system to a nearly Minkowskian system by
multiplying \(h_{ij}\) by a scale factor \(1/a^2\). Thus
\eqref{eq:15.10.40} corresponds to a plane gravitational wave of the form
\eqref{eq:10.2.1}, with
\[
  e_{\mu\nu}
  \propto
  \frac1a
  \exp\left\{-8\pi G\int\eta\,\dd t\right\},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{phys}}=\frac{\mathbf q}{a}.
\]
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.7}, the energy density
\(\tau_g^{00}\) of these gravitational waves decreases as
\begin{equation}
  \tau_g^{00}
  \propto
  a^{-4}
  \exp\left\{-16\pi G\int\eta\,\dd t\right\}.
  \tag{15.10.41}\label{eq:15.10.41}
\end{equation}
The factor \(a^{-4}\) is just what we should expect for the free expansion
of any wave representing a massless particle. [Compare
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.1.23}.] The extra factor in \eqref{eq:15.10.41} tells us
that gravitational waves in a viscous medium are absorbed at a
rate:\hyperref[ch15-ref-156]{\textsuperscript{156}}
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma_g=16\pi G\eta.
  \tag{15.10.42}\label{eq:15.10.42}
\end{equation}
Generally, \(\eta\) will be of the order of the thermal energy density times
some typical mean free time \(\tau\), so that \(\Gamma_g\) is at most of
order \(\dot a^2\tau/a^2\). Hence as long as the collision rate
\(\tau^{-1}\) is much greater than the expansion rate \(H\), the damping
rate \(\Gamma_g\) will be much less than \(H\), and viscosity will have
little effect on the wave propagation. With viscosity neglected, and with
\(a(t)\) assumed to have a power-law time dependence
\begin{equation}
  a(t)\propto t^n,
  \tag{15.10.43}\label{eq:15.10.43}
\end{equation}
we can find a solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.39} valid for all wave
numbers,
\begin{equation}
  h_{ij}
  \propto
  t^{(n+1)/2}
  J_{\pm\nu}
  \left(
    \frac{\lvert\mathbf q\rvert t}{(1-n)a}
  \right),
  \tag{15.10.44}\label{eq:15.10.44}
\end{equation}
where \(J_{\pm\nu}\) is the usual Bessel function of order \(\pm\nu\), and
\begin{equation}
  \nu=\frac{3n-1}{2-2n}.
  \tag{15.10.45}\label{eq:15.10.45}
\end{equation}
(In the matter-dominated or radiation-dominated eras,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.43} holds with \(n=2/3\) or \(n=1/2\), respectively.)
Unlike the case of electromagnetic waves in a plasma, there is no sharp lower
limit to the frequencies at which gravitational waves can propagate; instead,
the solutions start at \(\lvert\mathbf q\rvert t\ll a\) with the behavior
\begin{equation}
  h_{ij}\propto t^{2n}
  \quad\hbox{or}\quad
  t^{1-n},
  \tag{15.10.46}\label{eq:15.10.46}
\end{equation}
and gradually go over for \(\lvert\mathbf q\rvert t\gg a\) to the wavelike
solutions \eqref{eq:15.10.40}.

\subsubsection*{Rotational Modes}

There is also a simple class of solutions with
\begin{equation}
  0
  =
  \dot h_{kk}
  =
  \mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1
  =
  \rho_1
  =
  p_1
  =
  n_1
  =
  T_1.
  \tag{15.10.47}\label{eq:15.10.47}
\end{equation}
Here Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.31}, \eqref{eq:15.10.33}, and
\eqref{eq:15.10.34} are trivially satisfied, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.32} becomes an equation for the transverse part of
\(\mathbf U_1\):
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}+16\pi G\eta\right)
  \left[
    a^5\bigl(\rho+p-\chi\dot T\bigr)\mathbf U_1
  \right]
  =
  -\eta a^3q^2\mathbf U_1.
  \tag{15.10.48}\label{eq:15.10.48}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:15.10.29} and \eqref{eq:15.10.30} then dictate the
gravitational field produced by the rotations represented by
\(\mathbf U_1\); these field equations are automatically consistent with
\eqref{eq:15.10.48}, because the equations of motion from which
\eqref{eq:15.10.48} was derived were themselves derived from the field
equations. With dissipation neglected, Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.48} simply
tells us that \(\mathbf U_1\) has a time dependence inversely proportional
to \(a^5(\rho+p)\),
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf U_1\propto\frac1{a^5(\rho+p)},
  \tag{15.10.49}\label{eq:15.10.49}
\end{equation}
which may be regarded as the relativistic generalization (in comoving
coordinates) of the Newtonian result \eqref{eq:15.9.22}.

\subsubsection*{Compressional Modes}

Once again, the richest time dependence is displayed by the compressional
modes, in which the quantities \(h_{kk}\), \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1\),
\(\rho_1\), \(p_1\), \(T_1\), and \(n_1\) are not constrained to vanish.
Equations~\eqref{eq:15.10.31}, \eqref{eq:15.10.33},
\eqref{eq:15.10.34}, and the divergence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.32} here
provide a set of coupled equations for these quantities:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot h_{kk}
  -2H\dot h_{kk}
  +2\left(H^2-\frac{\ddot a}{a}\right)h_{kk}
  =
  -8\pi Ga^2(\rho_1+3p_1),
  \tag{15.10.50}\label{eq:15.10.50}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \dot\rho_1+3H(\rho_1+p_1)
  ={}&
  -(\rho+p)
  \left\{
    \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{2a^2}\right)
    +\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1
  \right\}\\
  &+\chi
  \left\{
    \ii\dot T\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1
    +\frac{\ii T}{a^2}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
      \left(a^2\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1\right)
    -\frac{q^2}{a^2}T_1
  \right\},
  \end{split}
  \tag{15.10.51}\label{eq:15.10.51}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{n_1}{n}\right)
  =
  -\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1
  -\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{2a^2}\right),
  \tag{15.10.52}\label{eq:15.10.52}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \left(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}+16\pi G\eta\right)
  &\left[
    \begin{aligned}
      &\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1\,
        a^5\bigl(\rho+p-\chi\dot T\bigr)\\[-0.2em]
      &\quad+\chi a^3
      \left\{
        q^2T_1
        -\ii\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
          \left(a^2\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1\right)
      \right\}
    \end{aligned}
  \right]\\
  &=
  a^3q^2p_1
  -\eta a^3q^2
  \left[
    \frac{4\ii}{3}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1
    +\frac23\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
      \left(\frac{h_{kk}}{a^2}\right)
  \right].
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{15.10.53}\label{eq:15.10.53}
\end{equation}
If we use the equation of state to express \(p_1\) and \(\rho_1\) in terms
of \(n_1\) and \(T_1\), these can be regarded as four equations for the four
unknowns \(h_{kk}\), \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1\), \(n_1\), and \(T_1\).
The reader may check that these equations yield the damping rate
\eqref{eq:15.8.30} for fluctuation wave numbers that are much larger than the
Jeans limit, for which gravitation and the expansion of the universe may be
neglected. Also, in the nonrelativistic limit with damping neglected, these
equations reduce to the previously derived Newtonian equations
\eqref{eq:15.9.20} and \eqref{eq:15.9.21}, provided that we identify
\[
  \delta\equiv\frac{\rho_1}{\rho},
  \qquad
  \epsilon
  \equiv
  -\frac a{q^2}
  \left\{
    \ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf U_1
    +\frac12\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{a^2}\right)
  \right\}.
\]

For a thorough discussion of the normal modes described by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.50}--\eqref{eq:15.10.53}, the reader is directed to
the review of Field.\hyperref[ch15-ref-157]{\textsuperscript{157}} For our
present purposes, it will be sufficient to consider only the limiting case of
very small wave numbers. In the limit \(\mathbf q\to0\), all dissipative
effects vanish; indeed, by eliminating \(h_{kk}\) in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.51} and \eqref{eq:15.10.52}, we can show that the
perturbations keep the entropy constant, so that
\begin{equation}
  p_1=v_s^2\rho_1.
  \tag{15.10.54}\label{eq:15.10.54}
\end{equation}
Also, it is convenient to use Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.1.21} to write
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.51} as
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  -\frac12\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{h_{kk}}{a^2}\right)
  &=
  -\frac1{\rho+p}
  \left\{
    \dot\rho_1
    -\frac{\dot\rho(1+v_s^2)\rho_1}{\rho+p}
  \right\}\\
  &=
  -\frac1{\rho+p}
  \left\{
    \dot\rho_1
    -\frac{(\dot\rho+\dot p)\rho_1}{\rho+p}
  \right\}\\
  % SOURCE ERRATUM: the scan prints a plus sign, but the preceding expression
  % is minus the derivative below.
  &=
  -\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho+p}\right).
  \end{aligned}
\]
The addition of a time-independent term to \(h_{kk}/a^2\) would correspond
to a mere coordinate transformation of the form \eqref{eq:15.10.36}, so the
solution is essentially unique,
\begin{equation}
  h_{kk}=2a^2\delta,
  \tag{15.10.55}\label{eq:15.10.55}
\end{equation}
with \(\delta\) now defined by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_1=(\rho+p)\delta.
  \tag{15.10.56}\label{eq:15.10.56}
\end{equation}
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.10.54}--\eqref{eq:15.10.56} in
\eqref{eq:15.10.50} yields the second-order differential equation
\begin{equation}
  \ddot\delta
  +2H\dot\delta
  -4\pi G(\rho+p)(1+3v_s^2)\delta
  =0.
  \tag{15.10.57}\label{eq:15.10.57}
\end{equation}

We are now at last able to calculate the growth rate in ``Phase A,'' that is,
in the early period when the Jeans mass is very small and the energy density
is dominated by radiation (and neutrinos). In this case, we have
\[
  a\propto t^{1/2},
  \qquad
  \rho=\frac3{32\pi Gt^2},
  \qquad
  p=\frac\rho3,
  \qquad
  v_s=\frac1{\sqrt3},
\]
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.10.57} reads
\begin{equation}
  \ddot\delta+t^{-1}\dot\delta-t^{-2}\delta=0.
  \tag{15.10.58}\label{eq:15.10.58}
\end{equation}
Again, there is a growing solution \(\delta_+\) and a decaying solution
\(\delta_-\):
\begin{equation}
  \delta_+\propto t,
  \qquad
  \delta_-\propto t^{-1},
  \tag{15.10.59}\label{eq:15.10.59}
\end{equation}
but no exponential growth.
