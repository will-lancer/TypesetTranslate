\subsection{Another Derivation\optionalreading}\label{sec:7.2}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and
may be omitted in a first reading.
\end{quote}

The derivation of Einstein's equations in the last section made heavy use of
the assumption that the left-hand side \(\mathcal G_{\mu\nu}\) is a tensor
depending solely on the metric and its first and second derivatives. We might
consider using a more general tensor, which involves elements unrelated to the
metric tensor or its derivatives, such as
\begin{equation}
  \left.
  \left(
    \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial\xi_X^\alpha(x)}
    \frac{\partial^3\xi_X^\alpha(x)}
         {\partial x^\nu\partial x^\lambda\partial x^\rho}
  \right)
  \right|_{x=X},
  \tag{7.2.1}\label{eq:7.2.1}
\end{equation}
where \(\xi_X^\alpha(x)\) are the coordinates locally inertial at \(X\).
(Reference to the precise definitions \eqref{eq:3.3.2} and
\eqref{eq:3.3.3} of the metric and affine connection will show that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.1} is not related to their derivatives.) Such a tensor
could be constructed by writing
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal G_{\mu\nu}
  =
  \left.
  \left(
    \frac{\partial\xi_X^\alpha(x)}{\partial x^\mu}
    \frac{\partial\xi_X^\beta(x)}{\partial x^\nu}
    \mathcal G^X_{\alpha\beta}(x)
  \right)
  \right|_{x=X},
  \tag{7.2.2}\label{eq:7.2.2}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal G^X_{\alpha\beta}\) is the most general possible linear
combination of second derivatives of the metric tensor in the \(\xi_X\)
coordinate system allowed by Lorentz covariance and symmetry.

For compactness in the following calculation, write
\[
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha
  \equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_X^\alpha},
  \qquad
  \Box_\xi
  \equiv
  \eta^{\alpha\beta}
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta.
\]
Then the general combination is
\begin{align}
  \mathcal G^X_{\alpha\beta}
  ={}&
  a_1\Box_\xi g_{\alpha\beta}
  +a_2\left(
    \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\beta{}^\gamma
    +\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\alpha{}^\gamma
  \right)
  \notag\\
  &+
  a_3\eta_{\alpha\beta}
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma\partial^{(\xi)}_\delta g^{\gamma\delta}
  +b_1
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta g^\gamma{}_\gamma
  +b_2\eta_{\alpha\beta}\Box_\xi g^\gamma{}_\gamma.
  \tag{7.2.3}\label{eq:7.2.3}
\end{align}
Here \(a_1,a_2,a_3,b_1,b_2\) are five arbitrary dimensionless constants.
(We have dropped the label \(X\). Also, all indices are raised and lowered
with the Minkowski tensors \(\eta^{\alpha\beta}\) and
\(\eta_{\alpha\beta}\).) For perfectly general values of the five constants,
this \(\mathcal G_{\mu\nu}\) would indeed depend on foreign elements such as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.1}. However, it is remarkable that by making use of
energy-momentum conservation, and the validity of Newton's theory for weak
static fields produced by nonrelativistic matter, we can put such stringent
requirements on the constants \(a_1,\ldots,b_2\) that the terms involving
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.1} drop out, and we get Einstein's theory.

In a weak field the requirement of energy and momentum conservation yields
the ordinary conservation law
\(\partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha T^\alpha{}_\beta=0\), and therefore the assumed
field equations
\(\mathcal G_{\alpha\beta}=8\pi G T_{\alpha\beta}\) require that
\begin{align*}
  0
  =
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\mathcal G^\alpha{}_\beta
  ={}&
  (a_1+a_2)\Box_\xi
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha g^\alpha{}_\beta
  \\
  &+
  (a_2+a_3)
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\beta
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma\partial^{(\xi)}_\delta g^{\gamma\delta}
  +(b_1+b_2)\Box_\xi
  \partial^{(\xi)}_\beta g^\gamma{}_\gamma.
\end{align*}
Hence \(a_1+a_2\), \(a_2+a_3\), and \(b_1+b_2\) must all vanish, giving
\begin{align}
  \mathcal G_{\alpha\beta}
  ={}&
  a_1\Bigl\{
    \Box_\xi g_{\alpha\beta}
    -\partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\beta{}^\gamma
    -\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\alpha{}^\gamma
    +\eta_{\alpha\beta}
     \partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma\partial^{(\xi)}_\delta g^{\gamma\delta}
  \Bigr\}
  \notag\\
  &+
  b_1\Bigl\{
    \partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta g^\gamma{}_\gamma
    -\eta_{\alpha\beta}\Box_\xi g^\gamma{}_\gamma
  \Bigr\}.
  \tag{7.2.4}\label{eq:7.2.4}
\end{align}

To determine \(a_1\) and \(b_1\), we pass to the Newtonian limit. For a
static field, Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.4} gives
\[
  \mathcal G_{ii}+\mathcal G_{00}
  =
  a_1\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2(g_{ii}+g_{00})
  -b_1\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2(g_{ii}-g_{00}).
\]
(Repeated Latin indices are summed over the values \(1,2,3\).) For a
nonrelativistic material system \(\lvert T_{ij}\rvert\) is much less than
\(\lvert T_{00}\rvert\), so we obtain the field equation
\begin{equation}
  (a_1+b_1)\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2g_{00}
  +(a_1-b_1)\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2g_{ii}
  =
  8\pi G T_{00}.
  \tag{7.2.5}\label{eq:7.2.5}
\end{equation}
We want the field equations in this limit to imply Newton's law,
\[
  \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2g_{00}=-8\pi G T_{00},
\]
but Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.5} is the only one of the field equations to involve
only \(g_{00}\) and/or \(g_{ii}\), so we must require that
\(a_1=b_1=-\frac12\). The left-hand side of the weak-field equations is then
\begin{align}
  \mathcal G_{\alpha\beta}
  ={}&
  -\frac12\Bigl\{
    \Box_\xi g_{\alpha\beta}
    -\partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\beta{}^\gamma
    -\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\alpha{}^\gamma
    +\partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta g^\gamma{}_\gamma
  \Bigr\}
  \notag\\
  &-
  \frac12\eta_{\alpha\beta}\Bigl\{
    \partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma\partial^{(\xi)}_\delta g^{\gamma\delta}
    -\Box_\xi g^\gamma{}_\gamma
  \Bigr\}.
  \tag{7.2.6}\label{eq:7.2.6}
\end{align}
But Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.2} shows that for a weak field the Ricci tensor is
\[
  R_{\alpha\beta}
  =
  -\frac12\Bigl\{
    \Box_\xi g_{\alpha\beta}
    -\partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\beta{}^\gamma
    -\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta\partial^{(\xi)}_\gamma g_\alpha{}^\gamma
    +\partial^{(\xi)}_\alpha\partial^{(\xi)}_\beta g^\gamma{}_\gamma
  \Bigr\},
\]
so Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.5} gives the field equation as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal G_{\alpha\beta}
  =
  R_{\alpha\beta}
  -\frac12\eta_{\alpha\beta}R
  =
  8\pi G T_{\alpha\beta}.
  \tag{7.2.7}\label{eq:7.2.7}
\end{equation}
The Principle of Equivalence then immediately yields the Einstein equations
for a general field,
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12g_{\mu\nu}R
  =
  8\pi G T_{\mu\nu},
  \tag{7.2.8}\label{eq:7.2.8}
\end{equation}
for Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.8} is generally covariant and reduces in locally
inertial coordinate systems to Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.6}. Thus, if we want a more
general equation than Einstein's, which reduces in the weak-field limit to a
second-order equation with Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.4} on the left-hand side, then
we must pay the price of allowing new elements such as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.1} to enter, \emph{and} we must give up the possibility of
deriving Newton's theory as a limiting case.
