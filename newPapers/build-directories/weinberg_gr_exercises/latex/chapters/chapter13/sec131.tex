\subsection{Killing Vectors}\label{sec:13.1}

A metric \(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\) is said to be \emph{form-invariant} under a given
coordinate transformation \(x\longrightarrow x'\), when the transformed
metric \(g'_{\mu\nu}(x')\) is the same function of its argument \(x'^\mu\) as
the original metric \(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\) was of \emph{its} argument \(x^\mu\);
that is,
\begin{equation}
  g'_{\mu\nu}(y)=g_{\mu\nu}(y)
  \qquad\text{for all \(y\)}.
  \tag{13.1.1}\label{eq:13.1.1}
\end{equation}
[This is different from the condition for a scalar, which is that
\(S'(x')=S(x)\).] At any given \emph{point} the transformed metric is given
by the relation
\[
  g'_{\mu\nu}(x')
  =
  \frac{\partial x^\rho}{\partial x'^\mu}
  \frac{\partial x^\sigma}{\partial x'^\nu}
  g_{\rho\sigma}(x),
\]
or equivalently
\[
  g_{\mu\nu}(x)
  =
  \frac{\partial x'^\rho}{\partial x^\mu}
  \frac{\partial x'^\sigma}{\partial x^\nu}
  g'_{\rho\sigma}(x').
\]
When \eqref{eq:13.1.1} is valid, we can replace
\(g'_{\rho\sigma}(x')\) with \(g_{\rho\sigma}(x')\), and so obtain the
fundamental requirement for a form invariance of the metric:
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mu\nu}(x)
  =
  \frac{\partial x'^\rho}{\partial x^\mu}
  \frac{\partial x'^\sigma}{\partial x^\nu}
  g_{\rho\sigma}(x').
  \tag{13.1.2}\label{eq:13.1.2}
\end{equation}
Any transformation \(x\longrightarrow x'\) that satisfies
\eqref{eq:13.1.2} is called an \emph{isometry}.

In general, Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.2} is a very complicated restriction on the
function \(x'^\mu(x)\). It can be greatly simplified by descending to the
special case of an infinitesimal coordinate transformation
\begin{equation}
  x'^\mu=x^\mu+\epsilon\xi^\mu(x),
  \qquad \lvert\epsilon\rvert\ll1.
  \tag{13.1.3}\label{eq:13.1.3}
\end{equation}
To first order in \(\epsilon\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.2} now reads
\begin{equation}
  0
  =
  g_{\mu\sigma}\partial_\rho\xi^\mu
  +
  g_{\rho\nu}\partial_\sigma\xi^\nu
  +
  \xi^\mu\partial_\mu g_{\rho\sigma}.
  \tag{13.1.4}\label{eq:13.1.4}
\end{equation}
This can be rewritten in terms of derivatives of the covariant components
\(\xi_\sigma=g_{\mu\sigma}\xi^\mu\):
\begin{align*}
  0
  &=
  \partial_\rho\xi_\sigma+\partial_\sigma\xi_\rho
  +\xi^\mu
  \left(
    \partial_\mu g_{\rho\sigma}
    -\partial_\rho g_{\mu\sigma}
    -\partial_\sigma g_{\rho\mu}
  \right)
  \\
  &=
  \partial_\rho\xi_\sigma+\partial_\sigma\xi_\rho
  -2\Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\sigma}\xi_\mu,
\end{align*}
or, more compactly,
\begin{equation}
  0=\nabla_\rho\xi_\sigma+\nabla_\sigma\xi_\rho.
  \tag{13.1.5}\label{eq:13.1.5}
\end{equation}
Any four-vector field \(\xi_\sigma(x)\) that satisfies
Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.5} will be said to form a \emph{Killing vector}%
\hyperref[ch13-ref-1]{\textsuperscript{1}} of the metric
\(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\). The problem of determining all infinitesimal isometries
of a given metric is now reduced to the problem of determining all Killing
vectors of the metric. Any linear combination of Killing vectors (with
constant coefficients) is a Killing vector, so it is the space of vector
fields spanned by the Killing vectors that really determines the
infinitesimal isometries of a metric.

The Killing condition \eqref{eq:13.1.5} is much more restrictive than it
looks, for it allows us to determine the whole function \(\xi_\mu(x)\) from
given values of \(\xi_\mu\) and \(\nabla_\nu\xi_\mu\) at some point \(X\).
To see this, we need only recall our formula \eqref{eq:6.5.1} for the
commutator of two covariant derivatives,
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\mu\nabla_\rho\xi_\sigma
  -\nabla_\rho\nabla_\mu\xi_\sigma
  =
  R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}\xi_\lambda,
  \tag{13.1.6}\label{eq:13.1.6}
\end{equation}
and the cyclic sum rule \eqref{eq:6.6.5} for the curvature tensor,
\begin{equation}
  R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}
  +R^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma\rho}
  +R^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu\sigma}
  =0.
  \tag{13.1.7}\label{eq:13.1.7}
\end{equation}
By adding \eqref{eq:13.1.6} and its two cyclic permutations, we find that
any vector \(\xi_\mu\) must satisfy the relation
\begin{align}
  0={}&
  \nabla_\mu\nabla_\rho\xi_\sigma
  -\nabla_\rho\nabla_\mu\xi_\sigma
  +\nabla_\rho\nabla_\sigma\xi_\mu
  -\nabla_\sigma\nabla_\rho\xi_\mu
  \notag\\
  &+\nabla_\sigma\nabla_\mu\xi_\rho
  -\nabla_\mu\nabla_\sigma\xi_\rho.
  \tag{13.1.8}\label{eq:13.1.8}
\end{align}
For a Killing vector, \eqref{eq:13.1.5} and \eqref{eq:13.1.8} give
\[
  0
  =
  \nabla_\mu\nabla_\rho\xi_\sigma
  -\nabla_\rho\nabla_\mu\xi_\sigma
  -\nabla_\rho\nabla_\sigma\xi_\mu,
\]
and thus \eqref{eq:13.1.6} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\sigma\nabla_\rho\xi_\mu
  =
  R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}\xi_\lambda.
  \tag{13.1.9}\label{eq:13.1.9}
\end{equation}
Hence, given \(\xi_\lambda\) and \(\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda\) at some point
\(X\), we can determine the second derivatives of \(\xi_\lambda(x)\) at
\(X\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.9}, and we can find successively higher
derivatives of \(\xi_\lambda\) at \(X\) by taking derivatives of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.9}. All the derivatives of \(\xi_\lambda\) at \(X\)
will thus be determined as linear combinations of \(\xi_\lambda(X)\) and
\(\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda(X)\). The function \(\xi_\lambda(x)\) can then
(when it exists) be constructed as a Taylor series in \(x^\lambda-X^\lambda\)
within some finite neighborhood of \(X\), and will again be linear in the
initial values \(\xi_\lambda(X)\), \(\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda(X)\). Thus any
particular Killing vector \(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)\) of the metric
\(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\) can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
  \xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)
  =
  A_\rho{}^\lambda(x;X)\xi_\lambda^{\,n}(X)
  +B_\rho{}^{\lambda\nu}(x;X)
  \nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda^{\,n}(X),
  \tag{13.1.10}\label{eq:13.1.10}
\end{equation}
where \(A_\rho{}^\lambda\) and \(B_\rho{}^{\lambda\nu}\) are functions
that of course depend on the metric and on \(X\), but do not depend on the
initial values \(\xi_\lambda(X)\) and \(\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda(X)\), and
hence are the same for all Killing vectors. Each Killing vector
\(\xi_\rho(x)\) of a given metric is uniquely specified by the values of
\(\xi_\rho(X)\) and \(\nabla_\sigma\xi_\rho(X)\) at any particular point
\(X\).

A set of Killing vectors \(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)\) is said to be
\emph{independent} if they do not satisfy any linear relations of the form
\begin{equation}
  \sum_n c_n\xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)=0
  \tag{13.1.11}\label{eq:13.1.11}
\end{equation}
with \emph{constant} coefficients \(c_n\). Equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.10}
tells us that \emph{there can be at most \(N(N+1)/2\) independent Killing
vectors in \(N\) dimensions}. For consider any \(M\) Killing vectors
\(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)\). For each \(n\), there are \(N\) quantities
\(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)\) and \(N(N-1)/2\) independent quantities
\(\nabla_\nu\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)\) [recall Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.5}], so we can
think of the quantities \(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)\) and
\(\nabla_\nu\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)\) as the components of \(M\) vectors in an
\(N(N+1)/2\)-dimensional space. If \(M>N(N+1)/2\), then these \(M\)
vectors cannot be linearly independent, so they must satisfy relations of
the form
\[
  \sum_n c_n\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)
  =
  \sum_n c_n\nabla_\nu\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)
  =0.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.10} then tells us that the Killing vectors
\(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)\) satisfy the relations \eqref{eq:13.1.11}
everywhere, and are therefore not independent Killing vectors.

This result is significant only because we defined independent Killing
vectors as vectors that are not subject to any linear relations with
\emph{constant} coefficients. At some given point \(X\) in an
\(N\)-dimensional space, any set of more than \(N\) Killing vectors will of
course be subject to one or more linear relations such as
\eqref{eq:13.1.11}. However, the coefficients \(c_n\) in these linear
relations need not be constant in \(x^\mu\). The above theorem says that any
set of more than \(N(N+1)/2\) Killing vectors will be subject to linear
relations with constant coefficients.

A metric space is said to be \emph{homogeneous} if there exist infinitesimal
isometries \eqref{eq:13.1.3} that carry any given point \(X\) into any other
point in its immediate neighborhood. That is, the metric must admit Killing
vectors that at any given point take all possible values. In particular, in
an \(N\)-dimensional space we can choose a set of \(N\) Killing vectors
\(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu)}(x;X)\) with
\[
  \xi_\lambda^{(\mu)}(X;X)=\delta_\lambda^\mu.
\]
These are evidently independent, because any relation of the form
\(c_\mu\xi_\lambda^{(\mu)}(x;X)=0\) would at \(x=X\) imply that all
\(c_\lambda\) vanish.

A metric space is said to be \emph{isotropic} about a given point \(X\) if
there exist infinitesimal isometries \eqref{eq:13.1.3} that leave the point
\(X\) fixed, so that \(\xi^\mu(X)=0\), and for which the first derivatives
\(\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda(X)\) take all possible values, subject only to the
antisymmetry condition \eqref{eq:13.1.5}. In particular, in \(N\)
dimensions we can choose a set of \(N(N-1)/2\) Killing vectors
\(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)\) with
\begin{gather*}
  \xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)
  \equiv-\xi_\lambda^{(\nu\mu)}(x;X),\\
  \xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(X;X)=0,\\
  \nabla_\rho\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(X;X)
  =
  \left[
    \partial_\rho\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)
  \right]_{x=X}
  =
  \delta_\lambda^\mu\delta_\rho^\nu
  -\delta_\rho^\mu\delta_\lambda^\nu.
\end{gather*}
These are independent, because any relation of the form
\(c_{\mu\nu}\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)=0\), with
\(c_{\mu\nu}=-c_{\nu\mu}\), would at \(X\) imply that
\(c_{\lambda\rho}-c_{\rho\lambda}=2c_{\lambda\rho}=0\).

We shall also have to deal with spaces that are isotropic about \emph{every}
point. In this case there are Killing vectors
\(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)\) and
\(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X+dX)\) that satisfy the above initial
conditions at \(X\) and at \(X+dX\), respectively. Any linear combination
of these will be a Killing vector, and so
\(\partial\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)/\partial X^\rho\) will also be a
Killing vector of the metric. In order to evaluate this Killing vector at
\(x=X\), we need only recall that \(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(X;X)\)
vanishes, and therefore
\[
  0
  =
  \frac{\partial}{\partial X^\rho}
  \xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(X;X)
  =
  \left[
    \partial_\rho\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)
  \right]_{x=X}
  +
  \left[
    \frac{\partial}{\partial X^\rho}
    \xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)
  \right]_{x=X}.
\]
This gives
\[
  \left[
    \frac{\partial}{\partial X^\rho}
    \xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)
  \right]_{x=X}
  =
  -\delta_\lambda^\mu\delta_\rho^\nu
  +\delta_\rho^\mu\delta_\lambda^\nu.
\]
It is now obvious that we can construct a Killing vector \(\xi_\lambda(x)\)
that takes any arbitrary value \(a_\lambda\) at \(x=X\); we need only take
\[
  \xi_\lambda(x)
  =
  \frac{a_\nu}{N-1}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial X^\rho}
  \xi_\lambda^{(\rho\nu)}(x;X).
\]
Hence \emph{any space that is isotropic about every point is also
homogeneous}.

A metric that admits the maximum number \(N(N+1)/2\) of Killing vectors is
said to be \emph{maximally symmetric}. In particular, a space that is both
homogeneous and isotropic about some given point \(X\) will admit the
\(N(N+1)/2\) Killing vectors \(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu)}(x;X)\) and
\(\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X)\). These Killing vectors are obviously
independent, for if they satisfy a linear relation
\[
  0
  =
  c_\mu\xi_\lambda^{(\mu)}(x;X)
  +c_{\mu\nu}\xi_\lambda^{(\mu\nu)}(x;X),
  \qquad
  c_{\mu\nu}=-c_{\nu\mu},
\]
then differentiating with respect to \(x^\rho\) and setting \(x=X\) gives
\(c_{\lambda\rho}=0\), and setting \(x=X\) then gives \(c_\lambda=0\).
Thus \emph{a homogeneous space that is isotropic about some point is
maximally symmetric}. It then also follows that \emph{any space that is
isotropic about every point is maximally symmetric}.

We can also prove the converse, that \emph{a maximally symmetric space is
necessarily homogeneous and isotropic about all points}. If there are
\(N(N+1)/2\) independent Killing vectors \(\xi_\lambda^{\,n}(x)\), then
we can think of the quantities \(\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)\),
\(\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda^{\,n}(X)\) as forming a square matrix, with
\(N(N+1)/2\) rows labeled by \(n\), and \(N(N+1)/2\) columns labeled by
the \(N\) values of \(\rho\) and the \(N(N-1)/2\) values of \(\lambda\)
and \(\nu\) with \(\lambda>\nu\). Furthermore, this matrix must have a
nonvanishing determinant, because any relation of the form
\[
  \sum_n c_n\xi_\rho^{\,n}(X)
  =
  \sum_n c_n\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda^{\,n}(X)
  =0
\]
would with \eqref{eq:13.1.10} imply that
\(\sum_n c_n\xi_\rho^{\,n}(x)\) vanishes, contrary to our assumption that
these Killing vectors are independent. It must therefore be possible, for any
``row vector'' with ``components'' \(a_\mu\) and
\(b_{\mu\nu}=-b_{\nu\mu}\), to find a solution of the equations
\[
  \sum_n d_n\xi_\mu^{\,n}(X)=a_\mu,
  \qquad
  \sum_n d_n\nabla_\nu\xi_\mu^{\,n}(X)=b_{\mu\nu}.
\]
Hence we can find a Killing vector \(\xi_\mu(x)\) for which
\(\xi_\mu(X)\) takes the value \(a_\mu\) and
\(\nabla_\nu\xi_\mu(X)\) takes the value \(b_{\mu\nu}\), by choosing
\[
  \xi_\mu(x)=\sum_n d_n\xi_\mu^{\,n}(x).
\]
But \(a_\mu\) is arbitrary, so the space is homogeneous, and \(b_{\mu\nu}\)
is arbitrary (except that \(b_{\mu\nu}=-b_{\nu\mu}\)), so the space is
isotropic about \(X\).

As an example of a maximally symmetric space, consider an \(N\)-dimensional
flat space, with vanishing curvature tensor. We can then choose Cartesian
coordinates with a constant metric and vanishing affine connection. In this
coordinate system, Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.1.9} reads
\[
  \partial_\rho\partial_\sigma\xi_\mu=0.
\]
The solution is
\[
  \xi_\mu(x)=a_\mu+b_{\mu\nu}x^\nu
\]
with \(a_\mu\) and \(b_{\mu\nu}\) constant. This satisfies the Killing-vector
condition \eqref{eq:13.1.5} if and only if
\[
  b_{\mu\nu}=-b_{\nu\mu}.
\]
We can thus choose a set of \(N(N+1)/2\) Killing vectors as follows:
\[
  \xi_\mu^{(\nu)}(x)=\delta_\mu^\nu,
  \qquad
  \xi_\mu^{(\nu\lambda)}(x)
  =
  \delta_\mu^\nu x^\lambda-\delta_\mu^\lambda x^\nu,
\]
and the general Killing vector is
\[
  \xi_\mu(x)
  =
  a_\nu\xi_\mu^{(\nu)}(x)
  +b_{\nu\lambda}\xi_\mu^{(\nu\lambda)}(x).
\]
The \(N\) vectors \(\xi_\mu^{(\nu)}(x)\) represent translations, whereas
the \(N(N-1)/2\) vectors \(\xi_\mu^{(\nu\lambda)}(x)\) represent
infinitesimal rotations (or, for a Minkowski space, Lorentz transformations).
Thus any flat metric admits \(N(N+1)/2\) independent Killing vectors and is
therefore maximally symmetric.

Of course not all metrics admit the maximum number of Killing vectors.
Whether \eqref{eq:13.1.9} is soluble for a given set of initial data
\(\xi_\lambda(X)\), \(\nabla_\rho\xi_\lambda(X)\) depends on the
integrability of this equation, which in turn depends on the metric. One
integrability condition we shall use below follows from the general formula
for commutators of covariant derivatives of tensors:
\[
  \nabla_\nu\nabla_\sigma\xi_{\rho\mu}
  -\nabla_\sigma\nabla_\nu\xi_{\rho\mu}
  =
  R^\lambda{}_{\rho\sigma\nu}\xi_{\lambda\mu}
  +R^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma\nu}\xi_{\rho\lambda}.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:13.1.9} will satisfy this condition if and only if
\begin{align*}
  R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}\nabla_\nu\xi_\lambda
  -R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu}\nabla_\sigma\xi_\lambda
  &+
  \left(
    \nabla_\nu R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}
    -\nabla_\sigma R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu}
  \right)\xi_\lambda
  \\
  &=
  -R^\lambda{}_{\rho\sigma\nu}\nabla_\mu\xi_\lambda
  -R^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma\nu}\nabla_\lambda\xi_\rho,
\end{align*}
or, using \eqref{eq:13.1.5},
\begin{align}
  \Bigl[
    -R^\lambda{}_{\rho\sigma\nu}\delta_\mu^\kappa
    +R^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma\nu}\delta_\rho^\kappa
    -R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}\delta_\nu^\kappa
    +R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu}\delta_\sigma^\kappa
  \Bigr]\nabla_\kappa\xi_\lambda
  &=
  \Bigl[
    \nabla_\nu R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\rho\mu}
    -\nabla_\sigma R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu}
  \Bigr]\xi_\lambda.
  \tag{13.1.12}\label{eq:13.1.12}
\end{align}
These conditions are of course empty for a flat space, but in general they
will impose linear relations among the \(\xi_\lambda\) and
\(\nabla_\kappa\xi_\lambda\) at any given point. Alternatively, if we know
something about the Killing vectors admitted by an unknown metric, then we can
use \eqref{eq:13.1.12} to learn something about its curvature tensor. In this
way, we shall be able in the following sections to deduce the form of a
maximally symmetric metric from its isometries.

It should be emphasized that the existence of a definite number of independent
Killing vectors does not depend on a particular choice of coordinate system.
If \(\xi^\mu(x)\) is a Killing vector of a metric \(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\), then
by performing a coordinate transformation \(x^\mu\longrightarrow x'^\mu\)
we obtain a metric
\[
  g'_{\mu\nu}(x')
  =
  \frac{\partial x^\rho}{\partial x'^\mu}
  \frac{\partial x^\sigma}{\partial x'^\nu}
  g_{\rho\sigma}(x)
\]
and, since \eqref{eq:13.1.5} is generally covariant, this obviously has a
Killing vector
\[
  \xi'^\mu(x')
  =
  \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\nu}\xi^\nu(x).
\]
If \(M\) Killing vectors \(\xi_\mu^{\,n}(x)\) are independent, then so are
the \(M\) Killing vectors \(\xi'{}_\mu^{\,n}(x')\), for any linear relation
among the \(\xi'{}^{\,n}\) would imply a linear relation among the
\(\xi^{\,n}\). Thus the maximal symmetry of a given space is an inner
property, not depending on how we choose the coordinate system. In particular,
it follows that any space with vanishing curvature tensor is maximally
symmetric; the converse, however, is not true. It is also easy to see that the
homogeneity or isotropy of a given space is independent of the choice of
coordinates. As far as these simple symmetries are concerned, we have
accomplished the task laid out in the introduction to this chapter, that of
describing symmetries of the metric in a generally covariant language.
