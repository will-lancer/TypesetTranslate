\subsection{Spaces with Maximally Symmetric Subspaces}\label{sec:13.5}

In many cases of physical importance, the whole space (or space-time) is not
maximally symmetric, but it can be decomposed into maximally symmetric
subspaces. For instance, a spherically symmetric three-dimensional space can
be decomposed into a family of spherical surfaces centered on the origin,
each of which is described by a metric of the form \eqref{eq:13.3.28}. Also,
in Chapter~14 we shall deal with space-times in which the metric is
spherically symmetric and homogeneous on each ``plane'' of constant time.

We shall see here that the maximal symmetry of a family of subspaces imposes
very strong constraints on the metric of the whole space. In order to state
and prove these results, let us first adopt a suitable coordinate system. If
the whole space has \(N\) dimensions and its maximally symmetric subspaces
have \(M\) dimensions, then we can distinguish these subspaces from each
other with \(N-M\) coordinate labels \(v^a\), and locate points within each
subspace with \(M\) coordinates \(u^i\). Some illustrations are given in
Table~\ref{tab:13.1}.

We say that the subspaces with constant \(v^a\) are \emph{maximally
symmetric} if the metric of the whole space is invariant under a group of
infinitesimal transformations
\begin{equation}
  u^i\longrightarrow u'^i
  =
  u^i+\epsilon\xi^i(u,v),
  \tag{13.5.1}\label{eq:13.5.1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  v^a\longrightarrow v'^a=v^a,
  \tag{13.5.2}\label{eq:13.5.2}
\end{equation}
with \(M(M+1)/2\) independent Killing vectors \(\xi^i\). These are
transformations of the general form \eqref{eq:13.1.3}, but with the special
feature that the \(v^a\) are invariant, so that
\begin{equation}
  \xi^a(u,v)=0.
  \tag{13.5.3}\label{eq:13.5.3}
\end{equation}
Note that, although these transformations affect only the \(u\) variables,
there is no reason why the transformation rules cannot depend parametrically
on the labels \(v^a\) of the particular subspace being transformed. Also, our
statement that there are \(M(M+1)/2\) ``independent'' Killing vectors should
be construed to mean that there are \(M(M+1)/2\) Killing vectors that are not
subject to any linear relations with \(u\)-independent coefficients.

The general result that governs the structure of such spaces is contained in
the following theorem. It is always possible to choose the \(u\) coordinates
so that the metric of the whole space is given by
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  g_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x^\mu\dd x^\nu
  =
  g_{ab}(v)\,\dd v^a\dd v^b
  +f(v)\bar g_{ij}(u)\,\dd u^i\dd u^j,
  \tag{13.5.4}\label{eq:13.5.4}
\end{equation}
where \(g_{ab}(v)\) and \(f(v)\) are functions of the \(v\) coordinates
alone, and \(\bar g_{ij}(u)\) is a function of the \(u\) coordinates alone
that is by itself the metric of an \(M\)-dimensional maximally symmetric
space. (The summation convention is in force, with \(a,b,\ldots\) running
over the \(N-M\) labels of the \(v\) coordinates, and
\(i,j,k,l,\ldots\) running over the \(M\) labels of the \(u\) coordinates.)

To begin our proof, we set down the condition that \eqref{eq:13.5.1} is an
isometry of the whole metric \(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\). It is convenient to use this
condition here in its original form \eqref{eq:13.1.4}, rather than in the
more elegant covariant form \eqref{eq:13.1.5}. Each index
\(\mu,\nu,\rho\) in \eqref{eq:13.1.4} now runs over the \(N-M\) coordinate
labels of the \(v^a\) and the \(M\) coordinate labels of the \(u^i\), so
\eqref{eq:13.1.4} now yields three separate equations. For
\(\rho=i,\sigma=j\) we have
\begin{equation}
  0
  =
  \partial_i\xi^k(u,v)\,g_{kj}(u,v)
  +\partial_j\xi^k(u,v)\,g_{ik}(u,v)
  +\xi^k(u,v)\partial_k g_{ij}(u,v),
  \tag{13.5.5}\label{eq:13.5.5}
\end{equation}
for \(\rho=i,\sigma=a\) we have
\begin{equation}
  0
  =
  \partial_i\xi^k(u,v)\,g_{ka}(u,v)
  +\partial_a\xi^k(u,v)\,g_{ik}(u,v)
  +\xi^k(u,v)\partial_k g_{ia}(u,v),
  \tag{13.5.6}\label{eq:13.5.6}
\end{equation}
and for \(\rho=a,\sigma=b\) we have
\begin{equation}
  0
  =
  \partial_a\xi^k(u,v)\,g_{kb}(u,v)
  +\partial_b\xi^k(u,v)\,g_{ka}(u,v)
  +\xi^k(u,v)\partial_k g_{ab}(u,v).
  \tag{13.5.7}\label{eq:13.5.7}
\end{equation}

Of these three equations, the first simply tells us that \(g_{ij}(u,v)\)
must, for each fixed set of \(v^a\), be the metric of an \(M\)-dimensional
space, with coordinates \(u^i\), which admits the Killing vector \(\xi^i\).
We assume here that there are \(M(M+1)/2\) such independent Killing vectors,
so this means that the submatrix \(g_{ij}(u,v)\) is by itself a maximally
symmetric metric for each set of fixed \(v^a\). According to the arguments
of Section~\ref{sec:13.1}, it also follows that at any given point \(u_0\)
we can find Killing vectors \(\xi^k(u,v)\) for which \(\xi^k(u_0,v)\) and
\(\xi_{k;l}(u_0,v)\) take arbitrary values, subject only to the requirement
that \(\xi_{k;l}=-\xi_{l;k}\). Thus the metric \(g_{ij}(u,v)\) is for each
\(v\) both homogeneous in \(u\) and isotropic about any point.

The other two equations contain information about the other elements
\(g_{ai}\) and \(g_{ab}\), and also about the \(v\)-dependence of the Killing
vectors. (This \(v\)-dependence is not entirely arbitrary. For instance, it
is true that by redefining the \(u\)-coordinates we can always arrange that
the metric \(g_{ij}(u,v)\) has \(v\)-independent Killing vectors
\(\bar\xi^i(u)\), but the Killing vectors \(\xi^i(u,v)\) of the whole space
will then in general be linear combinations of the \(\bar\xi^i(u)\), with
coefficients that can depend on the \(v\)-coordinates.) In order to
disentangle the different information contained in \eqref{eq:13.5.6} and
\eqref{eq:13.5.7}, it is extremely useful to choose a new set of coordinates
\(u'^i(u,v)\) of the maximally symmetric subspaces, so that \(g'_{ja}\)
vanishes. Suppose for a moment that we can find a function
\(U^k(v;u_0)\) that satisfies the differential equation
\begin{equation}
  g_{ik}(U,v)\partial_a U^k
  =
  -g_{ia}(U,v),
  \tag{13.5.8}\label{eq:13.5.8}
\end{equation}
with the initial condition that
\begin{equation}
  U^k(v_0;u_0)=u_0^k
  \tag{13.5.9}\label{eq:13.5.9}
\end{equation}
at some point \(v_0^a\). The coordinates \(u'^i,v'^a\) are then defined by
\begin{equation}
  u^i=U^i(v';u'),
  \tag{13.5.10}\label{eq:13.5.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  v^a=v'^a.
  \tag{13.5.11}\label{eq:13.5.11}
\end{equation}
In this coordinate system, the metric has
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  g'_{ja}(u',v')
  &=
  \frac{\partial u^l}{\partial u'^j}
  \frac{\partial u^k}{\partial v'^a}g_{lk}(u,v)
  +
  \frac{\partial u^l}{\partial u'^j}g_{la}(u,v)
  \\
  &=
  \frac{\partial U^l(v';u')}{\partial u'^j}
  \left[
    \frac{\partial U^k(v';u')}{\partial v'^a}
    g_{lk}(U,v')
    +g_{la}(U,v')
  \right],
  \end{aligned}
\]
and thus \eqref{eq:13.5.8} gives
\begin{equation}
  g'_{ia}=0.
  \tag{13.5.12}\label{eq:13.5.12}
\end{equation}

Thus we can construct \(u'\) coordinates in which \(g'_{ia}\) vanishes, if
we can find solutions of the differential equation \eqref{eq:13.5.8} with
arbitrary initial conditions \eqref{eq:13.5.9}. We can rewrite
\eqref{eq:13.5.8} in the equivalent form
\begin{equation}
  \partial_a U^k=-F_a^k(U,v),
  \tag{13.5.13}\label{eq:13.5.13}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  F_a^k(U,v)
  \equiv
  \bar g^{ki}(U,v)g_{ia}(U,v),
  \tag{13.5.14}\label{eq:13.5.14}
\end{equation}
and \(\bar g^{ij}\) is the matrix reciprocal to \(g_{ij}\), that is,
\begin{equation}
  \bar g^{ij}g_{jk}=\delta^i_k.
  \tag{13.5.15}\label{eq:13.5.15}
\end{equation}
(The bar is to remind us that the \(ij\) element \(\bar g^{ij}\) of the
matrix reciprocal to \(g_{ij}\) is not the same as the \(ij\) element
\(g^{ij}\) of the matrix \(g^{\mu\nu}\) reciprocal to \(g_{\mu\nu}\).)
When there is only one \(v\) coordinate, as will be the case in our chapters
on cosmology, it is obvious that \eqref{eq:13.5.13} can be solved with
arbitrary initial conditions. In the general case, we shall have to do some
work to prove that \eqref{eq:13.5.13} is integrable.

Our method is the same as in Section~\ref{sec:13.2}: we try to solve
\eqref{eq:13.5.13} in a neighborhood of \(v_0\) with a power series in
\(v-v_0\),
\begin{equation}
  U^k
  =
  \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
  \frac1{n!}
  c^k{}_{a_1\ldots a_n}
  (v^{a_1}-v_0^{a_1})\cdots(v^{a_n}-v_0^{a_n}).
  \tag{13.5.16}\label{eq:13.5.16}
\end{equation}
Clearly the initial conditions \eqref{eq:13.5.9} are satisfied if we choose
the \(n=0\) coefficient as
\[
  c^k=u_0^k,
\]
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.13} is satisfied to zero order in \(v-v_0\) if we
choose
\[
  c^k{}_a=-F_a^k(u_0,v_0).
\]
Now, proceeding by mathematical induction, suppose that we are able to
choose the terms in \eqref{eq:13.5.16} up to order \((v-v_0)^n\) so that
\eqref{eq:13.5.13} is satisfied to order \((v-v_0)^{n-1}\). Then we can
use these terms to calculate the term in \(F_a^k(U,v)\) of order
\((v-v_0)^n\). Let us write this term as
\[
  \left[
    F_a^k\bigl(U(v;u_0),v\bigr)
  \right]_{\text{order }n}
  =
  \frac1{n!}
  f^k{}_{a b_1\ldots b_n}
  (v^{b_1}-v_0^{b_1})\cdots(v^{b_n}-v_0^{b_n}).
\]
Then \eqref{eq:13.5.16} will satisfy \eqref{eq:13.5.13} to order
\((v-v_0)^n\) if we choose the term in \(U\) of order \(n+1\) as
\[
  \left[U^k(v;u_0)\right]_{n+1}
  =
  -\frac1{(n+1)!}
  f^k{}_{a b_1\ldots b_n}
  (v^a-v_0^a)
  (v^{b_1}-v_0^{b_1})\cdots(v^{b_n}-v_0^{b_n}),
\]
provided that \(f\) is symmetric in all its subscripts. Since
\(f^k{}_{a b_1\ldots b_n}\) can obviously be chosen symmetric in the
\(b\)'s, it is sufficient to require that it should also be symmetric
between \(a\) and any \(b\), or equivalently that
\[
  \left[
    \partial_b F_a^k\bigl(U(v;u_0),v\bigr)
  \right]_{\text{order }n-1}
\]
should be symmetric in \(a\) and \(b\). But \(U\) is assumed to satisfy
\eqref{eq:13.5.13} to order \((v-v_0)^{n-1}\), so this condition is
satisfied if
\[
  \left[
    -\partial_lF_a^k(u,v)F_b^l(u,v)
    +\partial_bF_a^k(u,v)
  \right]_{u=U(v;u_0)}
\]
is symmetric in \(a\) and \(b\). We thus conclude that
\eqref{eq:13.5.13} is integrable if
\begin{equation}
  \partial_lF_a^k\,F_b^l-\partial_bF_a^k
  =
  \partial_lF_b^k\,F_a^l-\partial_aF_b^k
  \tag{13.5.17}\label{eq:13.5.17}
\end{equation}
for all \(u,v\).

In order to prove that \eqref{eq:13.5.17} is indeed satisfied, we return to
the Killing-vector condition \eqref{eq:13.5.6}. Multiplying with
\(\bar g^{il}\), we have
\[
  \partial_a\xi^l
  =
  -\bar g^{il}\partial_i\xi^m\,g_{ma}
  -\bar g^{il}\xi^k\partial_k g_{ia}.
\]
Also, multiplying \eqref{eq:13.5.5} with
\(\bar g^{il}\bar g^{jm}\) gives
\[
  \bar g^{il}\partial_i\xi^m
  +\bar g^{jm}\partial_j\xi^l
  =
  -\xi^k\bar g^{il}\bar g^{jm}\partial_k g_{ij}
  =
  \xi^k\partial_k\bar g^{lm},
\]
so
\[
  \partial_a\xi^l
  =
  \bar g^{jm}\partial_j\xi^l\,g_{ma}
  -\xi^k\partial_k\bar g^{lm}\,g_{ma}
  -\xi^k\bar g^{lm}\partial_k g_{ma}.
\]
Recalling \eqref{eq:13.5.14}, we can write this as
\begin{equation}
  \partial_a\xi^l
  =
  F_a^j\partial_j\xi^l-\xi^k\partial_kF_a^l.
  \tag{13.5.18}\label{eq:13.5.18}
\end{equation}

Now differentiate with respect to \(v^b\); this gives
\[
  \partial_b\partial_a\xi^l
  =
  F_a^j\partial_j(\partial_b\xi^l)
  +\partial_bF_a^j\,\partial_j\xi^l
  -\partial_b\xi^k\,\partial_kF_a^l
  -\xi^k\partial_b\partial_kF_a^l,
\]
or, using \eqref{eq:13.5.18} on the right-hand side,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \partial_b\partial_a\xi^l
  ={}&
  F_a^jF_b^i\,\partial_j\partial_i\xi^l
  +F_a^j\partial_jF_b^i\,\partial_i\xi^l
  -F_a^j\partial_j\partial_kF_b^l\,\xi^k
  -F_a^j\partial_kF_b^l\,\partial_j\xi^k
  \\
  &+\partial_bF_a^j\,\partial_j\xi^l
  -F_b^i\partial_i\xi^k\,\partial_kF_a^l
  +\partial_iF_b^k\partial_kF_a^l\,\xi^i
  -\partial_b\partial_kF_a^l\,\xi^k.
  \end{aligned}
\]
But this must be symmetric between \(a\) and \(b\), so
\begin{align}
  0={}&
  \left(
    F_a^j\partial_jF_b^i
    -F_b^j\partial_jF_a^i
    +\partial_bF_a^i
    -\partial_aF_b^i
  \right)\partial_i\xi^l
  \notag\\
  &+
  \Bigl(
    -F_a^j\partial_k\partial_jF_b^l
    +F_b^j\partial_k\partial_jF_a^l
    +\partial_kF_b^j\partial_jF_a^l
    -\partial_kF_a^j\partial_jF_b^l
    \notag\\
  &\hspace{48mm}
    -\partial_b\partial_kF_a^l
    +\partial_a\partial_kF_b^l
  \Bigr)\xi^k.
  \tag{13.5.19}\label{eq:13.5.19}
\end{align}
We have already remarked that our assumption that there are \(M(M+1)/2\)
independent Killing vectors allows us at any given point to find Killing
vectors for which \(\xi^k\) vanishes and for which
\(\xi_{k,j}=g_{kl}\partial_j\xi^l\) is an arbitrary antisymmetric matrix.
In particular, we can at any given point choose \(\xi^i\) so that
\[
  \xi^k=0,
  \qquad
  \xi_{k,j}
  =
  g_{kl}\partial_j\xi^l
  =
  \delta_{km}\delta_{jn}-\delta_{kn}\delta_{jm}.
\]
Hence, multiplying \eqref{eq:13.5.19} with \(g_{li}\) and setting
\(k=n\neq m\), we find that
\[
  F_a^j\partial_jF_b^m-F_b^j\partial_jF_a^m
  =
  \partial_aF_b^m-\partial_bF_a^m,
\]
which is just the desired relation \eqref{eq:13.5.17}. The coefficient of
\(\xi^k\) in \eqref{eq:13.5.19} must also vanish, but we do not need this
information here.

% immediately used is (13.5.19).

To return now to the main line of our proof: having proved
\eqref{eq:13.5.17}, we know that \eqref{eq:13.5.13} is integrable, so we
can construct the coordinates \(u'^i,v'^a\) defined by
\eqref{eq:13.5.10} and \eqref{eq:13.5.11}, in which the metric components
\(g'_{ia}\) vanish. Let us do so, and drop the primes so that now
\begin{equation}
  g_{ia}=0.
  \tag{13.5.20}\label{eq:13.5.20}
\end{equation}
The Killing-vector conditions \eqref{eq:13.5.6}, \eqref{eq:13.5.7} now
read
\begin{equation}
  0=\partial_a\xi^k\,g_{ik},
  \tag{13.5.21}\label{eq:13.5.21}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  0=\xi^k\partial_k g_{ab}.
  \tag{13.5.22}\label{eq:13.5.22}
\end{equation}
Since \(g_{ik}\) is nonsingular, it follows from \eqref{eq:13.5.21} that
\begin{equation}
  \partial_a\xi^k=0.
  \tag{13.5.23}\label{eq:13.5.23}
\end{equation}
Also, we have noted that at each point we can find Killing vectors for which
\(\xi^k\) takes any arbitrary value, so the coefficient of \(\xi^k\) in
\eqref{eq:13.5.22} must vanish:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_k g_{ab}=0.
  \tag{13.5.24}\label{eq:13.5.24}
\end{equation}

It only remains to show that \(g_{ij}(u,v)\) is \(v\)-independent, except
for a possible factor \(f(v)\). We use the fact that for any fixed \(v_0\)
there are \(M(M+1)/2\) independent Killing vectors of \(g_{ij}(u,v_0)\)
that, according to \eqref{eq:13.5.23}, are also Killing vectors of
\(g_{ij}(u,v)\) for any \(v\). Each one of these Killing vectors
\(\xi^i(u)\) will then satisfy \eqref{eq:13.5.5} at \(v=v_0\) and for
general \(v\):
\[
  0
  =
  \partial_i\xi^k(u)\,g_{kj}(u,v_0)
  +\partial_j\xi^k(u)\,g_{ki}(u,v_0)
  +\xi^k(u)\partial_k g_{ij}(u,v_0),
\]
\[
  0
  =
  \partial_i\xi^k(u)\,g_{kj}(u,v)
  +\partial_j\xi^k(u)\,g_{ki}(u,v)
  +\xi^k(u)\partial_k g_{ij}(u,v).
\]
We can interpret these two equations as saying that \(g_{ij}(u,v)\) is a
maximally form-invariant tensor [in the sense of Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.4.3}] in
the maximally symmetric space with metric \(g_{ij}(u,v_0)\). It follows
then, according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:13.4.10} and \eqref{eq:13.4.11}, that
the tensor \(g_{ij}(u,v)\) is proportional to the metric \(g_{ij}(u,v_0)\),
with a \(u\)-independent coefficient:
\[
  g_{ij}(u,v)=f(v,v_0)g_{ij}(u,v_0).
\]
The value of \(v_0\) can be fixed in any way we like, so we can suppress the
label \(v_0\), and write this as
\begin{equation}
  g_{ij}(u,v)=f(v)\bar g_{ij}(u),
  \tag{13.5.25}\label{eq:13.5.25}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  f(v)\equiv f(v,v_0),
  \qquad
  \bar g_{ij}(u)\equiv g_{ij}(u,v_0).
  \tag{13.5.26}\label{eq:13.5.26}
\end{equation}
Putting together \eqref{eq:13.5.20}, \eqref{eq:13.5.24}, and
\eqref{eq:13.5.25} now shows that the metric \(g_{\mu\nu}(u,v)\) does have
the form given by \eqref{eq:13.5.4}, and \eqref{eq:13.5.26} and
\eqref{eq:13.5.5} with \(v=v_0\) show that \(\bar g_{ij}(u)\) is a
maximally symmetric metric, as was to be proved.

This theorem could also have been proved under the apparently weaker
assumption that the whole space can be decomposed into subspaces that are
isotropic about every point. This assumption means that at any point
\(u_0,v\) we can find Killing vectors of the whole space with \(\xi^a=0\),
for which \(\xi^i\) vanishes at \(u_0,v\), and for which \(\xi_{i;k}\) at
\(u_0,v\) is an arbitrary antisymmetric matrix. In particular, we can find
\(M(M-1)/2\) Killing vectors \(\xi^{\mu(lm)}(u,v;u_0)\) with
\[
  \xi^{a(lm)}(u,v;u_0)=0,
\]
\[
  \xi^{i(lm)}(u,v;u_0)
  =
  -\xi^{i(ml)}(u,v;u_0),
\]
for which
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \xi_{i;j}^{(lm)}(u_0,v;u_0)
  &=
  g_{ik}(u_0,v)
  \left.
    \partial_j\xi^{k(lm)}(u,v;u_0)
  \right|_{u=u_0}
  \\
  &=
  \delta_i^l\delta_j^m-\delta_i^m\delta_j^l.
  \end{aligned}
\]
We can then define
\[
  \xi^{\mu(l)}(u,v;u_0)
  \equiv
  \frac{\partial}{\partial u_0^m}
  \xi^{\mu(lm)}(u,v;u_0),
\]
and the arguments of Section~\ref{sec:13.1} show that these are Killing
vectors \emph{of the whole space}, with
\[
  \xi^{a(l)}(u,v;u_0)=0
\]
and with
\[
  \xi^{i(l)}(u_0,v;u_0)
  =
  -\frac1{M-1}\bar g^{il}(u_0,v).
\]
The existence of the \(M(M+1)/2\) independent Killing vectors
\(\xi^{\mu(lm)}\) and \(\xi^{\mu(l)}\) shows that the space does have
maximally symmetric subspaces after all.

In all cases of practical importance, the maximally symmetric subspaces are
\emph{spaces}, as opposed to space-times, so all eigenvalues of the
submatrix \(g_{ij}\) are positive. In this case, we can use
\eqref{eq:13.3.23}, \eqref{eq:13.3.24}, or \eqref{eq:13.3.25} to
evaluate \(\bar g_{ij}\,\dd u^i\dd u^j\), and \eqref{eq:13.5.4} then gives
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  g_{ab}(v)\,\dd v^a\dd v^b
  +f(v)
  \left[
    \dd\mathbf{u}^2
    \frac{k(\mathbf{u}\cdot\dd\mathbf{u})^2}
         {1-k\mathbf{u}^2}
  \right],
  \tag{13.5.27}\label{eq:13.5.27}
\end{equation}
where \(f(v)\) is positive and
\begin{equation}
  k=
  \begin{cases}
    +1, & \text{if the maximally symmetric subspace has \(K>0\),}\\
    -1, & \text{if the maximally symmetric subspace has \(K<0\),}\\
    0,  & \text{if the maximally symmetric subspace has \(K=0\).}
  \end{cases}
  \tag{13.5.28}\label{eq:13.5.28}
\end{equation}
[We have absorbed the curvature constant \(\lvert K\rvert^{-1}\) appearing
in \eqref{eq:13.3.23} and \eqref{eq:13.3.24} into the function \(f(v)\).]
Let us now use this formula to treat the special cases listed in
Table~\ref{tab:13.1}.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{(A) Spherically Symmetric Space.}
Suppose that the dimensionality of the whole space is \(N=3\), that all
eigenvalues of its metric are positive, and that it has maximally symmetric
two-dimensional subspaces with positive curvature. Then there is one
\(v\)-coordinate, which we can call \(r\), and two \(u\)-coordinates, which
we can replace with angles \(\theta,\phi\) defined by
\begin{equation}
  u^1=\sin\theta\cos\phi,
  \qquad
  u^2=\sin\theta\sin\phi.
  \tag{13.5.29}\label{eq:13.5.29}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:13.5.27}, with \(k=1\), then gives
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  g(r)\,\dd r^2
  +f(r)
  \left(
    \dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2
  \right),
  \tag{13.5.30}\label{eq:13.5.30}
\end{equation}
with \(f(r)\) and \(g(r)\) positive functions of \(r\).

\medskip
\noindent\emph{(B) Spherically Symmetric Space-Time.}
Suppose that the dimensionality of the whole space-time is \(N=4\), that
three of the eigenvalues of its metric are positive and one is negative, and
that it has maximally symmetric two-dimensional subspaces whose metric has
positive eigenvalues and positive curvature. Then there are two
\(v\)-coordinates, which we can call \(r\) and \(t\), and two
\(u\)-coordinates, which can be replaced with \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) as in
\eqref{eq:13.5.29}. Equation~\eqref{eq:13.5.27}, with \(k=1\), then gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \dd s^2={}&
  g_{tt}(r,t)\,\dd t^2
  +2g_{rt}(r,t)\,\dd r\,\dd t
  +g_{rr}(r,t)\,\dd r^2
  \\
  &+f(r,t)
  \left(
    \dd\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2
  \right),
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{13.5.31}\label{eq:13.5.31}
\end{equation}
where \(f(r,t)\) is a positive function and \(g_{ab}(r,t)\) is a
\(2\times2\) matrix with one positive and one negative eigenvalue.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{(C) Spherically Symmetric Homogeneous Space-Time.}
Suppose that the dimensionality of the whole space-time is \(N=4\), that
three of the eigenvalues of its metric are positive and one is negative, and
that it has maximally symmetric three-dimensional subspaces whose metric has
positive eigenvalues and arbitrary curvature. Then there is one
\(v\)-coordinate and three \(u\)-coordinates, and \eqref{eq:13.5.27} gives
\[
  \dd s^2
  =
  g(v)\,\dd v^2
  +f(v)
  \left[
    \dd\mathbf{u}^2
    +\frac{k(\mathbf{u}\cdot\dd\mathbf{u})^2}
          {1-k\mathbf{u}^2}
  \right],
\]
where \(f(v)\) is a positive function and \(g(v)\) is a negative function
of \(v\). It is very convenient to define new coordinates
\(t,r,\theta,\phi\) by
\[
  \int\!\left[-g(v)\right]^{1/2}\dd v=t,
\]
\[
  u^1=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,
  \qquad
  u^2=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,
  \qquad
  u^3=r\cos\theta.
\]
We then have
\begin{equation}
  \dd s^2
  =
  -\dd t^2
  +R^2(t)
  \left[
    \frac{\dd r^2}{1-kr^2}
    +r^2\dd\theta^2
    +r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dd\phi^2
  \right],
  \tag{13.5.32}\label{eq:13.5.32}
\end{equation}
where \(R(t)\equiv\sqrt{f(v)}\).

The first two examples show how it is possible to capture the essence of
spherical symmetry by giving a qualitative description of a space or
space-time in terms of dimensionalities, signs of eigenvalues and
curvatures, and the maximal symmetry of its subspaces. The metrics
\eqref{eq:13.5.30} and \eqref{eq:13.5.31} are just what we would have
expected on more elementary grounds; indeed, \eqref{eq:13.5.31} was our
starting point in Section~\ref{sec:11.7}.

On the other hand, our third example leads to a result that could not easily
have been anticipated. It is true that Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.32} was already
derived in Section~\ref{sec:11.9} as the metric inside a spherically
symmetric collapsing star of uniform density and zero pressure. The
beautiful new thing we have learned in this chapter is that this metric can
be derived solely from the assumption of homogeneity and isotropy, with no
use of the Einstein field equations.
