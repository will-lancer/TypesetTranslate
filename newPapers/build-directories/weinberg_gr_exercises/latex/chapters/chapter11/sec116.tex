\subsection{Stars of Uniform Density}\label{sec:11.6}

General relativity finds an interesting application to one other class of
stable stars, those consisting of incompressible fluids, with equation of
state
\begin{equation}
  \rho=\text{constant}.
  \tag{11.6.1}\label{eq:11.6.1}
\end{equation}
These stars are of interest, not because they actually exist (they do not),
but because they are simple enough to allow an exact solution of Einstein's
equations,\hyperref[ch11-ref-25]{\textsuperscript{25}} and because they set an
upper limit to the gravitational redshift of spectral lines from the surface
of any star.\hyperref[ch11-ref-26]{\textsuperscript{26}}

With \(\rho\) constant, the fundamental equation \eqref{eq:11.1.13} may be
written
\begin{equation}
  -\frac{p'(r)}
  {[\rho+p(r)][\rho/3+p(r)]}
  =
  4\pi Gr
  \left[1-\frac{8\pi G\rho r^2}{3}\right]^{-1}.
  \tag{11.6.2}\label{eq:11.6.2}
\end{equation}
The pressure must now be determined by integrating \emph{inward} from the
surface where \(p=0\), rather than outward, as for more realistic models. This
gives
\[
  \frac{p(r)+\rho}{3p(r)+\rho}
  =
  \left[
    \frac{1-8\pi G\rho R^2/3}
    {1-8\pi G\rho r^2/3}
  \right]^{1/2}.
\]
Solving for \(p(r)\), and expressing \(\rho\) in terms of the stellar mass,
\begin{equation}
  \rho=\frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}\qquad\text{for }r<R,
  \tag{11.6.3}\label{eq:11.6.3}
\end{equation}
we find
\begin{equation}
  p(r)
  =
  \frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}
  \left\{
    \frac{
      [1-2MG/R]^{1/2}
      -[1-2MGr^2/R^3]^{1/2}
    }{
      [1-2MGr^2/R^3]^{1/2}
      -3[1-2MG/R]^{1/2}
    }
  \right\}.
  \tag{11.6.4}\label{eq:11.6.4}
\end{equation}
The metric component \(A(r)\) is immediately given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.11}:
\begin{equation}
  A(r)=\left[1-\frac{2MGr^2}{R^3}\right]^{-1},
  \tag{11.6.5}\label{eq:11.6.5}
\end{equation}
whereas \(B(r)\) can be calculated by using Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.4} in the
integral \eqref{eq:11.1.16}:
\begin{equation}
  B(r)
  =
  \frac14
  \left[
    3\left(1-\frac{2MG}{R}\right)^{1/2}
    -\left(1-\frac{2MGr^2}{R^3}\right)^{1/2}
  \right]^2.
  \tag{11.6.6}\label{eq:11.6.6}
\end{equation}
The most interesting feature of this solution is that it does not make sense
for all values of \(M\) and \(R\). The pressure given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.4} will become infinite at a point \(r_\infty\) where
\begin{equation}
  r_\infty^2=9R^2-\frac{4R^3}{MG}.
  \tag{11.6.7}\label{eq:11.6.7}
\end{equation}
(Also, the metric becomes singular at \(r_\infty\) because
\(B(r_\infty)\) vanishes.) But the pressure is a scalar, and so an infinity
in \(p(r)\) cannot be blamed on an injudicious choice of coordinate system.
We must see to it that \(p(r)\) is not singular for any real \(r\), and the
only way to accomplish this is to have \(r_\infty^2\) negative, or
\begin{equation}
  \frac{MG}{R}<\frac49.
  \tag{11.6.8}\label{eq:11.6.8}
\end{equation}
Note that the Schwarzschild radius \(2MG\) is then less than \(8/9\) the
actual radius \(R\), so there is no singularity in either the exterior
solution \eqref{eq:11.1.17} or the interior solutions
\eqref{eq:11.6.5} and \eqref{eq:11.6.6}.

This is not the first time that we have discovered an upper bound on the
absolute value \(MG/R\) of the gravitational potential of a star. We learned
in Section~\ref{sec:11.4} that for a stable ideal-gas neutron star, \(MG/R\)
is never greater than \(0.36/3.2\), or \(0.11\). [See
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.4.15} and \eqref{eq:11.4.16}.] Is there then an absolute
upper limit to \(MG/R\) imposed by the structure of the Einstein equations,
irrespective of the equation of state?

To frame this question as a mathematical problem, we consider \(\rho\) as an
arbitrary finite positive function, subject only to these general
requirements:

\emph{(A)} The radius \(R\) is fixed, with
\begin{equation}
  \rho(r)=0\qquad\text{for }r>R.
  \tag{11.6.9}\label{eq:11.6.9}
\end{equation}

\emph{(B)} The mass \(M\) is fixed, with
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^R4\pi r^2\rho(r)\,dr=M.
  \tag{11.6.10}\label{eq:11.6.10}
\end{equation}

\emph{(C)} The metric coefficient \(A(r)\) given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.11} must not be singular, so
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}(r)<\frac r{2G},
  \tag{11.6.11}\label{eq:11.6.11}
\end{equation}
where
\[
  \mathcal{M}(r)=\int_0^r4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,dr'.
\]

\emph{(D)} The density \(\rho(r)\) must not increase outward:
\begin{equation}
  \rho'(r)\le0.
  \tag{11.6.12}\label{eq:11.6.12}
\end{equation}
(It is difficult to imagine that a fluid sphere with a larger density near the
surface than near the center could be stable.) Given any function \(\rho(r)\)
satisfying these conditions, we can calculate \(A(r)\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.11}; we can determine \(p(r)\) by integrating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.13} inward from the surface (with the boundary condition
that \(p(R)=0\)); and we can then calculate \(B(r)\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.16}. Equation~\eqref{eq:11.6.11} guarantees that \(A(r)\)
is well behaved, and as long as \(p(r)\) is finite,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.13} will give \(p(r)>0\), and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.16} will give a finite positive-definite \(B(r)\). Thus
any absolute limitations on the input function \(\rho(r)\) (such as an upper
bound on \(MG/R\)) can only come from the condition that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.13} must yield a finite solution for the pressure \(p(r)\).

We shall exploit this condition rather indirectly, by concentrating on the
metric coefficient \(B(r)\) rather than on \(p(r)\) itself. We first derive
an equation that allows \(B(r)\) to be calculated for a given density
function \(\rho(r)\), without having to solve for \(p(r)\). From
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.1.5} and \eqref{eq:11.1.7}, in the binding curvature
convention, we have
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: both the Ricci combination and its Einstein-equation
% source have been reversed from Weinberg's curvature convention.
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  3R_{rr}B+R_{tt}A
  &=
  -B''
  +\frac{B'}2\left(\frac{A'}A+\frac{B'}B\right)
  +\frac{3BA'}{rA}+\frac{B'}r\\
  &=
  16\pi G\rho AB,
  \end{aligned}
\]
or, equivalently,
\[
  B''
  -\frac{B'}2
  \left(\frac{A'}A+\frac{B'}B+\frac2r\right)
  =
  \frac{B}{rA}\left[3A'-16\pi G\rho rA^2\right].
\]
This equation can be linearized by defining
\begin{equation}
  B\equiv\zeta^2.
  \tag{11.6.13}\label{eq:11.6.13}
\end{equation}
Introducing Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.11} for \(A(r)\), and rearranging a bit, we
find
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dr}
  \left[
    \frac1r
    \left(1-\frac{2G\mathcal{M}(r)}r\right)^{1/2}
    \frac{d\zeta(r)}{dr}
  \right]
  =
  G
  \left(1-\frac{2G\mathcal{M}(r)}r\right)^{-1/2}
  \left(\frac{\mathcal{M}(r)}{r^3}\right)'
  \zeta(r).
  \tag{11.6.14}\label{eq:11.6.14}
\end{equation}
The initial conditions at \(r=R\) can be determined directly from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.16}, or from the condition that \(B(r)\) fit smoothly to
the exterior solution \eqref{eq:11.1.17}; either way, we find that
\begin{align}
  \zeta(R)
  &=
  \left(1-\frac{2MG}{R}\right)^{1/2},
  \tag{11.6.15}\label{eq:11.6.15}\\
  \zeta'(R)
  &=
  \frac{MG}{R^2}
  \left(1-\frac{2MG}{R}\right)^{-1/2}.
  \tag{11.6.16}\label{eq:11.6.16}
\end{align}
The solution for \(\zeta(r)\) must be \emph{positive}, because \(\zeta(r)\)
can become negative only if it passes through the value zero, at which point
\(B\) would vanish, and, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.1.16}, \(B\) can
vanish only if the pressure \(p(r)\) has a singularity.

We next proceed to derive an upper bound for \(\zeta(0)\). If \(\zeta\) is
positive, then the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.14} is negative,
because \(3\mathcal{M}(r)/(4\pi r^3)\) is the mean density within the radius
\(r\), and the mean density cannot increase with \(r\) if the density does
not. Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.14} gives
\[
  \frac{d}{dr}
  \left[
    \frac1r
    \left(1-\frac{2G\mathcal{M}(r)}r\right)^{1/2}
    \frac{d\zeta(r)}{dr}
  \right]
  \le0,
\]
the equality being attained only for uniform density. Integrating this
inequality from \(r\) to \(R\) and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.16}, we have
\[
  \zeta'(r)
  \ge
  \frac{MGr}{R^3}
  \left(1-\frac{2G\mathcal{M}(r)}r\right)^{-1/2}.
\]
Integrating again from \(0\) to \(R\) and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.15} gives
\[
  \zeta(0)
  \le
  \left(1-\frac{2MG}{R}\right)^{1/2}
  -\frac{MG}{R^3}
  \int_0^R
  \frac{r\,dr}
  {[1-2G\mathcal{M}(r)/r]^{1/2}}.
\]
The right-hand side is largest when \(\mathcal{M}(r)\) is as small as
possible. For a given mass \(M\) and radius \(R\), the density distribution
with \(\rho'(r)\le0\) that gives an \(\mathcal{M}(r)\) that is everywhere as
small as possible has \(\rho(r)\) constant, in which case
\[
  \mathcal{M}(r)=\frac{Mr^3}{R^3}.
\]
Using this in the integral, our inequality is
\begin{equation}
  \zeta(0)
  \le
  \frac32\left(1-\frac{2MG}{R}\right)^{1/2}-\frac12.
  \tag{11.6.17}\label{eq:11.6.17}
\end{equation}
We have already noted that \(\zeta(r)\) must be positive-definite; hence
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.17} implies that
\begin{equation}
  \frac{MG}{R}<\frac49.
  \tag{11.6.18}\label{eq:11.6.18}
\end{equation}
This is just the upper limit found earlier for stars of uniform density, but
now we know that Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.18} holds for all stars, uniform or not.

It can also be proved that for a given mass and radius, the stable stars with
smallest central pressure are those with uniform density. Hence the central
pressure of any star is not less than the value obtained by setting \(r=0\)
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.6.4}, that is,
\begin{equation}
  p(0)
  \ge
  \frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}
  \left\{
    \frac{[1-2MG/R]^{1/2}-1}
    {1-3[1-2MG/R]^{1/2}}
  \right\}.
  \tag{11.6.19}\label{eq:11.6.19}
\end{equation}
This again shows that \(MG/R\) can never equal the forbidden value \(4/9\).

Our result can be immediately translated into a statement about the redshift
of spectral lines from the surface of any star. According to
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:3.5.3}, \eqref{eq:11.1.1}, and \eqref{eq:11.1.17}, this is
\[
  z\equiv\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda}
  =
  B^{-1/2}(R)-1
  =
  \left(1-\frac{2MG}{R}\right)^{-1/2}-1.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:11.6.18} imposes on \(z\) the upper bound
\begin{equation}
  z<2.
  \tag{11.6.20}\label{eq:11.6.20}
\end{equation}
In fact, there seems to be a large concentration of quasi-stellar radio
sources (see Chapter~14) whose spectral lines show redshifts close to \(1.95\)!
However, we should not jump to the conclusion that these redshifts are
necessarily due to strong gravitational fields, for redshifts near \(z=2\)
require the star to be composed of a nearly incompressible fluid, with
\(d\rho/dp\) very small. This would seem unphysical, since we do not want the
speed of sound \((dp/d\rho)^{1/2}\) to become larger than the speed of
light!\hyperref[ch11-ref-26]{\textsuperscript{26}}
Bondi\hyperref[ch11-ref-27]{\textsuperscript{27}} has shown that for a stable
star with \(dp/d\rho<1\) and with \(p/\rho<1/3\) (as is the case for
particles that interact only electromagnetically and/or in localized
collisions; see Section~2.10) the redshift of spectral lines emitted from the
surface is bounded by \(z<0.615\). In any case, there are quasi-stellar
objects with redshifts \(z>2\), such as 4C~25.5, with \(z=2.358\).

However, there is no theorem that limits the redshifts of light signals from
the \emph{interior} of static spherically symmetric
bodies.\hyperref[ch11-ref-28]{\textsuperscript{28}} For instance, a light
signal from the center of a transparent uniform star would have a redshift
given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:3.5.3}, \eqref{eq:11.1.1}, and
\eqref{eq:11.6.6}:
\[
  1+z
  =
  B^{-1/2}(0)
  =
  \frac{2}{3[1-2MG/R]^{1/2}-1}.
\]
As \(MG/R\) approaches the maximum value \(4/9\), this redshift becomes
infinite. Hoyle and Fowler\hyperref[ch11-ref-29]{\textsuperscript{29}} have
suggested that a quasi-stellar object can consist of a cluster of small dense
stars, with the redshifts arising from emission and absorption in a hot cloud
of gas trapped near the cluster center. It is not yet clear whether the
redshifts of the QSOs arise internally, or from some other cause, such as the
general cosmological recession of distant objects discussed in Chapter~14.
