\subsection[Description of Curvature in N Dimensions*]
{Description of Curvature in \(N\) Dimensions\optionalreading}\label{sec:6.7}

\begin{quote}
\small\emph{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of
development and may be omitted in a first reading.}
\end{quote}

For the moment let us consider a general space of \(N\) dimensions. To count
the number of algebraically independent components of
\(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\), it is convenient to adopt what may be called the
Petrov notation,\hyperref[ch6-ref-2]{\textsuperscript{2}} and think of
\(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) as a matrix
\(R_{(\lambda\mu)(\nu\kappa)}\), with ``indices'' \((\lambda\mu)\) and
\((\nu\kappa)\). From Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.4} we see that each ``index'' takes a
number of independent values equal to the number of independent elements of
an antisymmetric matrix in \(N\) dimensions, or
\(\tfrac12N(N-1)\). From Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.3} we see that
\(R_{(\lambda\mu)(\nu\kappa)}\) is symmetric in these ``indices,'' so
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.6.3} and \eqref{eq:6.6.4} alone would leave
\(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) with a number of independent components equal to
the number of independent elements of a symmetric matrix in
\(\tfrac12N(N-1)\) dimensions, or
\[
  \frac12
  \left[\frac12N(N-1)\right]
  \left[\frac12N(N-1)+1\right]
  =
  \frac18N(N-1)(N^2-N+2).
\]
Equations~\eqref{eq:6.6.3} and \eqref{eq:6.6.4} also make the cyclic sum in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.5} completely antisymmetric, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.5} adds
\(N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)/4!\) further constraints, leaving
\(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) with a number of independent components equal to
\[
  C_N
  =
  \frac18N(N-1)(N^2-N+2)
  -\frac1{24}N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3),
\]
or, combining terms,
\begin{equation}
  C_N
  =
  \frac1{12}N^2(N^2-1).
  \tag{6.7.1}\label{eq:6.7.1}
\end{equation}

In one dimension the curvature tensor \(R_{1111}\) always vanishes, as can
be seen from Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.4} or \eqref{eq:6.6.5}, or from the fact that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.1} gives \(C_1=0\) independent components. It may strike
the reader as odd that a curved line should have zero curvature, but this just
emphasizes that \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) reflects only the inner
properties of the space, not how it is embedded in a higher-dimensional
space. Indeed, we note that the transformation rule for the metric tensor in
one dimension is
\[
  g'_{11}
  =
  \left(\frac{\dd x}{\dd x'}\right)^2g_{11},
\]
so that \(g'_{11}\) can be made equal to \(+1\) or \(-1\) everywhere by
choosing
\[
  x'=\int\dd x\,\sqrt{\lvert g_{11}\rvert}.
\]

In two dimensions Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.1} gives
\(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) only one independent component, which can be
taken as \(R_{1212}\); the other components are related to \(R_{1212}\) by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.6.4}:
\[
  R_{1212}
  =
  -R_{2112}
  =
  -R_{1221}
  =
  R_{2121},
\]
\[
  R_{1111}=R_{1122}=R_{2211}=R_{2222}=0.
\]
These formulas can be summarized more elegantly by
\[
  R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
  =
  (g_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\kappa}
   -g_{\lambda\kappa}g_{\mu\nu})
  \frac{R_{1212}}{g},
\]
where \(g\) is the determinant \(g_{11}g_{22}-g_{12}^2\). Contracting
\(\lambda\) with \(\nu\) gives the Ricci tensor
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mu\kappa}
  =
  g_{\mu\kappa}\frac{R_{1212}}{g},
  \tag{6.7.2}\label{eq:6.7.2}
\end{equation}
and contracting \(\mu\) and \(\kappa\) gives the curvature scalar
\begin{equation}
  R
  =
  \frac{2R_{1212}}{g},
  \tag{6.7.3}\label{eq:6.7.3}
\end{equation}
so the curvature tensor is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
  =
  \frac12R
  (g_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\kappa}
   -g_{\lambda\kappa}g_{\mu\nu}).
  \tag{6.7.4}\label{eq:6.7.4}
\end{equation}
The Gaussian curvature \(K\) discussed in the first section of this book is,
in the curvature convention of this edition,
\begin{equation}
  K
  =
  \frac{R}{2}
  =
  \frac{R_{1212}}{g}.
  \tag{6.7.5}\label{eq:6.7.5}
\end{equation}
The factor \(1/2\) is of purely historical interest; the sign is positive
here because the curvature convention in Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.1.5} is the
opposite of Weinberg's source convention. Equation~\eqref{eq:1.1.12}
follows from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.6.2} and \eqref{eq:6.7.5}.

In three dimensions Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.1} gives the curvature tensor
\(C_3=6\) independent components. This is also the number of independent
components of the Ricci tensor \(R_{\mu\nu}\) in three dimensions, so we may
anticipate that here \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) may be expressed in terms of
\(R_{\mu\nu}\) alone. By using the covariance, symmetry, and contraction
properties of \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\), we may further guess that this
relation is
\begin{align}
  R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
  ={}&
  g_{\lambda\nu}R_{\mu\kappa}
  -g_{\lambda\kappa}R_{\mu\nu}
  -g_{\mu\nu}R_{\lambda\kappa}
  +g_{\mu\kappa}R_{\lambda\nu}
  \notag\\
  &-\frac12
  (g_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\kappa}
   -g_{\lambda\kappa}g_{\mu\nu})R.
  \tag{6.7.6}\label{eq:6.7.6}
\end{align}
To prove that Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.6} is correct, let us adopt a coordinate
system such that \(g_{\mu\nu}\) vanishes for \(\mu\ne\nu\) at some point
\(X\). (This can be managed by choosing
\(\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu\) at \(X\) as the orthogonal matrix that
diagonalizes \(g_{\mu\nu}\) at \(X\).) In this system we have at \(X\)
\[
  R_{12}=g^{33}R_{1323},
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  R_{1323}=g_{33}R_{12},
\]
in agreement with Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.6}. Furthermore,
\[
  R_{11}=g^{22}R_{1212}+g^{33}R_{1313},
  \qquad
  R_{22}=g^{33}R_{2323}+g^{11}R_{2121},
\]
so
\begin{align*}
  g_{22}R_{11}+g_{11}R_{22}
  &=
  2R_{1212}
  +g^{33}(g_{22}R_{1313}+g_{11}R_{2323})
  \\
  &=
  R_{1212}
  +g_{11}g_{22}
   \left(
     g^{11}g^{22}R_{1212}
     +g^{11}g^{33}R_{1313}
     +g^{22}g^{33}R_{2323}
   \right),
\end{align*}
or
\[
  R_{1212}
  =
  g_{22}R_{11}
  +g_{11}R_{22}
  -\frac12g_{11}g_{22}R,
\]
again in agreement with Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.6}. The other independent
components of \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) are
\(R_{1223},R_{1213},R_{2323}\), and \(R_{3131}\), which can be obtained from
\(R_{1323}\) and \(R_{1212}\) by permuting the values \(1,2,3\), so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.6} holds for these components as well. Since
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.6} thus holds in a coordinate system that is orthogonal at
\(X\), and is manifestly covariant, it holds in general.

It is only in four or more dimensions that the full Riemann--Christoffel
tensor \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) is needed to describe the curvature of a
space. For instance, in four dimensions Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.1} gives the
curvature tensor \(C_4=20\) independent components, whereas \(R_{\mu\nu}\)
has only 10 independent components, so \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) has 10
components beyond those which can be expressed in terms of \(R_{\mu\nu}\).

The \(C_N\) components of \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) describe the curvature
of a general
\(N\)-dimensional space, but they do not do so in an invariant manner, for
the values of these components depend not only on the intrinsic properties
of the space but also on the particular coordinate system chosen. The
invariant characterization of a curved space must be in terms of scalars
constructed from \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) and \(g_{\mu\nu}\). Let us count
how many such scalars there are. The \(N^2\) quantities
\(\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\nu\) can for a general coordinate
transformation \(x\to x'\) be made anything we like at a given point \(X\).
Hence the \(C_N\) independent components of \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) and
the \(\tfrac12N(N+1)\) independent
components of \(g_{\mu\nu}\) at this point may by general coordinate
transformation be subjected to \(N^2\) algebraic conditions; the number of
scalars that can be constructed from \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) and
\(g_{\mu\nu}\) is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \frac1{12}N^2(N^2-1)
  +\frac12N(N+1)-N^2
  =
  \frac1{12}N(N-1)(N-2)(N+3).
  \tag{6.7.7}\label{eq:6.7.7}
\end{equation}
The case \(N=2\) is an exception to this argument, because in two dimensions
there is a one-parameter subgroup of coordinate transformations that has no
effect on \(g_{\mu\nu}\) and \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\); the correct number
of invariants here is not zero but one, that is, the curvature scalar \(R\)
itself. This exception does not occur for higher-dimensional spaces, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.7} holds for \(N\geq3\). For \(N=3\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.7} tells us that there are \emph{three} curvature scalars,
which can conveniently be chosen as the three roots of the secular equation
\[
  \det(R_{\mu\nu}-\lambda g_{\mu\nu})=0,
\]
or equivalently as the three quantities
\[
  R,
  \qquad
  R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu},
  \qquad
  \frac{\det R}{\det g}.
\]

For \(N=4\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.7} tells us that there are \emph{fourteen}
curvature scalars. To enumerate them (and for other purposes as well) it is
convenient to decompose \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) into terms that depend
only on the Ricci tensor \(R_{\mu\nu}\), plus a term
\(C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) that has no nontrivial contractions. In
\(N\geq3\) dimensions this decomposition is
\begin{align*}
  R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
  ={}&
  \frac1{N-2}
  \left(
    g_{\lambda\nu}R_{\mu\kappa}
    -g_{\lambda\kappa}R_{\mu\nu}
    -g_{\mu\nu}R_{\lambda\kappa}
    +g_{\mu\kappa}R_{\lambda\nu}
  \right)
  \\
  &-
  \frac{R}{(N-1)(N-2)}
  (g_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\kappa}
   -g_{\lambda\kappa}g_{\mu\nu})
  +C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}.
\end{align*}
The tensor \(C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) is called the \emph{Weyl
tensor}\hyperref[ch6-ref-3]{\textsuperscript{3}} or the \emph{conformal
tensor}. (The latter name is used because the necessary and sufficient
condition for the existence of a coordinate system in which \(g_{\mu\nu}\)
is proportional to a constant matrix throughout the space is that
\(C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\) vanish
everywhere.\hyperref[ch6-ref-4]{\textsuperscript{4}}) This tensor has the same
algebraic properties as \(R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}\), and in addition it
satisfies the \(\tfrac12N(N+1)\) conditions
\[
  C^\lambda{}_{\mu\lambda\kappa}=0,
\]
so the number of its linearly independent components is
\[
  \frac1{12}N^2(N^2-1)-\frac12N(N+1)
  =
  \frac1{12}N(N+1)(N+2)(N-3).
\]
[Equation~\eqref{eq:6.7.6} simply says that
\(C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}=0\) for \(N=3\).] Barring degeneracies, the
curvature invariants can be described as consisting of all the components of
the Weyl tensor for that unique choice of coordinate axes that makes
\(R_{\mu\nu}\) and \(g_{\mu\nu}\) diagonal, the elements of \(g_{\mu\nu}\)
being \(+1\)'s, \(-1\)'s, and \(0\)'s, plus the \(N\) eigenvalues of
\(R_{\mu\nu}\). However, this enumeration breaks down when some of the
eigenvalues of \(R_{\mu\nu}\) are degenerate. A particularly interesting case
is that for \(R_{\mu\nu}=0\), which we shall see in the next chapter describes
physical gravitational fields in empty space. In this case the curvature
invariants for \(N=4\) are the 10 vanishing components of \(R_{\mu\nu}\) (the
vanishing of a tensor is an invariant statement) plus the four quantities
\[
  C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}C^{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa},
  \qquad
  ({}^\star C)_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}C^{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa},
\]
\[
  C_{\lambda\mu}{}^{\nu\kappa}
  C_{\nu\kappa}{}^{\rho\sigma}
  C_{\rho\sigma}{}^{\lambda\mu},
  \qquad
  ({}^\star C)_{\lambda\mu}{}^{\nu\kappa}
  C_{\nu\kappa}{}^{\rho\sigma}
  C_{\rho\sigma}{}^{\lambda\mu},
\]
where the dual is formed with the actual Levi-Civita tensor,
\[
  ({}^\star C)_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
  \equiv
  \frac12\varepsilon_{\lambda\mu}{}^{\rho\sigma}
  C_{\rho\sigma\nu\kappa}.
\]
The two contractions containing a dual are orientation-odd pseudoscalars:
they change sign when the chosen orientation is reversed. Petrov
\hyperref[ch6-ref-2]{\textsuperscript{2}} has given an equivalent description
of the four nonvanishing curvature invariants as roots of a secular equation,
and has classified various algebraic types of Weyl tensor according to the
degeneracies of these roots.

Finally it should be emphasized that Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.7} gives the number of
\emph{algebraically} independent curvature invariants. There are in general
differential relations among these invariants, and the number of functionally
independent curvature invariants is less than Eq.~\eqref{eq:6.7.7}.
