\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\(p\)-Forms}{p-Forms} and Exterior
Derivatives\optionalreading}\label{sec:4.11}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and
may be omitted in a first reading.
\end{quote}

Antisymmetric tensors and their antisymmetrized derivatives possess certain
remarkably simple and useful properties, some of which we have already
encountered in Section~\ref{sec:4.7}. In order to deal with these properties
in a unified way, mathematicians have developed a general formalism known as
the theory of differential forms.~\hyperref[ch4-ref-7]{[7]} Unfortunately,
the rather abstract and compact notation associated with this formalism has
in recent years seriously impeded communication between pure mathematicians
and physicists. This section presents some of the fundamental results of the
theory of differential forms, using modern exterior-calculus notation while
retaining the tensor components familiar to physicists.

A covariant tensor of rank \(p\), antisymmetric under exchange of any pair of
indices, will be called a \(p\)-form. In \(n\) dimensions, the number of
algebraically independent components of a \(p\)-form is just the binomial
coefficient
\begin{equation}
  \binom{n}{p}
  \equiv
  \frac{n!}{p!(n-p)!}.
  \tag{4.11.1}\label{eq:4.11.1}
\end{equation}
For instance, a scalar field is a \(0\)-form, a covariant vector field is a
\(1\)-form, and an antisymmetric covariant tensor with two indices is a
\(2\)-form. We use the standard component normalization
\[
  t
  =
  \frac{1}{p!}\,
  t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}\,
  \dd x^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^{\mu_p}.
\]

Linear combinations of \(p\)-forms are \(p\)-forms. However, the direct
product of a \(p\)-form \(s\) and a \(q\)-form \(t\) is not a
\((p+q)\)-form, because it is not completely antisymmetric. We can form a
\((p+q)\)-form \(s\wedge t\), called the \textit{exterior product}, by
antisymmetrizing the direct product:
\begin{equation}
  (s\wedge t)_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_{p+q}}
  \equiv
  \frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!}\,
  s_{[\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}
  t_{\mu_{p+1}\ldots\mu_{p+q}]}.
  \tag{4.11.2}\label{eq:4.11.2}
\end{equation}
Here and below brackets denote the normalized average over all permutations:
\begin{equation}
  T_{[\mu_1\ldots\mu_m]}
  \equiv
  \frac{1}{m!}
  \sum_{\pi\in S_m}
  \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,
  T_{\mu_{\pi(1)}\ldots\mu_{\pi(m)}} ,
  \tag{4.11.3}\label{eq:4.11.3}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)
  \equiv
  \begin{cases}
    +1,&\pi\text{ even},\\
    -1,&\pi\text{ odd}.
  \end{cases}
  \tag{4.11.4}\label{eq:4.11.4}
\end{equation}
For instance, the exterior product of a \(0\)-form \(s\) and a \(1\)-form
\(t\) is simply the ordinary product,
\[
  (s\wedge t)_\mu=s\,t_\mu,
\]
whereas the exterior product of two \(1\)-forms is the \(2\)-form
\[
  (s\wedge t)_{\mu\nu}
  =
  s_\mu t_\nu-s_\nu t_\mu
  =
  2s_{[\mu}t_{\nu]}.
\]

The reader can easily verify that the exterior product is associative,
\begin{equation}
  (s\wedge t)\wedge u=s\wedge(t\wedge u),
  \tag{4.11.5}\label{eq:4.11.5}
\end{equation}
and bilinear,
\begin{align}
  (\alpha_1s_1+\alpha_2s_2)\wedge t
  &=
  \alpha_1(s_1\wedge t)+\alpha_2(s_2\wedge t),
  \notag\\
  s\wedge(\alpha_1t_1+\alpha_2t_2)
  &=
  \alpha_1(s\wedge t_1)+\alpha_2(s\wedge t_2),
  \tag{4.11.6}\label{eq:4.11.6}
\end{align}
where \(\alpha_1\) and \(\alpha_2\) are scalars. However, the exterior
product is not commutative. Rather, if \(s\) is a \(p\)-form and \(t\) is a
\(q\)-form, then
\begin{equation}
  s\wedge t=(-1)^{pq}t\wedge s.
  \tag{4.11.7}\label{eq:4.11.7}
\end{equation}

This is a good spot to pause and mention that in the mathematical theory of
differential forms, a \(p\)-form \(t\) is generally represented not by the
tensor components \(t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}\) alone, but by the differential
form displayed after Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.1}. The symbols
\(\dd x^\mu\) are the coordinate coframe one-forms. Their exterior products
are associative and anticommutative:
\[
  (\dd x^\mu\wedge\dd x^\nu)\wedge\dd x^\lambda
  =
  \dd x^\mu\wedge(\dd x^\nu\wedge\dd x^\lambda),
  \qquad
  \dd x^\mu\wedge\dd x^\nu
  =
  -\dd x^\nu\wedge\dd x^\mu.
\]
The tensor components of the product are given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.2}. The associativity rule \eqref{eq:4.11.5} and the
graded-commutativity rule \eqref{eq:4.11.7} then follow directly.
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: The source describes dx^mu as contravariant and then
% declines to use differential-form notation. Here dx^mu is correctly treated
% as a coordinate coframe one-form, and standard form notation is used.

The point of developing the theory of \(p\)-forms separately from the rest
of tensor analysis emerges when we study their derivatives. Given any
\(p\)-form \(t\), we define the \((p+1)\)-form \(\dd t\), known as its
\textit{exterior derivative}, by
\begin{equation}
  \dd t
  \equiv
  \dd x^\mu\wedge\partial_\mu t,
  \tag{4.11.8}\label{eq:4.11.8}
\end{equation}
or, in components,
\begin{equation}
  (\dd t)_{\mu_0\ldots\mu_p}
  \equiv
  (p+1)\partial_{[\mu_0}t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p]}.
  \tag{4.11.9}\label{eq:4.11.9}
\end{equation}
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: Weinberg's D uses normalized antisymmetrization, so
% D acting on a p-form equals d/(p+1) in standard exterior-calculus
% normalization. Equations 4.11.2 and 4.11.8--4.11.19 are converted
% consistently to the standard wedge product and exterior derivative.
For instance, the exterior derivative of a \(0\)-form \(t\) is simply the
ordinary gradient,
\[
  (\dd t)_\mu=\partial_\mu t,
\]
whereas the exterior derivative of a \(1\)-form is just the curl,
\[
  (\dd t)_{\mu\nu}
  =
  2\partial_{[\mu}t_{\nu]}
  =
  \partial_\mu t_\nu-\partial_\nu t_\mu.
\]
In three dimensions, the exterior derivative of a \(2\)-form \(t_{ij}\) may
be expressed as an ordinary divergence:
\[
  (\dd t)_{ijk}
  =
  \varepsilon_{ijk}
  \left(
    \partial_1t_{23}
    +\partial_2t_{31}
    +\partial_3t_{12}
  \right).
\]

The first remarkable property of the exterior derivative is that, acting on
a tensor \(p\)-form, it gives a tensor \((p+1)\)-form. The easiest way to see
this is to note that the partial derivatives used in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.9} can be replaced with covariant derivatives:
\begin{equation}
  (\dd t)_{\mu_0\ldots\mu_p}
  =
  (p+1)\nabla_{[\mu_0}t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p]}.
  \tag{4.11.10}\label{eq:4.11.10}
\end{equation}
The contributions from the affine connections vanish upon
antisymmetrization. Our previously derived results
\eqref{eq:4.7.1}, \eqref{eq:4.7.2}, and \eqref{eq:4.7.11} are special
cases of Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.10} for \(p=0\), \(p=1\), and \(p=2\),
respectively.

The rules \eqref{eq:4.11.5} and \eqref{eq:4.11.7} give a simple formula for
the exterior derivative of the exterior product of a \(p\)-form \(s\) and a
\(q\)-form \(t\):
\begin{align}
  \dd(s\wedge t)
  &=
  \dd s\wedge t+(-1)^{pq}\dd t\wedge s
  \notag\\
  &=
  \dd s\wedge t+(-1)^p s\wedge\dd t.
  \tag{4.11.11}\label{eq:4.11.11}
\end{align}
From the same rules it also follows that repeated exterior derivatives
vanish:
\begin{equation}
  \dd^2t
  =
  \dd x^\mu\wedge\dd x^\nu\,
  \partial_\mu\partial_\nu t
  =
  0.
  \tag{4.11.12}\label{eq:4.11.12}
\end{equation}
Among the special cases of this result are two well-known statements of
three-dimensional vector analysis: a gradient has zero curl, and a curl has
zero divergence.

The question naturally arises whether the converse is also valid. That is,
if \(s\) is a \((p+1)\)-form for which
\begin{equation}
  \dd s=0,
  \tag{4.11.13}\label{eq:4.11.13}
\end{equation}
can we express \(s\) as
\begin{equation}
  s=\dd t
  \tag{4.11.14}\label{eq:4.11.14}
\end{equation}
for some \(p\)-form \(t\)? The answer is yes, provided that the region
\(\mathcal R\) over which Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.13} holds, and over which we
want Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.14} to hold, can be deformed to a point. This local
exactness statement is Poincar\'e's lemma.
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: The source calls d^2=0 itself Poincare's lemma.
% Modern terminology reserves the name for the local exactness statement
% represented by Eqs. 4.11.13--4.11.14.

In general, we say that a region \(\mathcal R\) can be deformed to a point
\(y^\mu\) if every point \(x^\mu\) in \(\mathcal R\) can be connected with
\(y^\mu\) by a path \(X^\mu(\lambda;x)\) lying entirely in \(\mathcal R\).
Here \(\lambda\) is a real parameter that can be taken to run from \(0\) to
\(1\), with
\[
  X^\mu(0;x)=y^\mu,
  \qquad
  X^\mu(1;x)=x^\mu.
\]
It is straightforward to verify that if Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.13} holds over
such a region \(\mathcal R\), then Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.14} will be satisfied
throughout this region by the \(p\)-form
\begin{align}
  t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}(x)
  &=
  \int_0^1\dd\lambda\,
  \frac{\partial X^\nu(\lambda;x)}{\partial\lambda}
  \frac{\partial X^{\nu_1}(\lambda;x)}{\partial x^{\mu_1}}
  \cdots
  \frac{\partial X^{\nu_p}(\lambda;x)}{\partial x^{\mu_p}}
  \notag\\[-2pt]
  &\hspace{5.2cm}\times
  s_{\nu\nu_1\ldots\nu_p}\!\left(X(\lambda;x)\right).
  \tag{4.11.15}\label{eq:4.11.15}
\end{align}

The well-known results of three-dimensional vector analysis, that a vector
may be expressed as a gradient if it has zero curl, or as a curl if it has
zero divergence, may be regarded as special cases of this theorem for
\(p=0\) and \(p=1\), respectively. Maxwell's equations provide an example
in four dimensions. The field-strength tensor \(F_{\mu\nu}\) is a
\(2\)-form that, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.10}, has vanishing exterior
derivative, so it can be expressed as the exterior derivative of the
potential \(1\)-form \(A\):
\[
  F=\dd A,
  \qquad
  F_{\mu\nu}
  =
  2\partial_{[\mu}A_{\nu]}
  =
  \partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu,
\]
as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.11}.
% MODERNIZATION CHECK: With Weinberg's normalized D, the source denotes the
% potential 1-form by -2A. The standard d convention makes the modern
% relation F=dA; the component sign agrees with the displayed source formula.

In general, the \(p\)-form \(t\) satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.14} is not
unique. Given one such \(t\), the most general \(p\)-form satisfying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.14} is of the form
\begin{equation}
  t'=t+\dd u,
  \tag{4.11.16}\label{eq:4.11.16}
\end{equation}
where \(u\) is an arbitrary \((p-1)\)-form. For instance, if \(A_\mu\) is
one vector potential whose curl is \(F_{\mu\nu}\), then the most general
such vector potential is given by the gauge transformation
\[
  A'=A+\dd\Phi,
  \qquad
  A'_\mu=A_\mu+\partial_\mu\Phi,
\]
where \(\Phi\) is an arbitrary \(0\)-form, that is, an arbitrary scalar.

Just as the exterior derivative provides a natural generalization of the
familiar gradient, curl, and divergence, it is also possible to construct a
scalar integral of \(p\)-forms over manifolds of dimensionality \(p\). This
provides a natural generalization of the familiar volume integrals of scalar
densities and surface integrals of normal components of vector densities. A
manifold \(\mathcal M\) of dimensionality \(p\) in an \(n\)-dimensional
space is simply a region within which the \(n\) coordinates \(x^\mu\) may be
expressed in a smooth one-to-one way as functions of \(p\) parameters
\(u^i\):
\begin{equation}
  x^\mu=x^\mu(u^1,u^2,\ldots,u^p).
  \tag{4.11.17}\label{eq:4.11.17}
\end{equation}
Actually, it is often impossible to cover the whole of a manifold with a
single set of \(u\)-coordinates. In the general case it is necessary to
introduce different sets of \(u\)-coordinates in overlapping patches of the
manifold, with the proviso that in the overlap between one patch with
coordinates \(u^i\) and another with coordinates \(\bar u^i\), each set can
be expressed in a smooth one-to-one way as functions of the other. We shall
be concerned here with \textit{orientable manifolds}, for which the
coordinates in each patch can be chosen so that in the overlap regions all
Jacobians \(\det(\partial u/\partial\bar u)\) are positive. For instance,
the surface of a sphere is orientable.

For simplicity of notation, the discussion below does not take account of
these complications, but it should be kept in mind that more than one set of
\(u\)-coordinates may be needed to cover the manifold. With this
understanding, the integral of a \(p\)-form \(t\) over a manifold
\(\mathcal M\) of dimensionality \(p\) is defined as the repeated integral
\begin{align}
  \int_{\mathcal M}t
  &\equiv
  \int
  t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}\!\left(x(u)\right)
  \frac{\partial x^{\mu_1}}{\partial u^1}
  \cdots
  \frac{\partial x^{\mu_p}}{\partial u^p}
  \,\dd u^1\cdots\dd u^p,
  \tag{4.11.18}\label{eq:4.11.18}
\end{align}
with limits of integration set by the boundaries of the manifold.

This integral is obviously a scalar with respect to transformations of the
\(x^\mu\) coordinates used to define the \(p\)-form. It is also necessary
to consider how the integral behaves if we decide to describe the manifold
with a new set of parameters \(\bar u^1,\ldots,\bar u^p\) instead of
\(u^1,\ldots,u^p\). Taking account of the antisymmetry of \(t\), it is easy
to see that the pulled-back component changes by the determinant
\(\det(\partial u/\partial\bar u)\), whereas the \(p\)-dimensional volume
element changes by the positive factor
\(\lvert\det(\partial\bar u/\partial u)\rvert\). Thus the whole integral is
either unchanged or changes by a minus sign, according to whether
\(\det(\partial u/\partial\bar u)\) is positive or negative. We tacitly
assume that the transformation \(u\to\bar u\) is nonsingular, so this
determinant cannot vanish and therefore keeps the same sign throughout
\(\mathcal M\).

This result also shows that, when several \(u\)-coordinate systems are
needed to cover the manifold, the integral of a \(p\)-form over the overlap
between two patches described by coordinates \(u^i\) and \(\bar u^i\) can
be evaluated using either coordinate system, provided that
\(\det(\partial u/\partial\bar u)\) is positive. It is for this reason that
we have to restrict our attention to manifolds that are orientable.

The simplest example of Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.18} is the special case in which
\(p\) equals the dimensionality \(n\) of the \(x^\mu\) coordinate space.
Here the \(x^\mu\) coordinates themselves may be used as the
\(u\)-coordinates, so
\[
  \int_{\mathcal M}t
  =
  \int
  t_{0\,1\,\ldots\,n-1}\,
  \dd x^0\dd x^1\cdots\dd x^{n-1}.
\]
Note that the integrand \(t_{0\,1\,\ldots\,n-1}\), in addition to being one
component of a tensor, is also a scalar density of weight \(-1\), because
we can write it as
\[
  t_{0\,1\,\ldots\,n-1}
  =
  \frac{1}{n!}\,
  \tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}
  t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n},
\]
and \(\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}\) is a tensor density of weight
\(-1\);
see Section~\ref{sec:4.4}.

In the next simplest case, \(p=n-1\), we can write
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.11.18} in the familiar form
\[
  \int_{\mathcal M}t
  =
  \int t^\mu\,\dd S_\mu,
\]
where \(t^\mu\) is a vector density defined implicitly by
\[
  t_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}
  \equiv
  \tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p\mu}\,t^\mu,
\]
and \(\dd S_\mu\) is a surface element oriented normal to the manifold:
\[
  \dd S_\mu
  \equiv
  \tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p\mu}
  \frac{\partial x^{\mu_1}}{\partial u^1}
  \cdots
  \frac{\partial x^{\mu_p}}{\partial u^p}
  \,\dd u^1\cdots\dd u^p.
\]

With this general definition of the integrals of \(p\)-forms, it is possible
to prove that the integral of the exterior derivative of a \(p\)-form over
a manifold of dimensionality \(p+1\) is simply the integral of the
\(p\)-form itself over the \(p\)-dimensional boundary of the manifold:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\mathcal M}\dd t
  =
  \int_{\partial\mathcal M}t.
  \tag{4.11.19}\label{eq:4.11.19}
\end{equation}
We shall not go into the problem of defining the orientation of the
boundary, which is needed in order to specify the sign of the right-hand
side. Stokes's theorem and Gauss's theorem are simply the special cases of
this general formula for \(n=3,p=1\) and \(n=3,p=2\), respectively.
