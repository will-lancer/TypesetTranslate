\subsection{The Electromagnetic Analogy\optionalreading}\label{sec:4.10}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and
may be omitted in a first reading.
\end{quote}

I emphasized in Section~\ref{sec:4.1} of this chapter that general covariance
is not an ordinary symmetry principle like Lorentz invariance, but is rather
a dynamical principle that governs the effect of gravitational fields. As
such, it bears a strong resemblance to another ``dynamic symmetry,'' local
gauge invariance, which governs the effects of electromagnetic fields. Local
gauge invariance says that the differential equations satisfied by a set of
charged fields \(\psi(x)\) and the electromagnetic potential \(A_\mu(x)\)
retain the same form when these fields are subjected to the
transformations~\hyperref[ch4-ref-6]{[6]}
\begin{equation}
  \psi(x)
  \longrightarrow
  \psi(x)e^{\ii e\phi(x)}
  \tag{4.10.1}\label{eq:4.10.1}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  A_\mu(x)
  \longrightarrow
  A_\mu(x)+\partial_\mu\phi(x),
  \tag{4.10.2}\label{eq:4.10.2}
\end{equation}
where \(e\) is the charge of the particle represented by \(\psi\), and
\(\phi(x)\) is an arbitrary function of the spacetime coordinates \(x^\mu\).
How are we to construct gauge-invariant equations? Note that derivatives of
a charged field \(\psi\) do not behave under gauge transformations like
\(\psi\), but rather
\begin{align*}
  \partial_\mu\psi(x)
  &\longrightarrow
  \partial_\mu\!\left[\psi(x)e^{\ii e\phi(x)}\right]
  \\
  &=
  e^{\ii e\phi(x)}
  \left[
    \partial_\mu\psi(x)
    +\ii e\psi(x)\partial_\mu\phi(x)
  \right],
\end{align*}
just as derivatives of tensors do not behave like tensors under general
coordinate transformations. It follows that an equation such as
\[
  (\Box-m^2)\psi(x)=0,
  \qquad
  \Box\equiv\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu,
  \qquad
  \eta_{\mu\nu}=\diag(-1,+1,+1,+1),
\]
is not gauge invariant, just as it is not generally covariant. Also note
that the electromagnetic potential \(A_\mu(x)\) obeys an inhomogeneous
gauge-transformation law, just as the affine connection obeys the
inhomogeneous transformation law \eqref{eq:4.5.2} for general coordinate
transformations. In tensor analysis we put together derivatives of tensors
and the affine connection to form ``covariant derivatives'' that transform
like tensors. In electrodynamics we put together derivatives of fields and
the vector potential to form ``gauge-covariant derivatives''
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)
  \equiv
  \left[\partial_\mu-\ii eA_\mu(x)\right]\psi(x),
  \tag{4.10.3}\label{eq:4.10.3}
\end{equation}
that transform like the fields themselves,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)
  \longrightarrow
  \left[\mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)\right]e^{\ii e\phi(x)}.
  \tag{4.10.4}\label{eq:4.10.4}
\end{equation}

An equation that is invariant under gauge transformations with \(\phi\)
constant (such invariance is simply tantamount to charge conservation) will
be invariant under the general gauge transformations
\eqref{eq:4.10.1}--\eqref{eq:4.10.2}, provided that it is constructed only
out of fields \(\psi(x)\) and their gauge-covariant derivatives
\(\mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)\), just as an equation that is invariant under
Lorentz transformations will be invariant under general coordinate
transformations provided that it is constructed out of tensors and their
covariant derivatives. For instance, we can write a gauge-invariant equation
that might represent the effect of electromagnetism on a charged scalar
field \(\psi(x)\) as
% SOURCE ERRATUM: the scan prints +m^2, but the immediately preceding
% free-field equation and the adopted (-+++) convention require -m^2.
\begin{equation}
  \left[\mathcal D^\mu\mathcal D_\mu-m^2\right]\psi(x)=0,
  \tag{4.10.5}\label{eq:4.10.5}
\end{equation}
or, in more detail,
\[
  \left(
    \Box
    -2\ii eA^\mu\partial_\mu
    -\ii e\,\partial_\mu A^\mu
    -e^2A^\mu A_\mu
    -m^2
  \right)\psi(x)=0.
\]

One important property of such theories is that they admit the construction
of a conserved gauge-invariant current; in this example we can define
\[
  J_\mu(x)
  \equiv
  -\ii e
  \left\{
    \psi^\dagger(x)\mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)
    -\psi(x)\left[\mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)\right]^\dagger
  \right\}.
\]
(A dagger denotes complex conjugation, or in quantum theories the Hermitian
adjoint.) That this is gauge invariant is obvious; to see that it is
conserved, we write
\begin{align*}
  \partial_\mu J^\mu(x)
  =-\ii e\Bigl\{&
    \bigl[\partial_\mu\psi^\dagger(x)\bigr]
    \bigl[\partial^\mu\psi(x)-\ii eA^\mu(x)\psi(x)\bigr]
    \\
    &-
    \bigl[\partial_\mu\psi(x)\bigr]
    \bigl[\partial^\mu\psi^\dagger(x)+\ii eA^\mu(x)\psi^\dagger(x)\bigr]
    \\
    &+
    \psi^\dagger(x)
    \bigl[\mathcal D^\mu+\ii eA^\mu(x)\bigr]
    \mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)
    \\
    &-
    \psi(x)
    \left[
      \bigl(\mathcal D^\mu+\ii eA^\mu(x)\bigr)
      \mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)
    \right]^\dagger
  \Bigr\}
  \\
  % SOURCE ERRATUM: the scan drops the overall -ie prefactor on this line.
  &=
  -\ii e\left\{
    \psi^\dagger(x)\mathcal D^\mu\mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)
    -
    \psi(x)
    \left[\mathcal D^\mu\mathcal D_\mu\psi(x)\right]^\dagger
  \right\}.
\end{align*}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.10.5}, this gives
\[
  \partial_\mu J^\mu(x)=0.
\]

We can thus use this current in the right-hand side of Maxwell's equations
\eqref{eq:2.7.6}, and these equations will then be gauge invariant also. We
see in Chapter~7 that the field equations for gravitation are constructed in
an analogous manner.

The analogy between the gauge invariance of electrodynamics and the general
covariance of general relativity can be extended to a similar dynamic
symmetry, called chirality,~\hyperref[ch4-ref-3]{[3]} that governs the
interactions of pi-mesons. A proper explanation of this point would fill
another book.
