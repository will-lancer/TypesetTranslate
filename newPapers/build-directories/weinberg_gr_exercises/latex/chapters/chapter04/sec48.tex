\subsection{Vector Analysis in Orthogonal Coordinates\optionalreading}
\label{sec:4.8}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and
may be omitted in a first reading.
\end{quote}

The reader may be wondering what the tensor-analysis formalism outlined in
this chapter has to do with the familiar formulas for gradient, curl, and
divergence in the classical curvilinear coordinate systems. These are
three-dimensional coordinate systems characterized by the condition that
\(g_{ij}\) is diagonal; that is,
\begin{equation}
  g_{ij}=h_i^2\delta_{ij},
  \qquad (i,j=1,2,3),
  \tag{4.8.1}\label{eq:4.8.1}
\end{equation}
where \(h_i\) is some function of the coordinates. (The summation convention
is suspended for the duration of this section.) The inverse metric tensor is
then
\begin{equation}
  g^{ij}=h_i^{-2}\delta^{ij}.
  \tag{4.8.2}\label{eq:4.8.2}
\end{equation}
The invariant proper length is now
\begin{equation}
  \dd\ell^2
  \equiv
  \sum_{ij}g_{ij}\,\dd x^i\dd x^j
  =
  h_1^2(\dd x^1)^2+h_2^2(\dd x^2)^2+h_3^2(\dd x^3)^2,
  \tag{4.8.3}\label{eq:4.8.3}
\end{equation}
and the invariant volume element is
\begin{equation}
  \dd V
  \equiv
  \sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,\dd x^1\dd x^2\dd x^3
  =
  h_1h_2h_3\,\dd x^1\dd x^2\dd x^3.
  \tag{4.8.4}\label{eq:4.8.4}
\end{equation}

What are usually called the components of a vector \(\mathbf V\) in
elementary treatments are not the covariant components \(V_i\) or the
contravariant components \(V^i\), but the orthonormal-frame components
\(V_{\hat\imath}\):
\begin{equation}
  V_{\hat\imath}
  \equiv
  h_iV^i
  =
  h_i^{-1}V_i.
  \tag{4.8.5}\label{eq:4.8.5}
\end{equation}
The scalar product of two vectors is then very simple:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf V\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf U
  \equiv
  \sum_{ij}g_{ij}V^iU^j
  =
  V_{\hat1}U_{\hat1}+V_{\hat2}U_{\hat2}+V_{\hat3}U_{\hat3}.
  \tag{4.8.6}\label{eq:4.8.6}
\end{equation}
This, of course, is the motivation for the definition
\eqref{eq:4.8.5}. However, the gradient of a scalar is now a little more
complicated:
\begin{equation}
  (\bm\nabla S)_{\hat\imath}
  \equiv
  h_i^{-1}\partial_iS.
  \tag{4.8.7}\label{eq:4.8.7}
\end{equation}

The curl of a vector \(V\) is likewise defined by taking the orthonormal-frame
components of a vector:
\begin{align}
  (\bm\nabla\times\mathbf V)_{\hat\imath}
  &\equiv
  h_i\sum_{jk}\bigl[\det(g_{mn})\bigr]^{-1/2}
  \varepsilon^{ijk}\nabla_jV_k
  \notag\\
  &=
  \frac{h_i}{h_1h_2h_3}
  \sum_{jk}\varepsilon^{ijk}\partial_j(h_kV_{\hat k}).
  \tag{4.8.8}\label{eq:4.8.8}
\end{align}
(We have used Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.7.2}, since \(\varepsilon^{ijk}\) is
antisymmetric in \(j\) and \(k\).) For instance, the first component of the
curl is
\begin{equation}
  (\bm\nabla\times\mathbf V)_{\hat1}
  =
  \frac{1}{h_2h_3}
  \left[
    \partial_2(h_3V_{\hat3})
    -\partial_3(h_2V_{\hat2})
  \right].
  \tag{4.8.9}\label{eq:4.8.9}
\end{equation}

The divergence of a vector \(V\) is nothing but the covariant divergence
\eqref{eq:4.7.7}:
\begin{align}
  \bm\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf V
  &\equiv
  \sum_i\nabla_iV^i
  \notag\\
  &=
  \frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}
  \left[
    \partial_1(h_2h_3V_{\hat1})
    +\partial_2(h_1h_3V_{\hat2})
    +\partial_3(h_1h_2V_{\hat3})
  \right].
  \tag{4.8.10}\label{eq:4.8.10}
\end{align}
The Laplacian of a scalar \(S\) is the divergence of its gradient:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2S
  \equiv
  \sum_{ij}\nabla_j\!\left(g^{ij}\partial_iS\right).
  \tag{4.8.11}\label{eq:4.8.11}
\end{equation}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:4.8.10} and \eqref{eq:4.8.7}, this is
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2S
  =
  \frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}
  \left[
    \partial_1\!\left(\frac{h_2h_3}{h_1}\partial_1S\right)
    +\partial_2\!\left(\frac{h_1h_3}{h_2}\partial_2S\right)
    +\partial_3\!\left(\frac{h_1h_2}{h_3}\partial_3S\right)
  \right].
  \tag{4.8.12}\label{eq:4.8.12}
\end{equation}
The reader may easily check that the usual formulas for gradient, curl,
divergence, and Laplacian are obtained if the \(h_i\) take the forms
appropriate for spherical or cylindrical coordinates.
