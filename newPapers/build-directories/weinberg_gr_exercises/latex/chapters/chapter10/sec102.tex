\subsection{Plane Waves}\label{sec:10.2}

We now consider the plane-wave solutions of the homogeneous equations
\eqref{eq:10.1.13} and \eqref{eq:10.1.14}, both because they are important
in their own right and, as we shall see, because the retarded wave
\eqref{eq:10.1.11} approaches a plane wave as \(r\to\infty\). The general
solution of \eqref{eq:10.1.13} and \eqref{eq:10.1.14} is a linear
superposition of solutions of the form
\begin{equation}
  h_{\mu\nu}(x)
  =
  e_{\mu\nu}\exp(\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda)
  +e_{\mu\nu}^*\exp(-\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda).
  \tag{10.2.1}\label{eq:10.2.1}
\end{equation}
This satisfies \eqref{eq:10.1.13} if
\begin{equation}
  k_\mu k^\mu=0,
  \tag{10.2.2}\label{eq:10.2.2}
\end{equation}
and satisfies \eqref{eq:10.1.14} if
\begin{equation}
  k^\mu e_{\mu\nu}
  =
  \frac12 k_\nu e^\mu{}_\mu.
  \tag{10.2.3}\label{eq:10.2.3}
\end{equation}
(Of course we are still raising and lowering indices with
\(\eta_{\mu\nu}\), so that \(k^\mu\equiv\eta^{\mu\nu}k_\nu\).) The matrix
\(e_{\mu\nu}\) is obviously symmetric:
\begin{equation}
  e_{\mu\nu}=e_{\nu\mu}.
  \tag{10.2.4}\label{eq:10.2.4}
\end{equation}
It will be called the \emph{polarization tensor}.

A symmetric \(4\times4\) matrix would in general have ten independent
components, and the four relations \eqref{eq:10.2.3} would lower this number
to six, but of these six only two represent physically significant degrees
of freedom. By a change of coordinates
\(x^\mu\to x^\mu+\xi^\mu(x)\) we can transform the metric
\(\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}\) into a new metric
\(\eta_{\mu\nu}+h'_{\mu\nu}\), with \(h'_{\mu\nu}\) given by
\eqref{eq:10.1.8}. Suppose that we choose
\begin{equation}
  \xi^\mu(x)
  =
  \ii\zeta^\mu\exp(\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda)
  -\ii\zeta^{\mu*}\exp(-\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda).
  \tag{10.2.5}\label{eq:10.2.5}
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{eq:10.1.8} gives
\begin{equation}
  h'_{\mu\nu}(x)
  =
  e'_{\mu\nu}\exp(\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda)
  +e_{\mu\nu}'{}^*\exp(-\ii k_\lambda x^\lambda),
  \tag{10.2.6}\label{eq:10.2.6}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  e'_{\mu\nu}
  =
  e_{\mu\nu}+k_\mu\zeta_\nu+k_\nu\zeta_\mu.
  \tag{10.2.7}\label{eq:10.2.7}
\end{equation}
[Note that the wave still satisfies the harmonic coordinate condition
\eqref{eq:10.2.3}.] We conclude that \(e'_{\mu\nu}\) and \(e_{\mu\nu}\)
represent the same physical situation for arbitrary values of the four
parameters \(\zeta_\mu\), so of the six independent \(e_{\mu\nu}\)'s
satisfying \eqref{eq:10.2.3} and \eqref{eq:10.2.4}, only
\(6-4=2\) are physically significant.

For instance, consider a wave traveling in the \(+z\)-direction, with wave
vector
\begin{equation}
  k^\mu=(k,0,0,k),
  \qquad k>0,
  \tag{10.2.8}\label{eq:10.2.8}
\end{equation}
so \(k_\mu=(-k,0,0,k)\). In this case \eqref{eq:10.2.3} gives
\[
  e_{31}+e_{01}=e_{32}+e_{02}=0,
\]
\[
  e_{33}+e_{03}
  =
  -e_{03}-e_{00}
  =
  \frac12(e_{11}+e_{22}+e_{33}-e_{00}).
\]
These four equations allow us to express \(e_{01},e_{02},e_{03}\), and
\(e_{22}\) in terms of the other six \(e_{\mu\nu}\):
\begin{equation}
  e_{01}=-e_{31},
  \qquad
  e_{02}=-e_{32},
  \qquad
  e_{03}=-\frac12(e_{33}+e_{00}),
  \qquad
  e_{22}=-e_{11}.
  \tag{10.2.9}\label{eq:10.2.9}
\end{equation}
When the coordinate system is subjected to the transformation defined by
\eqref{eq:10.1.7} and \eqref{eq:10.2.5}, these six independent components
of \(e_{\mu\nu}\) change according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.7}:
\[
  e'_{11}=e_{11},\qquad e'_{12}=e_{12},
\]
\[
  e'_{13}=e_{13}+k\zeta_1,\qquad
  e'_{23}=e_{23}+k\zeta_2,
\]
\[
  e'_{33}=e_{33}+2k\zeta_3,\qquad
  e'_{00}=e_{00}-2k\zeta_0.
\]
Thus it is only \(e_{11}\) and \(e_{12}\) that have an absolute physical
significance. Indeed, we can arrange that all components of
\(e'_{\mu\nu}\) vanish except for \(e'_{11},e'_{12}\), and
\(e'_{22}=-e'_{11}\), by performing a coordinate transformation with
\[
  \zeta_1=-\frac{e_{13}}{k},\qquad
  \zeta_2=-\frac{e_{23}}{k},\qquad
  \zeta_3=-\frac{e_{33}}{2k},\qquad
  \zeta_0=\frac{e_{00}}{2k}.
\]

The distinction between the different components of the polarization tensor
is clarified if we ask how \(e_{\mu\nu}\) changes when we subject the
coordinate system to a rotation about the \(z\)-axis. This is just a Lorentz
transformation of the form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  R^1{}_1=\cos\theta,\qquad R^2{}_1=\sin\theta,\\
  R^1{}_2=-\sin\theta,\qquad R^2{}_2=\cos\theta,\\
  R^0{}_0=R^3{}_3=1,\qquad\text{all other }R^\nu{}_\mu=0.
  \end{gathered}
  \tag{10.2.10}\label{eq:10.2.10}
\end{equation}
Since it leaves \(k_\mu\) invariant (i.e.,
\(R^\nu{}_\mu k_\nu=k_\mu\)), the only effect is to transform
\(e_{\mu\nu}\) into
\begin{equation}
  e'_{\mu\nu}
  =
  R^\rho{}_\mu R^\sigma{}_\nu e_{\rho\sigma}.
  \tag{10.2.11}\label{eq:10.2.11}
\end{equation}
Using the relations \eqref{eq:10.2.9}, we find that
\begin{equation}
  e'_\pm=\exp(\pm2\ii\theta)e_\pm,
  \tag{10.2.12}\label{eq:10.2.12}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  f'_\pm=\exp(\pm\ii\theta)f_\pm,
  \tag{10.2.13}\label{eq:10.2.13}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  e'_{33}=e_{33},
  \qquad
  e'_{00}=e_{00},
  \tag{10.2.14}\label{eq:10.2.14}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  e_\pm
  \equiv e_{11}\mp\ii e_{12}
  =-e_{22}\mp\ii e_{12},
  \tag{10.2.15}\label{eq:10.2.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  f_\pm
  \equiv e_{31}\mp\ii e_{32}
  =-e_{01}\pm\ii e_{02}.
  \tag{10.2.16}\label{eq:10.2.16}
\end{equation}
In general, any plane wave \(\psi\), which is transformed by a rotation of
any angle \(\theta\) about the direction of propagation into
\begin{equation}
  \psi'=\exp(\ii h\theta)\psi,
  \tag{10.2.17}\label{eq:10.2.17}
\end{equation}
is said to have \emph{helicity} \(h\). We thus have shown that a
gravitational plane wave can be decomposed into parts \(e_\pm\) with helicity
\(\pm2\), parts \(f_\pm\) with helicity \(\pm1\), and parts \(e_{00}\) and
\(e_{33}\) with helicity zero. However, we have also seen that the parts with
helicity \(0\) and \(\pm1\) can be made to vanish by a suitable choice of
coordinates, so the physically significant components are just those with
helicity \(\pm2\).

Once again we find a fruitful analogy with electromagnetism. The Maxwell
equations in Lorenz gauge are Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.7.12} and
\eqref{eq:2.7.13}; in empty space they become
\[
  \Box A_\mu=0,
  \qquad
  \partial_\mu A^\mu=0,
\]
in analogy with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.1.13} and \eqref{eq:10.1.14} for the
metric in harmonic coordinates. (We are now in an inertial coordinate
system, and so \(\Box=\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\).) We can find
a plane-wave solution of the form
\[
  A_\mu
  =
  e_\mu\exp(\ii k_\nu x^\nu)
  +e_\mu^*\exp(-\ii k_\nu x^\nu),
\]
where
\[
  k_\mu k^\mu=0,
  \qquad
  k_\mu e^\mu=0,
\]
in analogy with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.2.1}--\eqref{eq:10.2.3}.

In general \(e^\mu\) would have four independent components, but the
condition that \(k_\mu e^\mu\) vanish reduces the number of independent
components to three, just as \eqref{eq:10.2.3} reduces the number of
independent components of \(e_{\mu\nu}\) from ten to six. Furthermore,
without changing the physical fields \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) and
without leaving the Lorenz gauge, we can change \(A_\mu\) by a gauge
transformation
\[
  A_\mu\longrightarrow A'_\mu=A_\mu+\partial_\mu\Phi,
\]
\[
  \Phi(x)
  =
  \ii\zeta\exp(\ii k_\nu x^\nu)
  -\ii\zeta^*\exp(-\ii k_\nu x^\nu),
\]
in analogy with \eqref{eq:10.1.8} and \eqref{eq:10.2.5}. The new potential
can be written
\[
  A'_\mu
  =
  e'_\mu\exp(\ii k_\nu x^\nu)
  +e_\mu'{}^*\exp(-\ii k_\nu x^\nu),
  \qquad
  e'_\mu=e_\mu-\zeta k_\mu,
\]
in analogy with \eqref{eq:10.2.6} and \eqref{eq:10.2.7}. The parameter
\(\zeta\) is arbitrary, so of the three algebraically independent components
of \(e_\mu\) only \(3-1=2\) are physically significant, just as general
covariance renders only two of the six independent components of
\(e_{\mu\nu}\) physically significant.

To identify the two significant components of \(e_\mu\), we may consider a
wave traveling in the \(z\)-direction, with \(k^\mu\) given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.2.8}. Then the condition that \(k_\mu e^\mu\) vanish allows
us to determine \(e_0=-e_3\), just as \eqref{eq:10.2.3} allows us to
determine \(e_{22}\) and \(e_{0i}\) in terms of the other six
\(e_{\mu\nu}\). Also, the preceding gauge transformation leaves \(e_1\) and
\(e_2\) invariant but changes \(e_3\) into
\[
  e'_3=e_3-\zeta k.
\]
Hence \(e'_3\) can be made equal to zero by choosing \(\zeta=e_3/k\), so it
is only \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) that carry physical significance, just as it was
only \(e_{11}\) and \(e_{12}\) that could not be made equal to zero by a
suitable coordinate transformation.

Finally, we can work out the meaning of these two components by subjecting
the plane electromagnetic wave to the rotation \eqref{eq:10.2.10}. The
polarization vector is then changed into
\[
  e'_\mu=R^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu,
\]
and therefore
\[
  e'_\pm=\exp(\pm\ii\theta)e_\pm,
  \qquad
  e'_3=e_3,
  \qquad
  e_\pm\equiv e_1\mp\ii e_2.
\]
Thus the electromagnetic wave can be decomposed into parts with helicity
\(\pm1\) and \(0\). However, the physically significant helicities are
\(\pm1\), not \(0\), just as for gravitational waves they are \(\pm2\), not
\(\pm1\) or \(0\). This is what we mean when we say, speaking classically,
that electromagnetism and gravitation are carried by waves of spin 1 and spin
2, respectively.
