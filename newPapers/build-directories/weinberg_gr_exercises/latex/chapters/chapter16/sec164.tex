\subsection{Models with a Varying Constant of Gravitation}\label{sec:16.4}

Gravitational forces are remarkably weak by the standards of atomic or nuclear
physics. For instance, the ratio of the gravitational to the electric force
between the electron and the proton has the value
\begin{equation}
  \frac{Gm_pm_e}{e^2}
  =
  4.4\times10^{-40}.
  \tag{16.4.1}\label{eq:16.4.1}
\end{equation}
Despite many attempts,\hyperref[ch16-ref-22]{[22]} there have been no
convincing explanations of why such a tiny dimensionless number should appear
in the fundamental laws of physics. However, there is one clue, which suggests
that numbers like \eqref{eq:16.4.1} are not determined solely by considerations
of microphysics, but in part by the influence of the whole universe. This clue
is simply the fact that, from the quantities \(G,\hbar,c\), and the Hubble
constant \(H_0\), it is possible to construct a mass, which is not too different
from the mass of a typical elementary particle, such as the pion:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\hbar^2H_0}{Gc}\right)^{1/3}
  \simeq m_\pi.
  \tag{16.4.2}\label{eq:16.4.2}
\end{equation}
(For \(H_0^{-1}=10^{10}\) years, the left-hand side has the value
\(60\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\), while the pion mass is
\(140\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\). If \(e^2/c\) were used in place of \(\hbar\),
the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:16.4.2} would become of the same order of
magnitude as the electron mass.) Of course, one is perfectly free to regard
\eqref{eq:16.4.2} as a meaningless numerical coincidence, but it should be
noted that the particular combination of \(\hbar,H_0,G\), and \(c\) appearing
in \eqref{eq:16.4.2} is very much closer to a typical elementary-particle mass
than other random combinations of these quantities; for instance, from
\(\hbar,G\), and \(c\) alone one can form a single quantity
\((\hbar c/G)^{1/2}\) with the dimensions of a mass, but this has the value
\(1.22\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\), more than a typical particle mass by
about 20 orders of magnitude!

In considering the possible interpretations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.2}, one
should be careful to distinguish it from other numerical ``coincidences'' such
as the rough relation among \(G,H_0,m_p\), and the present cosmic baryon number
density \(n_0\):
\begin{equation}
  Gn_0m_p\simeq H_0^2.
  \tag{16.4.3}\label{eq:16.4.3}
\end{equation}
This is a relation between \emph{two} cosmological parameters, \(n_0\) and
\(H_0\), and is in fact required by various cosmological models, such as the
Friedmann models (unless \(q_0\ll1\) or \(q_0\gg1\)) and Hoyle's version of the
steady state model. [See Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.2.6} and \eqref{eq:16.3.8}.]
In contrast, Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.2} relates a single cosmological parameter,
\(H_0\), to the fundamental constants \(\hbar,G,c\), and \(m_\pi\), and is so
far unexplained.

There are other numerical coincidences that have been noticed from time to
time, but most of these are combinations of \eqref{eq:16.4.2} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.3}, sometimes with \(e^2/c=137\hbar\) in place of \(\hbar\)
and with other masses in place of \(m_\pi\). For instance, it has often been
remarked that the ratio of the atomic time unit \(e^2/m_ec^3\) to the Hubble
time \(H_0^{-1}\) is of the same order of magnitude, about \(10^{-40}\), as
the ratio \eqref{eq:16.4.1} of gravitational and electric forces in atoms,
but this is equivalent to \eqref{eq:16.4.2}, with \(e^2/c\) in place of
\(\hbar\) and \(m_e^{2/3}m_p^{1/3}\) in place of \(m_\pi\).

If we choose to regard the numerical relation \eqref{eq:16.4.2} as having a
real though mysterious significance, then we must face the problem that in most
cosmologies \(H_0\) is not a constant, but a function of the age of the
universe. One way of dealing with this problem is to replace \(H_0\) with a
quantity of comparable magnitude that \emph{is} a constant; for instance, in a
closed Friedmann model we can use the reciprocal of the time it takes for the
universe to expand to its maximum extent, while in a steady state model, the
Hubble constant itself will do. The only trouble with this approach is that it
does not lead anywhere, and in particular, it leaves us with fundamental
dimensionless constants, such as \eqref{eq:16.4.1} or \(Gm^2/\hbar c\), which
are inexplicably tiny.

In 1937 a very different approach was suggested by
Dirac.\hyperref[ch16-ref-23]{[23]} He proposed that relations like
\eqref{eq:16.4.2} are fundamental though as yet unexplained truths, which
remain valid, with a constant factor of proportionality, even though the Hubble
``constant'' \(\dot a/a\) varies with the age of the universe. It follows then
that one or more of the ``constants'' \(\hbar,G,c\), and \(m_\pi\) must vary
over cosmic time scales.

In order to avoid having to reformulate the whole of atomic and nuclear physics,
Dirac chose \(G\) as the ``constant'' that varies with time, and in order to
preserve \eqref{eq:16.4.2}, he proposed that
\begin{equation}
  G\propto\frac{\dot a}{a}.
  \tag{16.4.4}\label{eq:16.4.4}
\end{equation}
In addition, Dirac suggested that relations like \eqref{eq:16.4.3} also remain
true, with a constant factor of proportionality, as the universe expands. Since
\(n\propto a^{-3}\), it follows that
\begin{equation}
  Ga^{-3}\propto\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}.
  \tag{16.4.5}\label{eq:16.4.5}
\end{equation}
Eliminating \(G(t)\) from \eqref{eq:16.4.4} and \eqref{eq:16.4.5} then
yields a differential equation for \(a(t)\):
\[
  \dot a\propto a^{-2},
\]
with solution
\begin{equation}
  a\propto t^{1/3}.
  \tag{16.4.6}\label{eq:16.4.6}
\end{equation}
Either \eqref{eq:16.4.4} or \eqref{eq:16.4.5} then gives the gravitational
constant a time dependence
\begin{equation}
  G\propto t^{-1}.
  \tag{16.4.7}\label{eq:16.4.7}
\end{equation}
Thus in Dirac's cosmology, there is no fundamental significance to very small
dimensionless numbers like \(10^{-40}\); the reason that
\eqref{eq:16.4.1} is this small is simply that the universe is old.

For \(k=\pm1\), there are still significant cosmological parameters that are
constant and grossly different from unity, such as the number \(na^3\) of
particles within a sphere whose radius is of the order of the radius of
curvature of space. To avoid this, Dirac also proposed that space is flat, with
\(k=0\), so that the absolute value of the Robertson--Walker scale factor
\(a(t)\) and pure numbers like \(na^3\) should be of no physical significance.

If the gravitational constant varies, then general relativity needs to be
replaced with some other field theory of gravitation. Dirac did not specify what
this field theory would be like, so his cosmological model remained incomplete.
Nevertheless, it made a number of definite predictions. First,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.6} gives a relation between the present Hubble constant
\(H_0\) and the present age of the universe \(t_0\):
\begin{equation}
  t_0=\frac13H_0^{-1}.
  \tag{16.4.8}\label{eq:16.4.8}
\end{equation}
Even for \(H_0^{-1}\) as large as \(13\times10^9\) years, this gives an age
of only \(4.3\times10^9\) years, less than the age of the earth and moon
determined by radioactive dating (which does not involve assumptions about
\(G\)). Thus the Dirac theory appears already to conflict with observation.
Equation~\eqref{eq:16.4.6} gives a deceleration parameter \(q_0=2\), which
cannot be ruled out with present data. (See Section~\ref{sec:14.6}.) Finally,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.7} gives a present rate of decrease of the ``constant'' of
gravitation
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\dot G}{G}\right)_0
  =
  -t_0^{-1}
  =
  -3H_0.
  \tag{16.4.9}\label{eq:16.4.9}
\end{equation}
The observable implications of a decreasing gravitational constant are
discussed at the end of this section.

Dirac's theory inspired a number of attempts to formulate a field theory of
gravitation in which the effective ``constant'' of gravitation is some function
of a scalar field. Jordan\hyperref[ch16-ref-24]{[24]} proposed one theory,
which involved a nonconserved energy-momentum tensor, and was severely
criticized on these and other grounds by Fierz\hyperref[ch16-ref-25]{[25]} and
Bondi.\hyperref[ch16-ref-26]{[26]} A subsequent
reformulation\hyperref[ch16-ref-27]{[27]} removed most of these objections, but
Jordan's theory still did not successfully incorporate nonrelativistic matter.
The most interesting and complete scalar-tensor theory of gravitation is that
proposed by Brans and Dicke\hyperref[ch16-ref-28]{[28]} in 1961, which we have
already discussed in some detail in Sections~\ref{sec:7.3} and \ref{sec:9.9}.
In this theory, the gravitational constant \(G\) is replaced with the reciprocal
of a scalar field \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\). In order to incorporate relations such
as \eqref{eq:16.4.3} into the theory, \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) is assumed to obey
a field equation
\begin{equation}
  \Box\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  \equiv
  \nabla_\mu\nabla^\mu\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  =
  \frac{8\pi}{3+2\omega}T^\mu{}_\mu,
  \tag{16.4.10}\label{eq:16.4.10}
\end{equation}
where \(T^{\mu\nu}\) is the energy-momentum tensor of matter (not including
\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\)) and \(\omega\) is a dimensionless coupling parameter.
In order not to interfere with the successes of the Principle of Equivalence,
\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) is assumed not to enter into the equations of motion of
ordinary matter and radiation, so that \(T^{\mu\nu}\) obeys the familiar
conservation law
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\nu T^{\mu\nu}=0.
  \tag{16.4.11}\label{eq:16.4.11}
\end{equation}
The Bianchi identities then require the gravitational field equations to take
the form \eqref{eq:7.3.14}, or equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  % NOTATION EXCEPTION: \lambda is a contracted dummy index, not the cosmological constant.
  \begin{split}
  R_{\mu\nu}
  ={}&
  \frac{8\pi}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
  \left[
    T_{\mu\nu}
    -\left(\frac{1+\omega}{3+2\omega}\right)
     g_{\mu\nu}T^\lambda{}_\lambda
  \right]\\
  &+
  \frac{\omega}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}^2}
  (\nabla_\mu\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}})
  (\nabla_\nu\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}})
  +
  \frac1{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
  \nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{16.4.12}\label{eq:16.4.12}
\end{equation}
This theory becomes equivalent to that of
Jordan\hyperref[ch16-ref-27]{[27]} in the special case of an energy-momentum
tensor with vanishing trace.

In applying the Brans--Dicke theory to cosmology, we again consider the
universe to be smeared out into a homogeneous isotropic continuum, as in
Chapters 14 and 15. The metric then has the Robertson--Walker form
\eqref{eq:14.2.1}; the energy-momentum tensor has the perfect-fluid form
\eqref{eq:14.2.12}, and the scalar field \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) is a function
of time alone. A straightforward calculation using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.1.6},
\eqref{eq:15.1.7}, \eqref{eq:15.1.11}, and \eqref{eq:15.1.13} gives the
time-time component of Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.12} as
\begin{equation}
  3\frac{\ddot a}{a}
  =
  -\frac{8\pi}{(3+2\omega)\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
   \left[(2+\omega)\rho+3(1+\omega)p\right]
  -\frac{\omega\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}^2}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}^2}
  -\frac{\ddot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}},
  \tag{16.4.13}\label{eq:16.4.13}
\end{equation}
while the space-space components of Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.12} give
\begin{equation}
  -\frac{\ddot a}{a}
  -2\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}
  -\frac{2k}{a^2}
  =
  -\frac{8\pi}{(3+2\omega)\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
   \left[(1+\omega)\rho-\omega p\right]
  +\frac{\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\dot a}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}a},
  \tag{16.4.14}\label{eq:16.4.14}
\end{equation}
and the time-space components simply say that zero equals zero. The field
equation \eqref{eq:16.4.10} for \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) here reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \left(\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}a^3\right)
  =
  \frac{8\pi}{3+2\omega}(\rho-3p)a^3,
  \tag{16.4.15}\label{eq:16.4.15}
\end{equation}
and the conservation laws \eqref{eq:16.4.11} give, as in Chapter 14,
\begin{equation}
  \dot\rho=-3\frac{\dot a}{a}(\rho+p).
  \tag{16.4.16}\label{eq:16.4.16}
\end{equation}
By eliminating \(\ddot a\) from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.13} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.14}, and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.15} to eliminate
\(\ddot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\), we can derive a first-order equation analogous to
\eqref{eq:15.1.20}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}
  +\frac{k}{a^2}
  =
  \frac{8\pi\rho}{3\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
  -\frac{\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\dot a}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}a}
  +\frac{\omega\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}^2}
        {6\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}^2}.
  \tag{16.4.17}\label{eq:16.4.17}
\end{equation}
We can recover \eqref{eq:16.4.13} and \eqref{eq:16.4.14} from the derivative
of \eqref{eq:16.4.17}, so the fundamental equations of the Brans--Dicke
cosmology may be taken as Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.15}--\eqref{eq:16.4.17}, plus
an equation of state giving \(p\) as a function of \(\rho\). In addition,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.9.11} shows that the gravitational ``constant,'' measured by
the observation of slowly moving particles or in time-dilation experiments, is
\begin{equation}
  G
  =
  \left(\frac{2\omega+4}{2\omega+3}\right)
  \Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}^{-1}.
  \tag{16.4.18}\label{eq:16.4.18}
\end{equation}

For any given equation of state \(p=p(\rho)\),
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.15}--\eqref{eq:16.4.17} may be regarded as a
second-order differential equation plus two first-order differential equations
for the three variables \(a,\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\), and \(\rho\). It follows that
these equations uniquely determine \(a(t),\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}(t)\), and
\(\rho(t)\) for all \(t\), provided we are given the present value of
\emph{four} variables, say \(a_0,\dot a_0,\Phi_{\mathrm{BD},0}\), and
\(\rho_0\), as well as the constants \(\omega\) and \(k\). This is rather
surprising, because in a Friedmann model we only need to be given the initial
values of \emph{three} quantities, say \(a_0,\dot a_0\), and \(\rho_0\), and
of course \(G\), in order to be able to calculate \(a(t)\) and \(\rho(t)\) for
all \(t\). [See Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.2.1}.]

Originally, Brans and Dicke\hyperref[ch16-ref-28]{[28]} eliminated this extra
degree of freedom by imposing one additional constraint, that
\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}a^3\) vanishes at the initial singularity where \(a=0\):
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}a^3\longrightarrow0
  \qquad\text{for}\qquad
  a\longrightarrow0.
  \tag{16.4.19}\label{eq:16.4.19}
\end{equation}
With this initial condition, and with a given \(\omega,k\), and equation of
state, we can obtain a complete solution for \(a(t),\rho(t)\), and
\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}(t)\) by specifying only three present parameters, such as
\(a_0,\dot a_0\), and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD},0}\), or, more conveniently,
\(H_0,G_0\), and \(q_0\) (or \(\rho_0\)).

A few years later, Dicke\hyperref[ch16-ref-29]{[29]} suggested that the
relevant solutions may in fact be those that do \emph{not} satisfy the
constraint \eqref{eq:16.4.19}. In general, all solutions have a singularity
with \(a=0\) at a finite time, which as usual we define to be \(t=0\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:16.4.15} then has the solution:
\begin{equation}
  \dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}(t)a^3(t)
  =
  \frac{8\pi}{2\omega+3}
  \int_0^t
    [\rho(t')-3p(t')]a^3(t')\,\dd t'
  -C,
  \tag{16.4.20}\label{eq:16.4.20}
\end{equation}
where \(C\) is an integration constant, which may be positive, negative, or
zero. For \(C=0\), we obtain the three-parameter family of models satisfying
the initial condition \eqref{eq:16.4.19}. For \(C\ne0\), we obtain a
four-parameter family of solutions, the extra parameter being needed to fix
the value of \(C\).

The properties of these various solutions are sufficiently subtle to make it
worth our while to study in some detail the one case where
\eqref{eq:16.4.15}--\eqref{eq:16.4.17} can be solved analytically, the case
of zero pressure and zero curvature:
\[
  p=0,
  \qquad
  k=0.
\]
Here \eqref{eq:16.4.16} gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho\propto a^{-3},
  \tag{16.4.21}\label{eq:16.4.21}
\end{equation}
so Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.20} gives immediately
\begin{equation}
  \dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  =
  \frac{8\pi\rho}{2\omega+3}(t-t_c),
  \tag{16.4.22}\label{eq:16.4.22}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  t_c
  \equiv
  \frac{(2\omega+3)C}{8\pi\rho a^3}.
  \tag{16.4.23}\label{eq:16.4.23}
\end{equation}
It proves extremely convenient to introduce a new dependent variable
\begin{equation}
  u
  \equiv
  (t-t_c)\frac{\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
  =
  \frac{8\pi\rho(t-t_c)^2}
       {(2\omega+3)\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
  >0.
  \tag{16.4.24}\label{eq:16.4.24}
\end{equation}
By expressing \(\rho\) and
\(\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}/\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.17} in terms of \(u\), and setting \(k=0\), we can
immediately solve for \(\dot a/a\):
\begin{equation}
  2(t-t_c)\frac{\dot a}{a}
  =
  -u
  \mathbin{\pm}
  \left(\frac{3+2\omega}{3}\right)^{1/2}
  (u^2+4u)^{1/2}.
  \tag{16.4.25}\label{eq:16.4.25}
\end{equation}
Also, Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.21} and the logarithmic derivative of
\eqref{eq:16.4.22} give
\[
  \frac{\dot u}{u}
  =
  -3\frac{\dot a}{a}
  +2(t-t_c)^{-1}
  -\frac{\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}},
\]
or, using \eqref{eq:16.4.24} and \eqref{eq:16.4.25},
\begin{equation}
  (t-t_c)\dot u
  =
  \frac12u
  \left[
    u+4
    \mathbin{\mp}
    3\left(\frac{3+2\omega}{3}\right)^{1/2}
    (u^2+4u)^{1/2}
  \right].
  \tag{16.4.26}\label{eq:16.4.26}
\end{equation}
This first-order equation must be integrated to find \(u(t)\), following which
\eqref{eq:16.4.24} and \eqref{eq:16.4.25} can be integrated to determine
\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}(t)\) and \(a(t)\).

One obvious class of solutions to Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.26} are those with \(u\)
a constant, equal to one of the zeros of the expression on the right-hand side
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.26}. In order to have such a zero with \(u>0\), we must
take the upper sign of the square root in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.26} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.25}, and the solution is
\begin{equation}
  u=\frac{2}{3\omega+4}.
  \tag{16.4.27}\label{eq:16.4.27}
\end{equation}
For this solution, we must take \(t_c=0\), because otherwise
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.25} would give \(a=0\) only at the time \(t=t_c\), and
we have agreed to set our clocks so that this singularity occurs at \(t=0\).
With \(t_c=0\), Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.24} and \eqref{eq:16.4.25} yield the
solutions\hyperref[ch16-ref-28]{[28]}
\begin{align}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  &\propto
  t^{2/(4+3\omega)},
  \tag{16.4.28}\label{eq:16.4.28}\\
  a
  &\propto
  t^{(2\omega+2)/(3\omega+4)},
  \tag{16.4.29}\label{eq:16.4.29}\\
  \frac{4\pi\rho t^2}{\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
  &=
  \frac{2\omega+3}{3\omega+4}.
  \tag{16.4.30}\label{eq:16.4.30}
\end{align}

For \(t_c\ne0\), it is necessary to analyze how \(u\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.26} moves between the singular points \(u=0\),
\(u=2/(3\omega+4)\), and \(u=\infty\). The results depend critically on
whether \(t_c\) is positive or negative.

\emph{\(t_c>0\).} Here \(u\) drops monotonically from \(u=\infty\) at \(t=0\)
to \(u=0\) at \(t=t_c\), and then rises monotonically to the value
\eqref{eq:16.4.27} as \(t\to\infty\). The sign of the square root in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.25} and \eqref{eq:16.4.26} switches at \(t_c\) from the
bottom sign for \(t<t_c\) to the top sign for \(t>t_c\). The solutions of
\eqref{eq:16.4.26} are thus given by
\[
  \ln\left(1-\frac{t}{t_c}\right)
  =
  -2\int_0^u
  \frac{\dd u}{
    u\left\{
      u+4
      +3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}(u^2+4u)^{1/2}
    \right\}
  },
  \qquad t<t_c,
\]
\[
  \ln\left(\frac{t}{t_c}-1\right)
  =
  2\int_0^u
  \frac{\dd u}{
    u\left\{
      u+4
      -3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}(u^2+4u)^{1/2}
    \right\}
  },
  \qquad t>t_c.
\]
These integrals can be done in closed form, but it is more interesting to look
at the behavior of the solutions at very early and very late times. For
\(t\ll t_c\), we find
\[
  u\longrightarrow
  \frac{
    3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}-1
  }{
    (4+3\omega)(t/t_c)
  },
\]
so Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.24} and \eqref{eq:16.4.25} have the
solutions\hyperref[ch16-ref-29]{[29]}
\begin{align}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  &\propto
  t^{\{1-3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}\}/(4+3\omega)},
  \tag{16.4.31}\label{eq:16.4.31}\\
  a
  &\propto
  t^{\{1+\omega+(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}\}/(4+3\omega)}.
  \tag{16.4.32}\label{eq:16.4.32}
\end{align}
For \(t\gg t_c\), \(u\) approaches the value \eqref{eq:16.4.27}, and the
solutions of \eqref{eq:16.4.24} and \eqref{eq:16.4.25} go over to the forms
\eqref{eq:16.4.28} and \eqref{eq:16.4.29}.

\emph{\(t_c<0\).} Here \(u\) drops monotonically from \(u=\infty\) at \(t=0\)
to the value \eqref{eq:16.4.27} as \(t\to\infty\). The square roots in
\eqref{eq:16.4.25} and \eqref{eq:16.4.26} keep the upper sign, so
\eqref{eq:16.4.26} has the solution
\[
  \ln\left(1+\frac{t}{\lvert t_c\rvert}\right)
  =
  2\int_u^\infty
  \frac{\dd u}{
    u\left\{
      3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}(u^2+4u)^{1/2}
      -u-4
    \right\}
  }.
\]
For \(t\ll\lvert t_c\rvert\), we find
\[
  u\longrightarrow
  \frac{
    3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}+1
  }{
    (4+3\omega)(t/\lvert t_c\rvert)
  },
\]
so Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.24} and \eqref{eq:16.4.25} have the
solutions\hyperref[ch16-ref-29]{[29]}
\begin{align}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  &\propto
  t^{\{3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}+1\}/(4+3\omega)},
  \tag{16.4.33}\label{eq:16.4.33}\\
  a
  &\propto
  t^{\{1+\omega-(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}\}/(4+3\omega)}.
  \tag{16.4.34}\label{eq:16.4.34}
\end{align}
For \(t\gg\lvert t_c\rvert\), \(u\) approaches the value
\eqref{eq:16.4.27}, and the solutions of \eqref{eq:16.4.24} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.25} again go over to the forms \eqref{eq:16.4.28} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.29}.

Thus there are three kinds of solution, all of which behave alike for
\(t\gg\lvert t_c\rvert\), but which differ radically for
\(t\ll\lvert t_c\rvert\). Only the simple solution with \(t_c=0\) goes over
smoothly to the zero-curvature Friedmann solution
(\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) constant, \(a\propto t^{2/3}\)) in the limit of large
\(\omega\); the solutions with \(t_c>0\) or \(t_c<0\) have
\(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\to\infty\) or \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\to0\), respectively,
as \(t\to0\) for any finite \(\omega\).

Although these solutions were derived under the assumption of zero pressure
and zero curvature, they exhibit many of the properties of the much more
complicated general solutions. In general, the solutions may be classified
according to whether the integration constant \(C\) in \eqref{eq:16.4.20} is
zero, or positive, or negative. For sufficiently large \(t\), the integral in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.20} is dominated by the matter-dominated era, in which
\(\rho\propto a^{-3}\), so the integral grows like \(t\), and thus the
integration constant eventually becomes negligible. In this limit, we have
\begin{equation}
  \dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  =
  \frac{8\pi\rho t}{2\omega+3},
  \tag{16.4.35}\label{eq:16.4.35}
\end{equation}
and all solutions converge to the \(C=0\) solution, which unfortunately must
be calculated by a numerical solution of \eqref{eq:16.4.17} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.35} with \(\rho\propto a^{-3}\). On the other hand, for \(t\)
sufficiently small, the integration constant will dominate in
\eqref{eq:16.4.20}, provided of course that \(C\ne0\). In this case, the
curvature and density terms in \eqref{eq:16.4.17} become negligible for
\(t\to0\), and the solutions go over to the previously derived forms
\begin{align}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}
  &\propto
  t^{\{1\mp3(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}\}/(4+3\omega)},
  \tag{16.4.36}\label{eq:16.4.36}\\
  a
  &\propto
  t^{\{1+\omega\pm(1+2\omega/3)^{1/2}\}/(4+3\omega)},
  \tag{16.4.37}\label{eq:16.4.37}
\end{align}
with upper sign for \(C>0\) and lower sign for \(C<0\). The \(C=0\) solutions
go over smoothly to the Friedmann-model solutions for large \(\omega\), but
the \(C\ne0\) solutions behave peculiarly at \(t=0\) for any \(\omega\).

The Brans--Dicke theory does not offer a satisfactory solution to the numerical
relations discussed at the beginning of this section. Generally
\(\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}/\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) and \(1/t\) will be of the order
of the Hubble ``constant'' \(H\), and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}\) is of the order of
\(1/G\), so once the integration constant \(C\) becomes negligible,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.35} will become more or less the same as the relation
\eqref{eq:16.4.3}. However, Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.3} is not even approximately
valid at very early times when \(C\) is not negligible. More important, the
mysterious relation \eqref{eq:16.4.2} is not explained at all by the
Brans--Dicke theory. Indeed, in the simplest case of zero pressure, zero
curvature, and zero integration constant \(t_c\), Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.29} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.28} show that \(H\propto1/t\) while
\(G\propto t^{-2/(4+3\omega)}\), so the mass
\((\hbar^2H/Gc)^{1/3}\) decreases with time, and the relation
\eqref{eq:16.4.2} can only be valid for a brief period in the history of the
universe.

Now let us turn to the observational implications of this theory. Neither the
gravitational field nor the Brans--Dicke field have any direct effect on the
nuclear processes that are believed to produce helium in the early universe,
but they do affect the rate of expansion of the universe, which in turn governs
the amount of helium that can be produced. (See Section~\ref{sec:15.7}.) For
solutions with \(C=0\), the numerical
integration\hyperref[ch16-ref-29]{[29]} of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.17} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.20} shows that in the case
\(\omega=5\), \(k=0\), \(H_0^{-1}=9.5\times10^9\) years,
\(\rho_0=2\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), the effect of the Brans--Dicke
field is to shorten the time required for the temperature to drop to
\(10^{9\,\circ}\mathrm K\) by a factor of 0.45, so that more neutrons are left
when nucleosynthesis begins, and the cosmologically produced abundance of
helium is about \(42\%\) by weight, rather than \(27\%\). For \(k=-1\) models
with a smaller present density, the difference between the Friedmann and
Brans--Dicke models is considerably less.\hyperref[ch16-ref-30]{[30]} On the
other hand, with a nonvanishing integration constant in \eqref{eq:16.4.20},
we can make the expansion rate in the early universe essentially anything we
like. As remarked in Section~\ref{sec:15.7}, for a moderate speed-up of the
expansion the helium production is enhanced, but if the expansion is speeded
up too much, then there will not be enough time for the reaction
\[
  n+p\longrightarrow d+\gamma
\]
to produce enough deuterium to initiate nucleosynthesis, and very little
helium will be produced.

During the more recent epoch within the range of optical telescopes, the
integration constant \(C\) has presumably (though not certainly) been
negligible, so that, for large \(\omega\), the relations among curvature,
density, age, Hubble constant, and deceleration parameter are pretty much the
same as for the Friedmann models. For instance, for a zero-pressure
zero-curvature model in the limit \(t\gg\lvert t_c\rvert\),
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.28}--\eqref{eq:16.4.30} give the relations
\begin{align}
  H_0t_0
  &=
  \frac{2+2\omega}{4+3\omega},
  \tag{16.4.38}\label{eq:16.4.38}\\
  q_0
  &=
  \frac{\omega+2}{2\omega+2},
  \tag{16.4.39}\label{eq:16.4.39}\\
  \frac{4\pi G\rho_0}{H_0^2}
  &=
  \frac{(4+3\omega)(4+2\omega)}{(2+2\omega)^2}.
  \tag{16.4.40}\label{eq:16.4.40}
\end{align}
For \(\omega=6\) these three quantities have the values \(0.64,0.57,1.80\),
while the corresponding values for a Friedmann model with \(k=0\) are
\(0.67,0.50\), and \(1.50\).

Certainly the most distinctive observable feature of both the Dirac and the
Brans--Dicke theories is the decrease of the gravitational constant \(G\) with
time. In the Brans--Dicke theory the present rate of change of \(G\) is given
by \eqref{eq:16.4.35} as
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\dot G}{G}\right)_0
  =
  -\left(\frac{\dot\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}
              {\Phi_{\mathrm{BD}}}\right)_0
  =
  -\frac{8\pi\rho_0t_0}
        {(2\omega+3)\Phi_{\mathrm{BD},0}}
  =
  -\frac{8\pi G_0\rho_0t_0}{2\omega+4}.
  \tag{16.4.41}\label{eq:16.4.41}
\end{equation}
In general, in order to express \(\rho_0\) and \(t_0\) in terms of
\(G_0,H_0\), and \(q_0\), it would be necessary to resort to a numerical
solution of the differential equations \eqref{eq:16.4.35} and
\eqref{eq:16.4.17}. However, if \(\omega\) is reasonably large (say
\(\omega\gtrsim5\)), then the rate of decrease of \(G\) may be calculated to
a sufficient degree of accuracy by using for \(\rho_0\) and \(t_0\) in
\eqref{eq:16.4.41} the values calculated using the Einstein equations in
Sections~\ref{sec:15.2} and \ref{sec:15.3}. The general Friedmann-model result
for \(\rho_0\) is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{4\pi G_0\rho_0}{3H_0^2}=q_0.
  \tag{16.4.42}\label{eq:16.4.42}
\end{equation}
Values for \(H_0t_0\) and the resulting values for the rate
\eqref{eq:16.4.41} are given in Table~\ref{tab:16.1}.

In the special case \(k=0\), where we have an analytic solution,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:16.4.18}, \eqref{eq:16.4.28}, and \eqref{eq:16.4.29} yield
the ``exact'' result
\[
  \left(\frac{\dot G}{G}\right)_0
  =
  -\frac{H_0}{1+\omega},
\]
so the estimate of this rate given in Table~\ref{tab:16.1} is in this case
about \(12\%\) too low for \(\omega=6\). The estimates in Table~\ref{tab:16.1}
are in good agreement with the ``exact'' results that have been
computed\hyperref[ch16-ref-30]{[30]} for \(k=-1\) and \(\omega=5\) or
\(\omega=10\).

For \(H_0^{-1}=10^{10}\) years, \(q_0\) between 0.01 and 1.0, and
\(\omega=6\), Table~\ref{tab:16.1} gives a rate of decrease in \(G\) between
\(4\times10^{-13}\) parts per year and \(2\times10^{-11}\) parts per year.
In contrast, the Dirac model predicts a much more rapidly decreasing
gravitational ``constant''; for \(H_0^{-1}=10^{10}\) years, the rate of
decrease of \(G\) is \(3\times10^{-10}\) parts per year.

The best experimental upper limit on the present rate of change of \(G\) comes
from the analysis of radar observations of Mercury and
Venus.\hyperref[ch16-ref-31]{[31]} For a planet in a circular orbit with
radius \(r\) and velocity \(v\), we have \(M_\odot G=v^2r\), so if the orbital
angular momentum \(mrv\) stays fixed while \(G\) changes, then \(r\) and \(v\)
will vary as
\begin{equation}
  r\propto\frac1v\propto\frac1G,
  \tag{16.4.43}\label{eq:16.4.43}
\end{equation}
and the orbital period \(2\pi r/v\) will vary as
\begin{equation}
  2\pi\frac rv\propto\frac1{G^2}.
  \tag{16.4.44}\label{eq:16.4.44}
\end{equation}
By repeated comparison of the orbital periods of the inner planets over the
period 1966--1969, with time as told by an atomic clock (which does not depend
on \(G\)), Shapiro et al.\hyperref[ch16-ref-31]{[31]} have set an upper limit
\[
  \left\lvert\frac{\dot G}{G}\right\rvert_0
  \lesssim
  4\times10^{-10}/\mathrm{year}.
\]
This is almost good enough to rule out the Dirac theory, but is not yet
sufficiently stringent to put a useful limit on the Brans--Dicke coupling
parameter \(\omega\). However, the error in these measurements of
\((\dot G/G)_0\) is expected to decrease approximately with the
\(5/2\)-power of the time span of the observations, so another five years of
observation should reduce the upper limit on \(\dot G/G\) to the value
expected in a Brans--Dicke model with \(q_0\) of order unity and \(\omega=6\).

There is also a prospect of setting an upper limit on the rate of change of
\(G\) from analysis of the flight time of laser signals, sent from the earth to
the moon, and reflected back to earth by the corner reflectors placed on the
moon's surface by the Apollo expeditions.\hyperref[ch16-ref-32]{[32]} However,
the analysis of these observations is seriously complicated by tidal effects,
which play a major role in the dynamics of the earth-moon system.
(Fortunately, such tidal effects do not seriously affect the planetary motions
that were studied by Shapiro et al.)

Variations in \(G\) over the last few millennia can perhaps be determined from
the study of ancient eclipse records.\hyperref[ch16-ref-33]{[33]} A total
eclipse of the sun occurs only over a very small portion of the earth's surface,
so the knowledge that a particular total eclipse was seen at some particular
place provides precise information on the ratio of the length of the day, which
does not strongly depend on \(G\), to the length of the year and the lunar
month, which vary like \(1/G^2\). The analysis by
Curott\hyperref[ch16-ref-34]{[34]} and Dicke\hyperref[ch16-ref-35]{[35]} of
five eclipses, which occurred between 1062 B.C. and 71 A.D., gives an average
rate of decrease in the earth's rotation rate relative to planetary periods of
\((15.9\pm0.7)\times10^{-11}\) parts per year. The earth's rotation rate is
subject to a number of known influences,\hyperref[ch16-ref-36]{[36]} in
particular a tidal deceleration, between \(23.5\times10^{-11}\) and
\(25.6\times10^{-11}\) parts per year, and an acceleration owing to the rise
in sea level and the isostatic recovery of the geoid, between
\(0.5\times10^{-11}\) and \(3.0\times10^{-11}\) parts per year. This leaves
a residual unexplained acceleration of the earth's rotation between
\(4\times10^{-11}\) and \(10\times10^{-11}\) parts per year. Since the
eclipse data measure the rate of the earth's rotation relative to planetary
periods, this apparent residual acceleration could be explained in terms of a
deceleration of planetary motions, owing to a decrease of \(G\) at a rate
between \(2\times10^{-11}\) and \(5\times10^{-11}\) parts per year. [See
Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.44}.] However, the eclipse data are somewhat ambiguous.
(Was Archilochus on Paros or Thasos during the eclipse of 648 B.C.?) More
important, there are many uncertainties in the complicated dynamics of the
earth-moon system that could account for the small residual apparent
acceleration of the earth's rotation, without appealing to a decrease in
\(G\).

It may be possible to measure changes over the past 350 million years in the
number of days in a lunar month or a year, by counting monthly or annual growth
bands and daily growth ridges on fossil
corals.\hyperref[ch16-ref-37]{[37]} However, this approach has not yet yielded
results that are precise enough to be of use to cosmologists.

A secular decrease in the constant of gravitation over billions of years would
have interesting effects on the evolution of the earth and stars, but
unfortunately, none of these effects would yield unambiguous information about
whether \(G\) really does decrease. With decreasing \(G\), the radius of the
earth would increase roughly as \(G^{-0.1}\), causing complicated damage to the
earth's crust.\hyperref[ch16-ref-38]{[38]} If \(G\) were larger in the past,
then stars would have run through their thermonuclear evolution more
rapidly;\hyperref[ch16-ref-39]{[39]} for \(G\) decreasing at a rate of
\((1\text{--}2)\times10^{-11}\) parts per year, a star whose true age is 6 to
8 billion years would appear to us to be 15 to 25 billions years
old.\hyperref[ch16-ref-40]{[40]} Finally, if \(G\) were greater in the past,
then the sun's luminosity \(L_\odot\) would have been greater by a
factor\hyperref[ch16-ref-41]{[41]} roughly proportional to \(G^8\), and the
radius \(r_\oplus\) of the earth's orbit would have been smaller by a factor
proportional to \(G^{-1}\), so the surface temperature of the earth, which
varies more or less as \((L_\odot/r_\oplus^2)^{1/4}\), would have been greater
by a factor proportional to \(G^{2.5}\). If \(G\) decreases as \(t^{-0.09}\),
as would be expected according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.4.28} for a Brans--Dicke
model with \(k=0\) and \(\omega=6\), and if the age of the universe is
\(8\times10^9\) years, then the temperature of the earth's surface
\(2\times10^9\) years ago would have been only about
\(20^\circ\mathrm C\) higher than at present, which need not have had any
drastic effect on biological evolution. On the other hand, if \(G\) has
decreased as \(1/t\) as expected in Dirac's cosmology, then the temperature of
the earth's surface \(10^9\) years ago would have been above the boiling point
of water, unless the earth's albedo was very much higher than at
present.\hyperref[ch16-ref-42]{[42]} Thus, too large an early value of the
constant of gravitation could have prevented the evolution of life forms
capable of curiosity about the universe.
