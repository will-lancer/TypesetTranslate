\subsection{The Steady State Model Revisited}\label{sec:16.3}

If the universe is not only isotropic and homogeneous in space, but also
homogeneous in \emph{time}, then, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:14.8}, its
metric must have the Robertson--Walker form, with
\begin{equation}
  k=0,
  \qquad
  a(t)\propto e^{Ht},
  \tag{16.3.1}\label{eq:16.3.1}
\end{equation}
where \(H\) is the Hubble constant, here truly a constant of nature. Also, all
scalars, such as \(\rho\) and \(p\), must be time- as well as
position-independent:
\begin{equation}
  \dot\rho=\dot p=0.
  \tag{16.3.2}\label{eq:16.3.2}
\end{equation}
The field equations underlying the steady state model were left unspecified in
Section~\ref{sec:14.8}, but it is clear that Einstein's theory would need to be
modified to be used here. The Einstein field equations are only consistent
with the Bianchi identities if the energy-momentum tensor is conserved, but a
constant pressure would violate the energy-conservation equation
\eqref{eq:14.2.19} unless \(p=-\rho\), which would require either the energy
density or the pressure to be negative.

It is therefore necessary to modify the Einstein equations by adding a
correction term\hyperref[ch16-ref-14]{[14]} \(C_{\mu\nu}\):
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mu\nu}
  -\frac12g_{\mu\nu}R
  -C_{\mu\nu}
  =
  8\pi G T_{\mu\nu}.
  \tag{16.3.3}\label{eq:16.3.3}
\end{equation}
A straightforward calculation, using Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.3.1} in
\eqref{eq:15.1.6}, \eqref{eq:15.1.7}, and \eqref{eq:15.1.11}, gives
\[
  R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12g_{\mu\nu}R=-3H^2g_{\mu\nu},
\]
so the form of the correction term required by the steady state model is
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}
  =
  -(8\pi Gp+3H^2)g_{\mu\nu}
  -8\pi G(\rho+p)u_\mu u_\nu,
  \tag{16.3.4}\label{eq:16.3.4}
\end{equation}
where \(u^\mu\) is the velocity four-vector, with \(u^0=1\) and \(u^i=0\).

In order to learn anything from Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.3.4}, we need to impose some
\emph{a priori} ideas as to the form of the tensor \(C_{\mu\nu}\).
Hoyle\hyperref[ch16-ref-14]{[14]} suggests that in general
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu C,
  \tag{16.3.5}\label{eq:16.3.5}
\end{equation}
where \(C\) is a scalar, called the \(C\)-field. Hoyle further suggests that in
the absence of all inhomogeneities or anisotropies, \(C\) is simply
proportional to the cosmic time coordinate used in the Robertson--Walker
coordinate system:
\begin{equation}
  C=At,
  \qquad
  A\ \text{constant}.
  \tag{16.3.6}\label{eq:16.3.6}
\end{equation}
It is easy to calculate the second covariant derivative:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu C
  =
  -AH(g_{\mu\nu}+u_\mu u_\nu).
  \tag{16.3.7}\label{eq:16.3.7}
\end{equation}
Comparison of \eqref{eq:16.3.7} with \eqref{eq:16.3.4} shows that the density
must take the value
\begin{equation}
  \rho=\frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}
  \tag{16.3.8}\label{eq:16.3.8}
\end{equation}
and the constant of proportionality in \(C\) is
\begin{equation}
  A=\frac{8\pi G(\rho+p)}{H}.
  \tag{16.3.9}\label{eq:16.3.9}
\end{equation}
The pressure can take any value.

The predicted density \eqref{eq:16.3.8} is the same as given by a Friedmann
model with vanishing curvature. [See Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.2.1}.] Thus the
verification of Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.3.8} would not really serve to confirm the
steady state model. Also, the steady state cosmology does not require that the
\(C_{\mu\nu}\) tensor take the form \eqref{eq:16.3.5}, \eqref{eq:16.3.6}, so we
would not be forced to give up the steady state metric if the density were
found to be different from \eqref{eq:16.3.8}.

The clearest evidence against the steady state model is the observed cosmic
microwave background, which seems to be a remnant of a quite different earlier
stage of the universe. (See Section~\ref{sec:15.5}.) However, it is not out of
the question for a microwave background to be created along with the baryons
in a steady state model. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.4.9}, the number density
of photons per unit frequency interval in a steady state model is
\[
  \begin{split}
  n_\gamma(\nu)
  ={}&
  8\pi\nu^2
  \int_{-\infty}^{t_0}
  \exp\!\left[
    -\int_t^{t_0}
    \left\{
      A\!\left(\nu e^{H(t_0-t')}\right)
      -\Omega\!\left(\nu e^{H(t_0-t')}\right)
    \right\}\dd t'
  \right]
  \\
  &{}\times
  \Omega\!\left(\nu e^{H(t_0-t)}\right)\dd t ,
  \end{split}
\]
where \(A(\nu)\) is the absorption rate of a photon of frequency \(\nu\), and
\(8\pi\nu^2\Omega(\nu)\dd\nu\) is the emission rate per unit volume of photons
between frequency \(\nu\) and \(\nu+\dd\nu\). By a simple change of variables
this can be written in the \(t_0\)-independent form
\begin{equation}
  n_\gamma(\nu)
  =
  8\pi\nu^2
  \int_\nu^\infty
  \frac{\dd\nu'}{H\nu'}\,
  \Omega(\nu')
  \exp\!\left[
    -\int_\nu^{\nu'}
    \frac{\dd\nu''}{H\nu''}
    \bigl(A(\nu'')-\Omega(\nu'')\bigr)
  \right].
  \tag{16.3.10}\label{eq:16.3.10}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to \(\nu\) yields a differential equation for
\(n_\gamma(\nu)\), which can also be written as a formula for \(\Omega(\nu)\)
in terms of \(A(\nu)\), \(n_\gamma(\nu)\), and \(n_\gamma'(\nu)\):
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(\nu)
  =
  \frac{
    [A(\nu)+2H]n_\gamma(\nu)-H\nu n_\gamma'(\nu)
  }{
    8\pi\nu^2+n_\gamma(\nu)
  }.
  \tag{16.3.11}\label{eq:16.3.11}
\end{equation}
Thus, by a suitable choice of the photon emission rate, we can arrange to get
any background distribution function \(n_\gamma(\nu)\) we want. For instance,
if we demand the observed Rayleigh--Jeans low-frequency behavior
\(n_\gamma(\nu)\propto\nu\) [see Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.5.19}] then
\eqref{eq:16.3.11} yields in the limit \(\nu\to0\):
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(0)=A(0)+H.
  \tag{16.3.12}\label{eq:16.3.12}
\end{equation}
The term \(H\) represents a purely cosmological continuous creation of photons,
unrelated to any absorption processes. We can also obtain a Planck distribution
function
\[
  n_\gamma(\nu)
  =
  \frac{8\pi\nu^2}{\exp(h\nu/kT)-1}
\]
by choosing \(\Omega(\nu)\) as
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(\nu)
  =
  e^{-h\nu/kT}A(\nu)
  +
  \frac{Hh\nu/kT}{\exp(h\nu/kT)-1}.
  \tag{16.3.13}\label{eq:16.3.13}
\end{equation}
The first term represents simply the usual emission processes that will always
accompany any absorption [compare Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.4.7}], while the second term
represents a continuous creation of photons. However, there is no \emph{a
priori} reason why the rate of continuous creation of photons should have the
particular frequency dependence shown in Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.3.13}, so from the
standpoint of a steady state model, the Planck distribution law is possible
but quite artificial. Indeed, there is no particular reason why low-frequency
photons should be continuously created at precisely the rate
\(8\pi H\nu^2\dd\nu\) required by Eq.~\eqref{eq:16.3.12}, so even the
Rayleigh--Jeans low-frequency behavior is somewhat unnatural.

Some support for the steady state model comes from quite a different quarter.
From time to time attempts have been made to formulate electrodynamics and
other field theories in terms of a direct action at a
distance.\hyperref[ch16-ref-15]{[15]} Such attempts generally foundered,
because the electromagnetic effects of charged particles were found to
correspond to an equal mixture of advanced and retarded solutions of the
Maxwell equations, rather than the usual retarded solution. In 1945 Wheeler and
Feynman\hyperref[ch16-ref-16]{[16]} showed that this difficulty could be
overcome by taking into account the electromagnetic interaction at a distance
of the accelerated and test charges with all the other charges in the universe.
However, they considered a static cosmological model, and so could obtain a net
electromagnetic interaction corresponding to either a pure retarded or a pure
advanced solution. Hogarth\hyperref[ch16-ref-17]{[17]} later suggested that
this ambiguity could be removed by considering a more realistic model, taking
into account the expansion of the universe. According to Hoyle and
Narlikar,\hyperref[ch16-ref-18]{[18]} only a purely retarded solution is
possible for a steady state model, while only a purely advanced solution is
possible for a Friedmann model with \(k\leq0\). Hoyle and Narlikar subsequently
extended these considerations to the
\(C\)-field,\hyperref[ch16-ref-19]{[19]} to the theory of
gravitation,\hyperref[ch16-ref-20]{[20]} and to quantum
electrodynamics.\hyperref[ch16-ref-21]{[21]} This line of development
certainly represents an intriguing approach to the old problem of relating the
physics of the microcosm to the properties of the universe as a whole, a problem
to which we shall return in the next section. However, it is too early to
conclude that a steady state universe is in any sense required by
considerations of microphysics, because there is no reason to suppose that
electrodynamics and other field theories \emph{need} to be formulated in terms
of an action at a distance.
