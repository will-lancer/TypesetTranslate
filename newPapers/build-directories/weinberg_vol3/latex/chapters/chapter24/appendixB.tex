\chapterappendix{B}{The Coleman-Mandula Theorem}

This appendix provides a proof of the celebrated theorem of Coleman and
Mandula~\hyperref[ch24-ref-5]{[5]} that the only possible Lie algebra (as
opposed to super-algebra) of symmetry generators consists of the generators
$P_\mu$ and $J_{\mu\nu}$ of translations and homogeneous Lorentz
transformations, together with possible internal symmetry generators, which
commute with $P_\mu$ and $J_{\mu\nu}$ and act on physical states by multiplying
them with spin-independent, momentum-independent Hermitian
matrices.\footnote{As we shall see, in theories with only massless particles
there is also the possibility that in addition to the generators $P_\mu$ and
$J_{\mu\nu}$ there are additional generators $D$ and $K_\mu$ that fill out the
Lie algebra of the conformal group~\hyperref[ch24-ref-15]{[15]}.} By
``symmetry generators'' here is meant any Hermitian operators: that commute
with the $S$-matrix; whose commutators are also symmetry generators; which take
one-particle states into one-particle states; and whose action on multiparticle
states is the direct sum of their action on one-particle states (as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.1}). A further technical requirement will be added when
needed later. Apart from the general principles of relativistic quantum
mechanics described in Chapters 2 and 3, the only other assumptions needed in
this proof are:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Assumption 1}\quad
\textit{For any $M$ there are only a finite number of particle types with mass
less than $M$.}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Assumption 2}\quad
\textit{Any two-particle state undergoes some reaction at almost all energies
(that is, at all energies except perhaps an isolated set).}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Assumption 3}\quad
\textit{The amplitudes for elastic two-body scattering are analytic functions
of the scattering angle at almost all energies and angles.}\footnote{Strictly
speaking, this assumption is not satisfied in theories with infrared
divergences such as quantum electrodynamics, where, as shown in Section 13.3,
the $S$-matrix element for any one scattering process involving charged
particles actually vanishes, except for elastic forward scattering. In
Abelian gauge theories like electrodynamics this problem can be avoided by
applying the Coleman--Mandula theorem to the theory with a fictitious gauge
boson mass, and then working only with `infrared-safe' quantities like masses
and suitably integrated cross-sections that are finite in the limit of zero
gauge boson mass. There is no problem in non-Abelian gauge theories like
quantum chromodynamics, in which all massless particles are
trapped---symmetries if unbroken would only govern $S$-matrix elements for
gauge-neutral bound states, like the mesons and baryons in quantum
chromodynamics. As far as I know, the Coleman--Mandula theorem has not been
proved for non-Abelian gauge theories with untrapped massless particles, like
quantum chromodynamics with many quark flavors.}

It is not necessary to assume that the $S$-matrix is governed by a local
quantum field theory. The proof presented here is somewhat rearranged and
streamlined, and it spells out some steps that Coleman and Mandula left to the
reader.

It is convenient to start by proving this theorem for the subalgebra
consisting of those symmetry generators $B_a$ that commute with the
four-momentum operator $P_\mu$. (This part of the theorem is of some interest
in itself; it rules out symmetries in relativistic theories that act like the
$SU(6)$ symmetry of the non-relativistic quark model.) The action of such
symmetry generators on multiparticle states is given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 B_a\ket{p m,q n,\ldots}
 ={}&\sum_{m'}(b_a(p))_{m'm}\ket{p m',q n,\ldots}
 \\[-1pt]
 &+\sum_{n'}(b_a(q))_{n'n}\ket{p m,q n',\ldots}+\cdots\,,
\end{aligned}
\tag{24.B.1}\label{eq:24.B.1}
\end{equation}
where $m$, $n$, etc. are discrete indices labelling spin $z$-components and
particle type for particles of a definite mass $\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}$, and the
$b_a(p)$ are finite Hermitian matrices, which define the action of the
$B_a$ on one-particle states.

Now, we can see from Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.1} that the mapping that takes the
$B_a$ into $b_a(p)$ for some fixed $p$ is a homomorphism in the sense
that the commutation relations
\begin{equation}
 [B_a,B_b]
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}B_c
\tag{24.B.2}\label{eq:24.B.2}
\end{equation}
are also satisfied by the Hermitian matrices $b_a(p)$:
\begin{equation}
 [b_a(p),b_b(p)]
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}b_c(p).
\tag{24.B.3}\label{eq:24.B.3}
\end{equation}
A well-known theorem proved in Section 15.2 tells us that any Lie algebra of
finite Hermitian matrices like $b_a(p)$ must be a direct sum of a compact
semi-simple Lie algebra and $U(1)$ algebras. However, we cannot immediately
apply this result to the operator algebras $B_a$ because the homomorphism
between the operators $B_a$ and matrices $b_a(p)$ is not necessarily
an isomorphism. For it to be an isomorphism would require also that whenever
$\sum_a c^a b_a(p)=0$ for some coefficients $c^a$ and
momentum $p$, then $\sum_a c^a b_a(k)=0$ for all momenta $k$,
which is equivalent to the condition $\sum_a c^a B_a=0$.

Instead of considering the homomorphism that maps the $B_a$ into the
one-particle matrices $b_a(p)$, Coleman and Mandula considered the
homomorphism that maps the $B_a$ into the matrices that define the action
of $B_a$ on two-particle states with fixed four-momenta $p$ and $q$:
\begin{equation}
 (b_a(p,q))_{m'n',mn}
 =(b_a(p))_{m'm}\delta_{n'n}
 +(b_a(q))_{n'n}\delta_{m'm}.
\tag{24.B.4}\label{eq:24.B.4}
\end{equation}
The invariance of the $S$-matrix for the elastic or quasi-elastic scattering
of two particles with four-momenta $p$ and $q$ into two particles with momenta
$p'$ and $q'$, with masses
$\sqrt{-p'_\mu p'^\mu}=\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}$ and
$\sqrt{-q'_\mu q'^\mu}=\sqrt{-q_\mu q^\mu}$, yields the condition
\begin{equation}
 b_a(p',q')S(p',q';p,q)
 =S(p',q';p,q)b_a(p,q).
\tag{24.B.5}\label{eq:24.B.5}
\end{equation}
Here $S(p',q';p,q)$ is a matrix of the same dimensionality as $b(p,q)$ and
$b(p',q')$, defined in terms of the connected $S$-matrix elements
$S(pm,qn\mathbin{\to}p'm',q'n')$ by
\begin{equation}
 S(pm,qn\mathbin{\to}p'm',q'n')
 \equiv\delta^4(p'+q'-p-q)
 \bigl(S(p',q';p,q)\bigr)_{m'n',mn}.
\tag{24.B.6}\label{eq:24.B.6}
\end{equation}
According to Assumption 2 and the optical theorem (see Section 3.6), for almost
any choice of $p$ and $q$ the elastic scattering amplitude is non-vanishing in
the forward direction, and Assumption 3 then tells us that the matrix
$S(p',q';p,q)$ is non-singular for almost all $p'$ and $q'$ on the same mass
shells and satisfying the conservation condition $p'+q'=p+q$, so for almost
all such four-momenta Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.5} is a \emph{similarity
transformation}.

It follows then that if $\sum_a c^a b_a(p,q)=0$ for almost any
fixed four-momenta $p$ and $q$, then
$\sum_a c^a b_a(p',q')=0$ for almost any four-momenta $p'$ and
$q'$ on the same mass shells that satisfy $p'+q'=p+q$. Unfortunately, this
does not tell us that $\sum_a c^a b_a(p')$ and
$\sum_a c^a b_a(q')$ vanish, but only that these matrices are
proportional to the unit matrix (with opposite coefficients). To do better, it
is necessary to consider not the $b_a(p)$ or $b_a(p,q)$, but their
traceless parts.

One immediate consequence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.5} is that
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Tr}b_a(p',q')
 =\operatorname{Tr}b_a(p,q).
\tag{24.B.7}\label{eq:24.B.7}
\end{equation}
With Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.4}, this tells us that
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 &N\bigl(\sqrt{-q_\mu q^\mu}\bigr)\operatorname{tr}b_a(p')
 +N\bigl(\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}\bigr)\operatorname{tr}b_a(q')
 \\
 &\qquad
 =N\bigl(\sqrt{-q_\mu q^\mu}\bigr)\operatorname{tr}b_a(p)
 +N\bigl(\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}\bigr)\operatorname{tr}b_a(q),
\end{aligned}
\tag{24.B.8}\label{eq:24.B.8}
\end{equation}
where $N(m)$ is the multiplicity\footnote{These multiplicity factors were not
shown explicitly by Coleman and Mandula. They are needed in justifying a step
that Coleman and Mandula made without explanation, that of defining the
symmetry generators $B_a^\#$ with traceless kernels.} of particle types
with mass $m$, and the lower case t in `tr' indicates a sum over one-particle
rather than two-particle labels. In order for this to be satisfied for almost
all mass-shell four-momenta for which $p'+q'=p+q$, it is necessary that the
function
$\operatorname{tr}b_a(p)/N\bigl(\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}\bigr)$ be
linear\footnote{A constant term is easily seen to be ruled out in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.9} by the existence of processes in which the number of
particles is not conserved, processes which are inevitable in any relativistic
quantum theory satisfying the cluster decomposition principle. Even if we
considered only two-particle processes and did not use this argument, a
constant term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.9} would only amount to a change in the
action of internal symmetries on physical states.} in $p$:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\operatorname{tr}b_a(p)}
 {N\bigl(\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}\bigr)}
 =v_a^\mu p_\mu,
\tag{24.B.9}\label{eq:24.B.9}
\end{equation}
with $v_a^\mu$ independent of $p$ (and of everything else but the
displayed indices.) We may define new symmetry generators by subtracting terms
linear in the momentum operator $P_\mu$:
\begin{equation}
 B_a^\#\equiv B_a-v_a^\mu P_\mu,
\tag{24.B.10}\label{eq:24.B.10}
\end{equation}
which according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.9} are represented on one-particle
states by the traceless matrices
\begin{equation}
 \bigl(b_a^\#(p)\bigr)_{n'n}
 =\bigl(b_a(p)\bigr)_{n'n}
 -\frac{\operatorname{tr}b_a(p)}
 {N\bigl(\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}\bigr)}\delta_{n'n}.
\tag{24.B.11}\label{eq:24.B.11}
\end{equation}
Because $P_\mu$ commutes with $B_a$ and the unit matrix commutes with
everything, the commutators of the $B_a^\#$ are the same as those of the
$B_a$, and the commutators of the $b_a^\#(p)$ are the same as those
of the $b_a(p)$:
\begin{equation}
 [B_a^\#,B_b^\#]
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}B_c
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}
 \bigl[B_c^\#+v_c^\mu P_\mu\bigr],
\tag{24.B.12}\label{eq:24.B.12}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 [b_a^\#(p),b_b^\#(p)]
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}b_c(p)
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}
 \bigl[b_c^\#(p)+v_c^\mu p_\mu\bigr].
\tag{24.B.13}\label{eq:24.B.13}
\end{equation}
Also, Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.13} and the fact that commutators of the finite
matrices $b_a^\#(p)$ have zero trace\footnote{This is one of the places
where we use Assumption 1, without which commutators need not have zero trace.
Also, at this point it is crucial that we are dealing with commutation rather
than anticommutation relations, since the unit matrix does not anticommute with
other matrices, and anticommutators of finite matrices do not necessarily have
zero trace.} imply that
$\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}v_c^\mu=0$, and using this in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.12} then shows that the $B_a^\#$ satisfy the same
commutation relations as the $B_a$:
\begin{equation}
 [B_a^\#,B_b^\#]
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}B_c^\#.
\tag{24.B.14}\label{eq:24.B.14}
\end{equation}
Because $B_a^\#$ is a symmetry generator, the scattering amplitude
satisfies
\begin{equation}
 b_a^\#(p',q')S(p',q';p,q)
 =S(p',q';p,q)b_a^\#(p,q),
\tag{24.B.15}\label{eq:24.B.15}
\end{equation}
where the $b_a^\#(p,q)$ are the matrices representing the
$B_a^\#$ on the two-particle states
\begin{equation}
 \bigl(b_a^\#(p,q)\bigr)_{m'n',mn}
 =\bigl(b_a^\#(p)\bigr)_{m'm}\delta_{n'n}
 +\bigl(b_a^\#(q)\bigr)_{n'n}\delta_{m'm}
\tag{24.B.16}\label{eq:24.B.16}
\end{equation}
and satisfy the same commutation relations as the $B_a^\#$:
\begin{equation}
 [b_a^\#(p,q),b_b^\#(p,q)]
 =\ii\sum_c C^c{}_{ab}b_c^\#(p,q).
\tag{24.B.17}\label{eq:24.B.17}
\end{equation}
The advantage of dealing with these two-particle matrices is that, since
$S(p',q';p,q)$ is a non-singular matrix, it follows that if
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p,q)=0$ for some fixed mass-shell
four-momenta $p$ and $q$, then
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p',q')=0$ for almost all $p'$ and $q'$ on
the same respective mass shells with $p'+q'=p+q$. Because we are dealing now
with traceless matrices, this tells us that
\begin{equation}
 \sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p')
 =\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(q')=0.
\tag{24.B.18}\label{eq:24.B.18}
\end{equation}

We would like to conclude from this that
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(k)=0$ for \emph{all} mass-shell
four-momenta $k$, but so far we have proved only that
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p')=0$ for almost all of those $p'$ for
which $q'=p+q-p'$ as well as $p'$ is on the mass shell (and correspondingly
for $q'$.) To get around this limitation, we can use a trick of Coleman and
Mandula, noting that if $\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p,q)=0$ then
\eqref{eq:24.B.18} and \eqref{eq:24.B.16} together yield
\[
 \sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p,q')=0,
\]
so that, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.15},
\[
 \sum_a c^a b_a^\#(k,p+q'-k)=0,
\]
and therefore
\begin{equation}
 \sum_a c^a b_a^\#(k)=0,
\tag{24.B.19}\label{eq:24.B.19}
\end{equation}
for almost all mass-shell four-momenta $k$ for which $p+q'-k$ is also on the
mass shell. Now, the conditions that $q'$ and $p+q-q'$ be on the mass shell
leave two parameters free in $q'$, so that we have enough freedom in choosing
$q'$ that the condition that $p+q'-k$ is on the mass shell leaves us free to
choose $k$ to be anything we like, at least within a finite volume of momentum
space. This volume can be adjusted to be as large as we like by taking $p$ and
$q$ sufficiently large, so if
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p,q)=0$ for some fixed mass-shell
four-momenta $p$ and $q$ then $\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(k)=0$ for
almost all mass-shell four-momenta $k$. But then if
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(k_0)\ne0$ for some particular mass-shell
four-momentum $k_0$, a scattering process in which particles with four-momenta
$k_0$ and $k$ scatter into particles with four-momenta $k'$ and $k''$ will be
forbidden by the symmetry generated by $\sum_a c^a B_a^\#$ for
almost all $k$, $k'$, and $k''$, in contradiction with our assumption about
the analyticity of the scattering amplitude. We conclude then that if
$\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(p,q)=0$ for some fixed mass-shell
four-momenta $p$ and $q$ then $\sum_a c^a b_a^\#(k)=0$ for all
$k$, and therefore $\sum_a c^a B_a^\#=0$, so that the mapping
that takes $B_a$ into $b_a^\#(p,q)$ is an isomorphism.

One immediate consequence is that, since the number of independent matrices
$b_a^\#(p,q)$ cannot exceed
$N\bigl(\sqrt{-p_\mu p^\mu}\bigr)N\bigl(\sqrt{-q_\mu q^\mu}\bigr)$, there can
be at most a finite number of independent symmetry generators $B_a$. As
emphasized by Coleman and Mandula, in proving their theorem it is not necessary
to make an independent assumption that the symmetry algebra is
finite-dimensional.

The theorems of Section 15.2 tell us further that a Lie algebra of finite
Hermitian matrices like $b_a^\#(p,q)$ for fixed $p$ and $q$ is at most
the direct sum of a semi-simple compact Lie algebra and some number of $U(1)$
Lie algebras. We have seen that this Lie algebra is isomorphic to that of the
symmetry generators $B_a^\#$, so the $B_a^\#$ must also span the
direct sum of at most a compact semi-simple Lie algebra and $U(1)$ Lie
algebras.

Let us first dispose of the $U(1)$ Lie algebras. For any pair of mass-shell
momenta $p$ and $q$ we can find a Lorentz generator $J$ that leaves both $p$
and $q$ invariant. (If $p$ and $q$ are lightlike and parallel then take $J$ to
generate rotations around the common direction of $\mathbf{p}$ and
$\mathbf{q}$. Otherwise, $p+q$ will be timelike, so we can take $J$ to
generate rotations around the common direction of $\mathbf{p}$ and
$\mathbf{q}$ in the center-of-mass frame in which $\mathbf{p}=-\mathbf{q}$.)
We can choose the basis of two-particle states to diagonalize $J$, so that
\begin{equation}
 J\ket{p m,q n}=\sigma(m,n)\ket{p m,q n}.
\tag{24.B.20}\label{eq:24.B.20}
\end{equation}
Now, $P_\mu$ commutes with all $B_a^\#$, and $[J,P_\mu]$ is a linear
combination of components of $P_\mu$, so $P_\mu$ commutes with all
$[J,B_a^\#]$, and therefore the symmetry generator $[J,B_a^\#]$
must be a linear combination of the $B_b$, which by definition form a
complete set of symmetry generators that commute with $P_\mu$. More
specifically, since the matrices representing a commutator of symmetry
generators are necessarily traceless, $[J,B_a^\#]$ has to be a linear
combination of the $B_b^\#$. But any $U(1)$ generator $B_i^\#$ (taken
Hermitian) in the algebra of the $B_b^\#$ would have to commute with all
the $B_b^\#$, and hence in particular must commute with $[J,B_i^\#]$:
\[
 [B_i^\#,[J,B_i^\#]]=0.
\]
Taking the expectation value of this double commutator in the two-particle
basis in which $J$ is diagonal, we have
\begin{equation}
 0=\sum_{m',n'}
 \bigl(\sigma(m',n')-\sigma(m,n)\bigr)
 \left|\bigl(b_i^\#(p,q)\bigr)_{m'n',mn}\right|^2,
\tag{24.B.21}\label{eq:24.B.21}
\end{equation}
for any $m$ and $n$. The indices run over a finite range, so if there were any
$\sigma$ for which there existed an $m$ and $n$ with $\sigma(m,n)=\sigma$ and
an $m'$ and $n'$ with $\sigma(m',n')\ne\sigma$, with
$\bigl(b_i^\#(p,q)\bigr)_{m'n',mn}\ne0$, then there would have to be a
\emph{smallest} such $\sigma$, in which case the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.21} for this $m$ and $n$ would be positive-definite,
contradicting Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.21}. We conclude that
$\bigl(b_i^\#(p,q)\bigr)_{m'n',mn}$ must vanish for all $m$, $n$, $m'$, and
$n'$ for which $\sigma(m',n')\ne\sigma(m,n)$. Because the algebra of the
$b_i^\#(p,q)$ is isomorphic to that of the $B_i^\#$, this means that each of
the $U(1)$ generators $B_i^\#$ commutes with $J$. Since we can choose $p+q$ to
be in any timelike direction, it follows that each of the $U(1)$ generators
$B_i^\#$ commutes with all the generators $J_{\mu\nu}$ of the homogeneous
Lorentz group. The fact that they commute with what we called `boosts' in
Section 2.5 implies that the $\bigl(b_i^\#(p)\bigr)_{n'n}$ are independent of
three-momentum, and the fact that they commute with rotations implies that the
$\bigl(b_i^\#(p)\bigr)_{n'n}$ act as unit matrices on spin indices, so these
generators are the generators of an ordinary internal symmetry.

This leaves the $B_a^\#$ that generate a semi-simple compact Lie algebra.
The argument of Section 24.1 (a somewhat more explicit version of the
reasoning given by Coleman and Mandula) tells us that the generators of the
semi-simple compact part of the Lie algebra commute with Lorentz
transformations and, as shown for the $U(1)$ generators, this means that they
too are the generators of internal symmetries. We have thus shown that the
symmetry generators $B_a$ that commute with $P_\mu$ are either internal
symmetry generators or linear combinations of the components of $P_\mu$
itself.

Next, we must take up the possibility of symmetry generators that do not
commute with the momentum operator. The action of a general symmetry generator
$A_a$ on a one-particle state $\ket{p,n}$ of four-momentum $p$ would be
\begin{equation}
 A_a\ket{p,n}
 =\sum_{n'}\int \dd^4p'\,
 \bigl(\mathcal{A}_a(p',p)\bigr)_{n'n}\ket{p',n'},
\tag{24.B.22}\label{eq:24.B.22}
\end{equation}
where $n$ and $n'$ are again discrete indices labelling both spin
$z$-components and particle types. Of course, the kernel
$\mathcal{A}_a(p',p)$ must vanish unless both $p$ and $p'$ are on the
mass shell. We shall show first that the $\mathcal{A}_a(p',p)$ vanishes
for any $p'\ne p$.

For this purpose, note that if $A_a$ is a symmetry generator, then so is
\begin{equation}
 A_a^f\equiv\int \dd^4x\,
 \exp(\ii P\cdot x)A_a\exp(-\ii P\cdot x)f(x),
\tag{24.B.23}\label{eq:24.B.23}
\end{equation}
where $P_\mu$ is the four-momentum operator, and $f(x)$ is a function that can
be chosen as we like. Acting on a one-particle state, this gives
\begin{equation}
 A_a^f\ket{p,n}
 =\sum_{n'}\int \dd^4p'\,\widetilde f(p'-p)
 \bigl(\mathcal{A}_a(p',p)\bigr)_{n'n}\ket{p',n'},
\tag{24.B.24}\label{eq:24.B.24}
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde f$ is the Fourier transform
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde f(k)\equiv\int \dd^4x\,\exp(\ii x\cdot k)f(x).
\tag{24.B.25}\label{eq:24.B.25}
\end{equation}
Suppose that there is some pair of mass-shell four-momenta $p$ and $p+\Delta$
with $\Delta\ne0$ such that $\mathcal{A}(p+\Delta,p)\ne0$. Generic mass-shell
four-momenta $q$, $p'$, and $q'$ with $p'+q'=p+q$ will not have $q+\Delta$ or
$p'+\Delta$ or $q'+\Delta$ on the mass shell. If we take $\widetilde f(k)$ to
vanish outside a sufficiently small region around $\Delta$, then $A_a^f$
will annihilate all one-particle states with four-momenta $q$, $p'$, and $q'$,
but not the one-particle states with four-momentum $p$, so such a symmetry
would forbid any scattering process in which any particles of momenta $p$ and
$q$ go into any particles of momenta $p'$ and $q'$, in contradiction with the
consequence of Assumptions 2 and 3 that there is some scattering at almost all
energies and angles.

This result does not mean that any symmetry generator $A_a$ must commute
with $P_\mu$, because the kernels $\mathcal{A}_a(p',p)$ may include terms
proportional to derivatives of $\delta^4(p'-p)$ as well as terms proportional
to $\delta^4(p'-p)$ itself. To deal with this possibility, Coleman and Mandula
made the `ugly technical assumption' that the kernels
$\mathcal{A}_a(p',p)$ are \emph{distributions}, which means that each can
contain at most a finite number $D_a$ of derivatives of
$\delta^4(p'-p)$. To put this another way, each symmetry generator $A_a$
is assumed to act on one-particle states as a polynomial of order $D_a$
in the derivatives $\partial/\partial p_\mu$, with matrix coefficients that at
this point are allowed to depend on momentum and spin. To use the above
results for symmetry generators that commute with the momentum operators,
Coleman and Mandula considered the $D_a$-fold commutator of momentum
operators with $A_a$
\begin{equation}
 B_a^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 \equiv
 [P^{\mu_1},[P^{\mu_2},\ldots,
 [P^{\mu_{D_a}},A_a]]\ldots]].
\tag{24.B.26}\label{eq:24.B.26}
\end{equation}
The matrix elements of the commutators of
$B_a^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ with $P^\mu$ between states with
four-momenta $p'$ and $p$ are proportional to $D_a+1$ factors of $p'-p$,
times a polynomial of order $D_a$ in momentum derivatives acting on
$\delta^4(p'-p)$, and therefore vanish. Since the generators
$B_a^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ commute with the momentum operators,
according to the results obtained so far they act on one-particle states with
matrices of the form
\begin{equation}
 b_a^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}(p)
 =b_a^{\#\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 +v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}p_\mu\mathbf{1},
\tag{24.B.27}\label{eq:24.B.27}
\end{equation}
where the $b_a^{\#\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ are
momentum-independent traceless Hermitian matrices generating an ordinary
internal symmetry algebra, and the
$v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ are momentum-independent numerical
constants, with both $b_a^{\#\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ and
$v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ symmetric in the indices
$\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}$. Also, even though the $A_a$ do not
necessarily commute with $P_\mu$, they cannot take one-particle states off the
mass shell, so since Assumption 1 requires that the mass-squared operator
$-P_\mu P^\mu$ has only discrete eigenvalues, the $A_a$ must commute with
$-P_\mu P^\mu$. It follows in particular that for $D\geq1$
\[
 0=[P^\mu P_\mu,[P^{\mu_2},\ldots,
 [P^{\mu_{D_a}},A_a]]\ldots]]
 =2P_{\mu_1}B_a^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}},
\]
so that
\begin{equation}
 0=p_{\mu_1}b_a^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}(p).
\tag{24.B.28}\label{eq:24.B.28}
\end{equation}
As long as the theory contains massive particles this must be satisfied for
$p$ in any timelike direction, so for $D_a\geq1$
\begin{equation}
 b_a^{\#\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}=0,
\tag{24.B.29}\label{eq:24.B.29}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 =-v_a^{\mu_1\mu\cdots\mu_{D_a}}.
\tag{24.B.30}\label{eq:24.B.30}
\end{equation}
But for $D_a\geq2$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.30} together with the symmetry of
$v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}$ in the indices
$\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}$ would require that
$v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}=0$. (For then
\[
\begin{aligned}
 v_a^{\mu\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 &=v_a^{\mu\mu_2\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 =-v_a^{\mu_2\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 =-v_a^{\mu_2\mu_1\mu\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 \\
 &=v_a^{\mu_1\mu_2\mu\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 =v_a^{\mu_1\mu\mu_2\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 =-v_a^{\mu\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_{D_a}}\text{.)}
\end{aligned}
\]
We are left with at most two kinds of non-vanishing symmetry generator: those
with $D_a=0$, for which the generator $A_a$ commutes with $P_\mu$
and therefore must be either an internal symmetry generator or some linear
combination of the $P_\mu$; and those with $D_a=1$, in which case
\begin{equation}
 [P^\nu,A_a]=v_a^{\mu\nu}P_\mu,
\tag{24.B.31}\label{eq:24.B.31}
\end{equation}
with $v_a^{\mu\nu}$ some numerical constants antisymmetric in $\mu$ and
$\nu$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.31} requires that
\begin{equation}
 A_a=-\frac{1}{2}i v_a^{\mu\nu}J_{\mu\nu}+B_a,
\tag{24.B.32}\label{eq:24.B.32}
\end{equation}
\begingroup
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{10pt}
where $J_{\mu\nu}$ is the generator of proper Lorentz transformations, which
according to Eq.~(2.4.13) satisfies
$[P^\nu,J^{\rho\sigma}]
=-\ii\eta^{\nu\rho}P^\sigma\allowbreak
{}+\ii\eta^{\nu\sigma}P^\rho$, and $B_a$ commutes
with $P_\mu$. Since $A_a$ and $J_{\mu\nu}$ are symmetry generators, so is
$B_a$, which must therefore be a linear combination of internal symmetry
generators and/or the components of $P_\mu$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.32} therefore
completes the proof of the Coleman--Mandula theorem.
\par
\endgroup

In theories with only massless particles Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.30} does not
necessarily follow from Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.28}; since $p_\mu p^\mu=0$, we can
also have
\begin{equation}
 v_a^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 +v_a^{\mu_1\mu\cdots\mu_{D_a}}
 \propto\eta^{\mu\mu_1}.
\tag{24.B.33}\label{eq:24.B.33}
\end{equation}
In this case the symmetry algebra consists of internal symmetries plus the
algebra of the conformal group, which is spanned by generators $K^\mu$ and $D$
together with the generators $J^{\mu\nu}$ and $P^\mu$ of the Poincar\'e group.
The commutation relations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 [P^\mu,D]&=\ii P^\mu,
 &\qquad [K^\mu,D]&=-\ii K^\mu,
 \\
 [P^\mu,K^\nu]&=2\ii\eta^{\mu\nu}D+2\ii J^{\mu\nu},
 & [K^\mu,K^\nu]&=0,
 \\
 [J^{\rho\sigma},K^\mu]
 &=\ii\eta^{\mu\rho}K^\sigma-\ii\eta^{\mu\sigma}K^\rho,
 & [J^{\rho\sigma},D]&=0,
\end{aligned}
\tag{24.B.34}\label{eq:24.B.34}
\end{equation}
together with the commutation relations (2.4.12)--(2.4.14) of the Poincar\'e
algebra
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \ii[J^{\mu\nu},J^{\rho\sigma}]
 &=\eta^{\nu\rho}J^{\mu\sigma}
 -\eta^{\mu\rho}J^{\nu\sigma}
 -\eta^{\sigma\mu}J^{\rho\nu}
 +\eta^{\sigma\nu}J^{\rho\mu},
 \\
 \ii[P^\mu,J^{\rho\sigma}]
 &=\eta^{\mu\rho}P^\sigma-\eta^{\mu\sigma}P^\rho,
 \\
 [P^\mu,P^\rho]&=0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{24.B.35}\label{eq:24.B.35}
\end{equation}
The infinitesimal group element
\begin{equation}
 U(1+\omega,\epsilon,\lambda,\rho)
 =1+(\ii/2)J_{\mu\nu}\omega^{\mu\nu}
 +\ii P_\mu\epsilon^\mu+\ii\lambda D+\ii K_\mu\rho^\mu
\tag{24.B.36}\label{eq:24.B.36}
\end{equation}
induces the infinitesimal spacetime transformation
\begin{equation}
 x^\mu\longrightarrow x^\mu+\omega^{\mu\nu}x_\nu+\epsilon^\mu
 +\lambda x^\mu+\rho^\mu x^\nu x_\nu-2x^\mu\rho^\nu x_\nu.
\tag{24.B.37}\label{eq:24.B.37}
\end{equation}
These are the most general infinitesimal spacetime transformations that leave
the light-cone invariant.
