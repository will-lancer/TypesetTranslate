\subsection{General Supersymmetry Algebras}

Our analysis of the general supersymmetry algebra in higher dimensions will
follow the same logical outline as the work of Haag, Lopuszanski, and
Sohnius~\hyperref[ch32-ref-5]{[5]} on supersymmetry algebras in four
spacetime dimensions, described in Section 25.2. The proof of the
Coleman--Mandula theorem in the appendix of Chapter 24 makes it clear that
the list of possible bosonic symmetry generators is essentially the same in
\(d>2\) spacetime dimensions as in four spacetime dimensions: in an
\(S\)-matrix theory of particles, there are only the momentum \(d\)-vector
\(P^\mu\), a Lorentz generator \(J^{\mu\nu}=-J^{\nu\mu}\) (with \(\mu\)
and \(\nu\) here running over the values \(1,2,\ldots,d-1,0\)), and various
Lorentz scalar `charges.' (In some theories there are topologically stable
extended objects such as closed strings, membranes, etc., in addition to
particles, which make possible other conserved quantities, to which we will
return in Section 32.3.) The anticommutators of the fermionic symmetry
generators with each other are bosonic symmetry generators, and therefore
must be a linear combination of \(P^\mu\), \(J^{\mu\nu}\), and various
conserved scalars. This puts severe limits on the Lorentz transformation
properties of the fermionic generators, and on the superalgebra to which
they belong.

We will first prove that the general fermionic symmetry generator must
transform according to the fundamental spinor representations of the
Lorentz group, which are reviewed in the appendix to this chapter, and not
in higher spinor representations, such those obtained by adding vector
indices to a spinor. As we saw in Section 25.2, the proof for \(d=4\) by
Haag, Lopuszanski, and Sohnius made use of the isomorphism of \(SO(4)\) to
\(SU(2)\times SU(2)\), which has no analog in higher dimensions.
Here we will use an argument of Nahm~\hyperref[ch32-ref-6]{[6]}, which is
actually somewhat simpler and applies in any number of dimensions.

Since the Lorentz transform of any fermionic symmetry generator is another
fermionic symmetry generator, the fermionic symmetry generators furnish a
representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group \(O(d-1,d)\) (or, strictly
speaking, of its covering group \(Spin(d-1,1)\)). Assuming that there are at
most a finite number of fermionic symmetry generators, they must transform
according to a finite-dimensional representation of the homogeneous Lorentz
group. All of these representations can be obtained from the
finite-dimensional \textit{unitary} representations of the corresponding
orthogonal group \(O(d)\) (actually \(Spin(d)\)) by setting \(x^d=ix^0\).
So let us first consider the transformation of the fermionic generators
under \(O(d)\). For \(d\) even or odd, we can find \(d/2\) or \((d-1)/2\)
Lorentz generators \(J_{d1}\), \(J_{23}\), \(J_{45},\ldots\), which all
commute with each other, and classify fermionic generators \(Q\) according
to the values \(\sigma_{d1},\sigma_{23},\ldots\) that they destroy:
\begin{equation}
  [J_{d1},Q]=-\sigma_{d1}Q,\qquad
  [J_{23},Q]=-\sigma_{23}Q,\qquad
  [J_{45},Q]=-\sigma_{45}Q,\ldots
  \tag{32.1.1}\label{eq:32.1.1}
\end{equation}
Since the finite-dimensional representations of \(O(d)\) are all unitary,
the \(\sigma\)s are all real.

Let us focus on one of these quantum numbers, \(\sigma_{d1}\equiv w\), and
refer to any fermionic or bosonic operator \(O\) as having \textit{weight}
\(w\) if
\begin{equation}
  [J_{d1},O]=-wO\,,
  \tag{32.1.2}\label{eq:32.1.2}
\end{equation}
or, in terms of the Minkowski component \(J_{01}=iJ_{d1}\),
\begin{equation}
  [J_{01},O]=-iwO\,.
  \tag{32.1.3}\label{eq:32.1.3}
\end{equation}
The reason for concentrating on this particular quantum number is that it
has the special property of being the same for an operator and its Hermitian
adjoint. This is because \(J_{01}\) must be represented on Hilbert space
(though not on field variables or symmetry generators) by a Hermitian
operator, so that (remembering that \(w\) is real) the Hermitian adjoint of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.3} is
\begin{equation}
  -[J_{01},O^*]=+iwO^*\,,
  \tag{32.1.4}\label{eq:32.1.4}
\end{equation}
so \(O^*\) has the same weight as \(O\).

Now consider the anticommutator \(\{Q,Q^*\}\) of any fermionic symmetry
generator \(Q\) with its Hermitian adjoint. According to the
Coleman--Mandula theorem, it is at most a linear combination of \(P_\mu\),
\(J_{\mu\nu}\), and scalars. To calculate the weights of the components of
\(P_\mu\), we recall the commutation relation \eqref{eq:2.4.13}
\[
  i[P_\mu,J_{\rho\sigma}]
  =\eta_{\mu\rho}P_\sigma-\eta_{\mu\sigma}P_\rho\,,
\]
which shows that \(P_0\pm P_1\) has weight \(w=\pm1\), while the other
components \(P_2,P_3,\ldots,P_{d-1}\) all have weight zero. In the same
way, the commutation relation \eqref{eq:2.4.12} of the \(J_{\mu\nu}\) with
each other show that \(J_{0i}\pm J_{1i}\) with \(i=2,3,\ldots,d-1\) have
weight \(w=\pm1\), the \(J_{ij}\) with both \(i\) and \(j\) between 2 and
\(d-1\) have weight zero, \(J_{10}\) has weight zero, and of course all
scalars have weight zero. We conclude then that all bosonic symmetry
generators have weight \(\pm1\) or 0 and the anticommutator \(\{Q,Q^*\}\)
must be a linear combination of operators with such weights. If \(Q\) has
weight \(w\) then \(\{Q,Q^*\}\) has weight \(2w\), and it is manifestly
non-zero for any non-zero \(Q\), so each fermionic generator can only have
weight \(\pm1/2\). (Weight zero is excluded by the connection between spin
and statistics---fermionic operators can only be constructed from odd
numbers of operators with half-integer weights.) Going back to the
Euclidean formalism, since the commutators of the particular \(O(d)\)
generator \(J_{01}\) with all generators \(Q\) in a representation of
\(O(d)\) are given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.2} with \(w=\pm1/2\), and there
is nothing special about the 01 plane, \(O(d)\) invariance requires that
the same is true for all \(O(d)\) generators \(J_{ij}\), so that all the
\(\sigma\)s in Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.1} are \(\pm1/2\). The only irreducible
representations of the homogeneous Lorentz group with all \(\sigma\)s equal
to \(\pm1/2\) are the fundamental spinor representations, so \(Q\) must
belong to some direct sum of these representations.

We can also use this approach to show that the fermionic generators \(Q\)
all commute with the \(d\)-momentum \(P_\mu\). For this purpose, note that
the double commutator of a momentum operator \(P_0\pm P_1\) of weight
\(\pm1\) with any fermionic generator \(Q\) would have weight either
\(\pm5/2\) if \(Q\) has weight \(\pm1/2\) or weight \(\pm3/2\) if \(Q\)
has weight \(\mp1/2\), and since we have found that there are no fermionic
symmetry generators of weight \(\pm3/2\) or \(\pm5/2\), these double
commutators must all vanish:
\[
  [P_0\pm P_1,[P_0\pm P_1,Q]]=0\,.
\]
It follows then that
\[
  [P_0\pm P_1,[P_0\pm P_1,\{Q,Q^*\}]]
  =-2\{Q_\pm,Q_\pm^*\}\,,
\]
where
\[
  Q_\pm\equiv[P_0\pm P_1,Q]\,.
\]
Now, \(\{Q,Q^*\}\) is at most a linear combination of \(J\)s, \(P\)s,
and scalar symmetry generators. The commutators of \(P_0\pm P_1\) with
the \(P\)s and scalar symmetry generators vanish, while the commutators of
\(P_0\pm P_1\) with the \(J\)s are linear combinations of \(P\)s, which
commute with the other \(P_0\pm P_1\), so the double commutator
\([P_0\pm P_1,[P_0\pm P_1,\{Q,Q^*\}]]\) must vanish and therefore
\(\{Q_\pm,Q_\pm^*\}=0\), which implies that \(Q_\pm=0\). Since
\textit{all} members of the representation of the Lorentz group provided
by the \(Q\)s thus commute with \(P_0\) and \(P_1\), Lorentz invariance
implies that all \(Q\)s commute with all \(P\)s, as was to be shown.

There is an important corollary that since the Lorentz generators
\(J_{\mu\nu}\) do not commute with the momentum operators, they cannot
appear on the right-hand side of the anticommutation relations. For the
moment let us label the \(Q\)s as \(Q_n\), where \(n\) runs over the labels
for the different (not necessarily inequivalent) irreducible spinor
representations among the \(Q\)s, now \textit{including} their adjoints
\(Q^*\), and also over the index labelling members of these representations.
The general anticommutation relation is then of the form
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_n,Q_m\}=\Gamma^\mu_{nm}P_\mu+Z_{nm}\,,
  \tag{32.1.5}\label{eq:32.1.5}
\end{equation}
where the \(\Gamma^\mu_{nm}\) are c-number coefficients and the \(Z_{nm}\)
are conserved scalar symmetry generators, which commute with the \(P_\mu\)
and \(J_{\mu\nu}\). We now want to show that the \(Z_{nm}\) are
\textit{central charges} of the supersymmetry algebra---that is, that they
commute with the \(Q_\ell\) and each other as well as with the \(P_\mu\)
and \(J_{\mu\nu}\) and all other symmetry generators.

To prove this for \(d\geq4\), note that for a given \(Z_{nm}\) to be
non-zero, since it is a scalar all of the \(\sigma\)s in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.1} must be opposite for \(Q_n\) and \(Q_m\). Consider
another fermionic symmetry generator \(Q_\ell\), for which the \(\sigma\)s
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.1} are not all the same as those of either \(Q_n\)
or \(Q_m\). (For \(d\geq4\) there is always such a \(Q_\ell\) in each set
of \(Q\)s forming an irreducible spinor representation of \(O(d)\).) We
apply the super-Jacobi identity
\begin{equation}
  [Q_\ell,\{Q_m,Q_n\}]
  +[Q_m,\{Q_n,Q_\ell\}]
  +[Q_n,\{Q_\ell,Q_m\}]=0\,.
  \tag{32.1.6}\label{eq:32.1.6}
\end{equation}
The anticommutators \(\{Q_n,Q_\ell\}\) and \(\{Q_\ell,Q_m\}\) are
operators that have some \(\sigma\)s non-zero, so they can only be linear
combinations of \(P\)s rather than \(Z\)s, and so must commute with all
\(Q\)s. This leaves just
\begin{equation}
  0=[Q_\ell,\{Q_m,Q_n\}]=[Q_\ell,Z_{mn}]\,.
  \tag{32.1.7}\label{eq:32.1.7}
\end{equation}
Thus in each set of \(Q\)s forming an irreducible spinor representation of
\(O(d)\) there is at least one that commutes with the given \(Z_{mn}\).
But \(Z_{mn}\) is a Lorentz scalar, so it must then commute with all \(Q\)s.
It follows then immediately from Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.5} that they also
commute with each other.

The fermionic generators must form a representation (perhaps trivial) of
the algebra \(\mathcal A\) consisting of \textit{all} scalar bosonic
symmetry generators. It follows then by precisely the same argument used in
Section 25.2 that the central charges \(Z_{mn}\) furnish an invariant
Abelian subalgebra of \(\mathcal A\). The Coleman--Mandula theorem tells us
that \(\mathcal A\) must be a direct sum of a compact semi-simple Lie
algebra, which by definition contains no invariant Abelian subalgebras,
together with \(U(1)\) generators, so the \(Z_{mn}\) must be \(U(1)\)
generators, which commute with all other bosonic symmetry generators, not
just with each other.

To obtain more detailed information about the structure of the
anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:32.1.5}, we must be more specific about
the Lorentz transformation and reality properties of the fermionic symmetry
generators \(Q_n\). These are very different for spacetimes of even and odd
dimensionality.

\subsubsection*{Odd Dimensionality}

The appendix to this chapter shows that for odd spacetime dimensions \(d\)
there is just one fundamental spinor representation of the Lorentz algebra,
by matrices \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\) given in terms of Dirac matrices by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.2}, so we must label the fermionic generators as
\(Q_{\alpha r}\), where \(\alpha\) is a \(2^{(d-1)/2}\)-valued Dirac
index, and \(r=1,2,\ldots,N\) labels different spinors in the case of
\(N\)-extended supersymmetry. With this notation, the Lorentz transformation
properties of the \(Q\)s imply that
\begin{equation}
  [J_{\mu\nu},Q_{\alpha r}]
  =-\sum_\beta(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu})_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\beta r}\,,
  \tag{32.1.8}\label{eq:32.1.8}
\end{equation}
so that the anticommutators of these generators have the transformation rule
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  [J_{\mu\nu},\{Q_{\alpha r},Q_{\beta s}\}]
  ={}&-\sum_{\bar\alpha}
    (\mathcal J_{\mu\nu})_{\alpha\bar\alpha}
    \{Q_{\bar\alpha r},Q_{\beta s}\}
  \\
  &-\sum_{\bar\beta}
    (\mathcal J_{\mu\nu})_{\beta\bar\beta}
    \{Q_{\alpha r},Q_{\bar\beta s}\}\,.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Recalling the Lorentz transformation rule \eqref{eq:2.4.13} for the
momentum operator \(P_\lambda\), we see that the matrix
\(\Gamma^\lambda_{rs}\) and the operator \(Z_{rs}\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.5} (with Dirac indices now suppressed) must satisfy the
conditions
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}(\Gamma_\lambda)_{rs}
  +(\Gamma_\lambda)_{rs}\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm T}
  =-i(\Gamma_\mu)_{rs}\eta_{\nu\lambda}
   +i(\Gamma_\nu)_{rs}\eta_{\mu\lambda}\,,
  \tag{32.1.9}\label{eq:32.1.9}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}Z_{rs}
  +Z_{rs}\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm T}=0\,.
  \tag{32.1.10}\label{eq:32.1.10}
\end{equation}
But Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.38} gives
\(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm T}
=-\mathcal C^{-1}\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\mathcal C\), so
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.1.9} and \eqref{eq:32.1.10} may be expressed as the
requirement that \((\Gamma_\mu)_{rs}\mathcal C^{-1}\) satisfies the same
commutation relation \eqref{eq:32.A.32} with \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\) as
\(\gamma_\mu\), while \(Z_{rs}\mathcal C^{-1}\) commutes with
\(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\). For odd \(d\) the matrices satisfying these
conditions are unique up to multiplication with constants, so we can
conclude that
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha r\,\beta s}
  =ig_{rs}(\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C)_{\alpha\beta}
  \tag{32.1.11}\label{eq:32.1.11}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\alpha r\,\beta s}=\mathcal C_{\alpha\beta}z_{rs}\,,
  \tag{32.1.12}\label{eq:32.1.12}
\end{equation}
with the factor \(i\) inserted in Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.11} for later
convenience. With Dirac indices suppressed, the anticommutation relations
\eqref{eq:32.1.5} now read
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r,Q_s^{\mathrm T}\}
  =ig_{rs}\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda+z_{rs}\mathcal C\,.
  \tag{32.1.13}\label{eq:32.1.13}
\end{equation}

Both \(\Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha r\,\beta s}\) and
\(Z_{\alpha r\,\beta s}\) are symmetric under interchange of \(\alpha\)
and \(r\) with \(\beta\) and \(s\), while Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.30} and
\eqref{eq:32.A.31} (with \(d=2n+1\)) show that:
\(\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C\) is symmetric and \(\mathcal C\) is symmetric
for \(d=1\pmod 8\); \(\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C\) is symmetric and
\(\mathcal C\) is antisymmetric for \(d=3\pmod 8\);
\(\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C\) is antisymmetric and \(\mathcal C\) is
antisymmetric for \(d=5\pmod 8\); and
\(\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C\) is antisymmetric and \(\mathcal C\) is
symmetric for \(d=7\pmod 8\). It follows that: \(g_{rs}\) is symmetric and
\(z_{rs}\) is symmetric for \(d=1\pmod 8\); \(g_{rs}\) is symmetric and
\(z_{rs}\) is antisymmetric for \(d=3\pmod 8\); \(g_{rs}\) is
antisymmetric and \(z_{rs}\) is antisymmetric for \(d=1\pmod 8\); and
\(g_{rs}\) is antisymmetric and \(z_{rs}\) is symmetric for
\(d=1\pmod 8\).

The complex conjugate of the matrices \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\) is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.37}. By taking the Hermitian adjoint of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.8}, we see that
\(\sum_\beta(\mathcal C\beta)_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\beta r}^*\) has the same
Lorentz transformation properties as any \(Q_{\alpha s}\), and therefore
must be a linear combination of them
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\beta(\mathcal C\beta)_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\beta r}^*
  =\sum_s\mathcal S_{rs}Q_{\alpha s}\,.
  \tag{32.1.14}\label{eq:32.1.14}
\end{equation}
Taking the Hermitian adjoint of this equation and using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.28} and \eqref{eq:32.A.29} with \(d=2n+1\) yields
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal S^*\mathcal S=(-1)^a\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad a=(d-1)(d-3)/8\,.
  \tag{32.1.15}\label{eq:32.1.15}
\end{equation}
For \(d=1\pmod 8\) and \(d=3\pmod 8\) the spinor representation of the
Lorentz algebra is real, and we can choose a basis for the fermionic
generators such that \(\mathcal S=\mathbf{1}\). In contrast, for
\(d=5\pmod 8\) and \(d=7\pmod 8\) the spinor representation of the Lorentz
algebra is pseudoreal, and it is evidently impossible to choose a basis
with \(\mathcal S\propto\mathbf{1}\). By taking the determinant of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.15}, we see that in this case
\(\operatorname{Det}(-\mathbf{1})>0\), so for \(d=5\pmod 8\) and
\(d=7\pmod 8\) there must be an even number \(N\) of fermionic generators.
In this case we can choose a basis in which \(\mathcal S=\Omega\), where
\(\Omega\) is the real antisymmetric block-diagonal matrix
\begin{equation}
  \Omega=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    e&0&0&\cdots\\
    0&e&0&\cdots\\
    0&0&e&\cdots\\
    &&&\ddots
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  e=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&1\\
    -1&0
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{32.1.16}\label{eq:32.1.16}
\end{equation}

We can deduce the reality and positivity properties of \(g_{rs}\) and
\(z_{rs}\) by using Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.14} to rewrite the anticommutation
relation \eqref{eq:32.1.13} as
\[
  \{Q_r,Q_s^\dagger\}
  =i(g\mathcal S^{\mathrm T})_{rs}
    \gamma_\lambda\mathcal C(\mathcal C\beta)^{\mathrm T-1}P^\lambda
  +(z\mathcal S^{\mathrm T})_{rs}
    \mathcal C(\mathcal C\beta)^{\mathrm T-1}.
\]
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.12}, \eqref{eq:32.A.16}, and
\eqref{eq:32.A.30} with \(d=2n+1\) show that
\(\beta^{\mathrm T}=-\beta\) and
\(\mathcal C\mathcal C^{\mathrm T-1}
=(-1)^{(d-1)(d+1)/8}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{1}\), so
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r,Q_s^\dagger\}
  =-(-1)^{(d-1)(d+1)/8}
  \left[
    i(g\mathcal S^{\mathrm T})_{rs}\gamma_\lambda\beta P^\lambda
    +(z\mathcal S^{\mathrm T})_{rs}\beta
  \right].
  \tag{32.1.17}\label{eq:32.1.17}
\end{equation}
Recalling that \(\gamma_0=i\beta\), we note that the operator matrix
\(-i\gamma_\lambda\beta P^\lambda\) is positive, and positive-definite
aside from the vacuum state. By considering a state of sufficiently large
momentum so that the central charge term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.17} may be
neglected, we conclude that the matrix
\((-1)^{(d-1)(d+1)/8}g\mathcal S^{\mathrm T}\) is positive and Hermitian.
Then considering arbitrary momenta, we also find that the array of operators
\((z\mathcal S^{\mathrm T})_{rs}\) is Hermitian. (For non-zero central
charges there is a lower bound on the mass, analogous to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.5.22}, which will not be given here.) From the Hermiticity
of \(g\mathcal S^{\mathrm T}\) we have
\begin{equation}
  g^\dagger
  =\mathcal S^{\mathrm T\dagger-1}g\mathcal S^{\mathrm T}
  =(-1)^a\mathcal Sg\mathcal S^{\mathrm T}\,.
  \tag{32.1.18}\label{eq:32.1.18}
\end{equation}
We are now in a position by an appropriate choice of basis to put the
anticommutation relations in a convenient canonical form.

For \(d=1\pmod 8\) we have \(g\) and \(z\) symmetric and
\((-1)^a=+1\), so if we choose a basis in which
\(\mathcal S=\mathbf{1}\) then \(g\) is real and the individual \(z_{rs}\)
are Hermitian operators. We may introduce new \(Q\)s without changing
\(\mathcal S=\mathbf{1}\) by multiplying the old \(Q\)s by any real matrix
\(\mathcal A\), with the result that \(g\) is changed to
\(\mathcal A g\mathcal A^{\mathrm T}\). Since \(g\) is a positive matrix
for \(d=1\pmod 8\), by a well-known theorem~\hyperref[ch32-ref-6a]{[6a]}
we may choose \(\mathcal A\) to make \(g=\mathbf{1}\).

For \(d=3\pmod 8\) we have \(g\) symmetric, \(z\) antisymmetric, and
\((-1)^a=+1\), so if we choose a basis in which
\(\mathcal S=\mathbf{1}\) then \(g\) is real and the individual \(z_{rs}\)
are anti-Hermitian operators. As in the case of \(d=1\pmod 8\), we may
further adapt the basis so that \(g=\mathbf{1}\).

For \(d=5\pmod 8\) we have \(g\) antisymmetric, so, with the choice
\(\mathcal S=\Omega\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.18} reads
\(g^*=-\Omega g\Omega\), where \(\Omega\) is the standard antisymmetric
matrix \eqref{eq:32.1.16}. Here we may introduce new \(Q\)s while keeping
\(\mathcal S=\Omega\) by multiplying the old ones by any matrix
\(\mathcal B\) with \(\mathcal B^*=-\Omega\mathcal B\Omega\), with the
effect that \(g\) is changed to \(\mathcal B g\mathcal B^{\mathrm T}\).
Since \((-1)^a=-1\), \(g\Omega\) is positive, and so in this way we may
arrange that \(g=-\Omega\). Also, \(z\) is antisymmetric and \(z\Omega\)
is Hermitian, so \(z^*=-\Omega z\Omega\).

For \(d=7\pmod 8\) we again have \(g\) antisymmetric, but now
\((-1)^a=+1\), so by the same method as for \(d=5\pmod 8\), we may choose
a basis in which \(g=+\Omega\). Also, \(z\) is now symmetric and
\(z\Omega\) is again Hermitian, so now \(z^*=+\Omega z\Omega\).

\subsubsection*{Even Dimensionality}

The appendix to this chapter shows that for even spacetime dimensions \(d\)
there are two inequivalent fundamental spinor representations of the Lorentz
algebra by matrices \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\) given in terms of Dirac
matrices by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.22}, \eqref{eq:32.A.2}, and
\eqref{eq:32.A.17}. Therefore here we must label the fermionic generators as
\(Q_{\alpha r}^{\pm}\), where \(\alpha\) is a \(2^{d/2}\)-valued Dirac
index, \(r\) labels different \(Q\)s belonging to equivalent
representations of the Lorentz algebra in the case of extended
supersymmetry, and
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\beta(\gamma_{d+1})_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\beta r}^{\pm}
  =\pm Q_{\alpha r}^{\pm}\,,
  \tag{32.1.19}\label{eq:32.1.19}
\end{equation}
where
\(\gamma_{d+1}=i^{d/2-1}\gamma_1\cdots\gamma_{d-1}\gamma_0\). With this
notation, the Lorentz transformation properties of the \(Q\)s imply that
\begin{equation}
  [J_{\mu\nu},Q_{\alpha r}^{\pm}]
  =-\sum_\beta(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm})_{\alpha\beta}
  Q_{\beta r}^{\pm}\,,
  \tag{32.1.20}\label{eq:32.1.20}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\) are the matrices
\eqref{eq:32.A.22}. Taking account of Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.19} and the
relation
\(\mathcal C^{-1}\gamma_{d+1}\mathcal C=(-1)^{d/2}\gamma_{d+1}\), the same
arguments that gave the anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:32.1.13} for
odd \(d\) now give
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\mp(-1)^{d/2}\mathrm T}\}
  =ig_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda\,,
  \tag{32.1.21}\label{eq:32.1.21}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\pm(-1)^{d/2}\mathrm T}\}
  =z_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)\mathcal C\,.
  \tag{32.1.22}\label{eq:32.1.22}
\end{equation}

Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.30} and \eqref{eq:32.A.31} show that
\(\mathcal C\gamma^\lambda\) is symmetric for \(d=0\pmod 8\) and
\(d=2\pmod 8\) and antisymmetric for \(d=4\pmod 8\) and
\(d=6\pmod 8\), while \(\mathcal C\) is symmetric for \(d=0\pmod 8\)
and \(d=6\pmod 8\) and antisymmetric for \(d=2\pmod 8\) and
\(d=4\pmod 8\). Hence Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.21} requires the symmetry
properties
\begin{equation}
  g_{rs}^{\pm}=
  \begin{cases}
    g_{sr}^{\mp(-1)^{d/2}}, & d=0,2\pmod 8,\\
    -g_{sr}^{\mp(-1)^{d/2}}, & d=4,6\pmod 8,
  \end{cases}
  \tag{32.1.23}\label{eq:32.1.23}
\end{equation}
while Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.22} requires that
\begin{equation}
  z_{rs}^{\pm}=
  \begin{cases}
    z_{sr}^{\pm(-1)^{d/2}}, & d=0,6\pmod 8,\\
    -z_{sr}^{\pm(-1)^{d/2}}, & d=2,4\pmod 8.
  \end{cases}
  \tag{32.1.24}\label{eq:32.1.24}
\end{equation}
In particular, \(z^\pm\) is symmetric for \(d=0\pmod 8\), \(g^\pm\) is
symmetric for \(d=2\pmod 8\), \(z^\pm\) is antisymmetric for
\(d=4\pmod 8\), and \(g^\pm\) is antisymmetric for \(d=6\pmod 8\).

Taking the Hermitian adjoint of Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.20} and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.25} shows that
\(\mathcal C\beta Q_r^{\pm*}\) has the same Lorentz transformation
properties as the operators \(Q_s^{\mp(-1)^{d/2}}\) and is therefore a
linear combination of them:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^{\pm*}
  =\sum_s\mathcal S_{rs}^{\pm}Q_s^{\mp(-1)^{d/2}}\,.
  \tag{32.1.25}\label{eq:32.1.25}
\end{equation}
Taking the Hermitian adjoint of this equation and using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.28} and \eqref{eq:32.A.29} with \(d=2n\) gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal S^{\pm*}\mathcal S^{\mp(-)^{d/2}}
  =(-1)^a\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad a=d(d-2)/8\,.
  \tag{32.1.26}\label{eq:32.1.26}
\end{equation}
For \(d=0\pmod 8\) and \(d=4\pmod 8\)
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.25} relates one irreducible representation to another,
and we may choose bases in which \(\mathcal S^\pm=\mathbf{1}\) for
\(d=0\pmod 8\) and \(\mathcal S^\pm=\mp\mathbf{1}\) for
\(d=4\pmod 8\). For \(d=2\pmod 8\) Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.25} relates real
representations to themselves, and we may choose bases with
\(\mathcal S^\pm=\mathbf{1}\). For \(d=6\pmod 8\)
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.25} relates pseudoreal representations to themselves;
the determinant of Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.26} shows that there must be an even
number of \(Q^+\) and an even number (not necessarily the same!) of \(Q^-\),
and we may choose bases with \(\mathcal S^\pm=\Omega^\pm\), where
\(\Omega^\pm\) are standard real antisymmetric matrices of the form
\eqref{eq:32.1.16}.

We can deduce the reality and positivity properties of \(g_{rs}^{\pm}\)
and the reality properties of \(z_{rs}^{\pm}\) by using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.1.25} to rewrite the anticommutation relations
\eqref{eq:32.1.21} and \eqref{eq:32.1.22} in the form
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\pm\dagger}\}
  =
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \gamma_\lambda\beta P^\lambda
  \mathcal C(\mathcal C\beta)^{\mathrm T-1}
  (g^\pm\mathcal S^{\mp\mathrm T})_{rs}\,,
  \tag{32.1.27}\label{eq:32.1.27}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\mp\dagger}\}
  =
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \mathcal C(\mathcal C\beta)^{\mathrm T-1}
  (z^\pm\mathcal S^{\mp\mathrm T})_{rs}\,.
  \tag{32.1.28}\label{eq:32.1.28}
\end{equation}
We again use the relations
\(\mathcal C\mathcal C^{\mathrm T-1}=(-1)^{d(d+2)/8}\),
\(\beta^{\mathrm T}=-\beta\), and \(\gamma_0=i\beta\), and conclude that
\((-1)^{d(d+2)/8}g^\pm\mathcal S^{\pm\mathrm T}\) is Hermitian and
positive, while
\((z^+\mathcal S^{-\mathrm T})^\dagger=z^-\mathcal S^{+\mathrm T}\).
For \(d=0\pmod 8\) we can adopt a basis with
\(\mathcal S^\pm=\mathbf{1}\), \(g^\pm=\mathbf{1}\), and
\(z^{+\dagger}=z^-\); for \(d=2\pmod 8\) we can adopt a basis with
\(\mathcal S^\pm=\mathbf{1}\), \(g^\pm=-\mathbf{1}\), and
\(z^{+\dagger}=z^-\); for \(d=4\pmod 8\) we can adopt a basis with
\(\mathcal S^\pm=\mp\mathbf{1}\), \(g^\pm=\mp\mathbf{1}\), and
\(z^{+\dagger}=-z^-\); and for \(d=6\pmod 8\) we can adopt a basis with
\(\mathcal S^\pm=\Omega^\pm\), \(g^\pm=\Omega^\pm\), and
\((z^+\Omega^-)^\dagger=z^-\Omega^+\).

To summarize, in appropriate bases the anticommutation relations and reality
and symmetry conditions are as follows:~\hyperref[ch32-ref-6b]{[6b]}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=0\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\mp\mathrm T}\}
  =i\delta_{rs}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda\,,
  \tag{32.1.29}\label{eq:32.1.29}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\pm\mathrm T}\}
  =z_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.30}\label{eq:32.1.30}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^{\pm*}=Q_r^\mp\,,
  \qquad
  z_{rs}^{\pm}=z_{sr}^{\pm}=(z_{rs}^{\mp})^*\,.
  \tag{32.1.31}\label{eq:32.1.31}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=1\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r,Q_s^{\mathrm T}\}
  =i\delta_{rs}\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda+z_{rs}\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.32}\label{eq:32.1.32}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^*=Q_r\,,
  \qquad z_{rs}=z_{sr}=z_{rs}^*\,.
  \tag{32.1.33}\label{eq:32.1.33}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=2\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\mp\mathrm T}\}
  =-i\delta_{rs}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda\,,
  \tag{32.1.34}\label{eq:32.1.34}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\pm\mathrm T}\}
  =z_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.35}\label{eq:32.1.35}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^{\pm*}=Q_r^\pm\,,
  \qquad
  z_{rs}^{\pm}=-z_{rs}^{\pm*}=-z_{sr}^{\pm}\,.
  \tag{32.1.36}\label{eq:32.1.36}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=3\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r,Q_s^{\mathrm T}\}
  =i\delta_{rs}\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda+z_{rs}\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.37}\label{eq:32.1.37}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^*=Q_r\,,
  \qquad z_{rs}=-z_{sr}=-z_{rs}^*\,.
  \tag{32.1.38}\label{eq:32.1.38}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=4\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\mp\mathrm T}\}
  =\mp i\delta_{rs}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda\,,
  \tag{32.1.39}\label{eq:32.1.39}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\pm\mathrm T}\}
  =z_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.40}\label{eq:32.1.40}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^{\pm*}=\mp Q_r^\mp\,,
  \qquad
  z_{rs}^{\pm}=z_{rs}^{\mp*}=-z_{sr}^{\pm}\,.
  \tag{32.1.41}\label{eq:32.1.41}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=5\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r,Q_s^{\mathrm T}\}
  =-i\Omega_{rs}\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda
   +z_{rs}\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.42}\label{eq:32.1.42}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^*=\sum_s\Omega_{rs}Q_s\,,
  \qquad
  z_{rs}=-z_{sr}\,,
  \qquad
  z^*=-\Omega z\Omega\,.
  \tag{32.1.43}\label{eq:32.1.43}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=6\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\pm\mathrm T}\}
  =i\Omega_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)
  \gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda\,,
  \tag{32.1.44}\label{eq:32.1.44}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r^\pm,Q_s^{\mp\mathrm T}\}
  =z_{rs}^{\pm}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{d+1}}{2}\right)\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.45}\label{eq:32.1.45}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^{\pm*}
  =\sum_s\Omega_{rs}^{\pm}Q_s^\pm\,,
  \qquad
  z_{rs}^{\pm*}
  =\Omega^\mp z_{rs}^{\pm}\Omega^\pm
  =z_{sr}^{\mp}\,.
  \tag{32.1.46}\label{eq:32.1.46}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=7\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  \{Q_r,Q_s^{\mathrm T}\}
  =i\Omega_{rs}\gamma^\lambda\mathcal C P_\lambda
   +z_{rs}\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.1.47}\label{eq:32.1.47}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta Q_r^*=\sum_s\Omega_{rs}Q_s\,,
  \qquad
  z_{rs}=z_{sr}\,,
  \qquad
  z^*=+\Omega z\Omega\,.
  \tag{32.1.48}\label{eq:32.1.48}
\end{equation}

Inspection of these anticommutation relations reveals that in the absence
of central charges they are invariant under groups of linear transformations
on the fermionic generators, of the form
\(Q_r\longrightarrow\sum_sV_{rs}Q_s\) for \(d\) odd and
\(Q_r^\pm\longrightarrow\sum_sV_{rs}^\pm Q_s^\pm\) for \(d\) even. In
order to preserve the relations \eqref{eq:32.1.29}--\eqref{eq:32.1.48} it
is necessary that the \(V\)s should satisfy the conditions:

\medskip
\noindent \(d=0\) and \(d=4\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  V^\pm V^{\mp\mathrm T}=\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad V^{\pm*}=V^\mp\,.
  \tag{32.1.49}\label{eq:32.1.49}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=1\) and \(d=3\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  VV^{\mathrm T}=\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad V^*=V\,.
  \tag{32.1.50}\label{eq:32.1.50}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=2\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  V^\pm V^{\pm\mathrm T}=\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad V^\pm=V^{\pm*}\,.
  \tag{32.1.51}\label{eq:32.1.51}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=5\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  V\Omega V^{\mathrm T}=\Omega\,,
  \qquad V^*=-\Omega V\Omega\,.
  \tag{32.1.52}\label{eq:32.1.52}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=6\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  V^\pm V^{\pm\mathrm T}=\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad V^{\pm*}=-\Omega^\pm V^\pm\Omega^\pm\,.
  \tag{32.1.53}\label{eq:32.1.53}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent \(d=7\pmod 8\)
\begin{equation}
  V\Omega V^{\mathrm T}=\Omega\,,
  \qquad V^*=-\Omega V\Omega\,.
  \tag{32.1.54}\label{eq:32.1.54}
\end{equation}
These matrices form the groups:
\[
  \begin{array}{rcl}
    d=0\ \text{and}\ d=4\pmod 8
      &\qquad& U(N)\,,
    \\[4pt]
    d=1\ \text{and}\ d=3\pmod 8
      && O(N)\,,
    \\[4pt]
    d=2\pmod 8
      && O(N_+)\times O(N_-)\,,
    \\[4pt]
    d=5\ \text{and}\ d=7\pmod 8
      && USp(N)\,,\qquad N\ \text{even}\,,
    \\[4pt]
    d=6\pmod 8
      && USp(N_+)\times USp(N_-)\,,
         \qquad N_\pm\ \text{even}\,.
  \end{array}
\]
Here \(N\) is the number of fundamental spinor representations among the
\(Q\)s for \(d\) odd and the number of fundamental spinor representations
of each chirality among the \(Q\)s for \(d=0\pmod 8\) and
\(d=4\pmod 8\). For \(d=2\pmod 8\) and \(d=6\pmod 8\) the numbers of
fundamental spinor representations among the \(Q\)s need not be the same
for each chirality, and so these are denoted \(N_+\) and \(N_-\).
