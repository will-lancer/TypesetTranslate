\subsection{Direct Construction of Field Supermultiplets}

% Preserve the source's typographic distinction between two-component,
% upright Weyl supercharges and four-component, italic Majorana supercharges.
\providecommand{\WeylQ}{\mathrm{Q}}
\providecommand{\MajoranaQ}{Q}

To illustrate the direct construction of a field supermultiplet, we will
consider fields that can destroy the particles belonging to the simplest
supermultiplet of arbitrary mass discussed in Section 25.5: two spinless
particles and one particle of spin \(1/2\). We saw in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.5.15} that the one-particle zero spin state \(\ket{0,0}\) is
annihilated by the supersymmetry generator \(\WeylQ_a\) but not by
\(\WeylQ_a^*\), so we would expect the scalar field \(\phi(x)\) that creates
this particle from the vacuum (which is assumed to be annihilated by all
supersymmetry generators) to commute with \(\WeylQ_a\) but not with
\(\WeylQ_a^*\). That is,
\begin{equation}
  [\WeylQ_a,\phi(x)]=0\,,
  \tag{26.1.1}\label{eq:26.1.1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  -i\sum_b e_{ab}[\WeylQ_b^*,\phi(x)]
  \equiv\zeta_a(x)\ne0\,.
  \tag{26.1.2}\label{eq:26.1.2}
\end{equation}
The antisymmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(e_{ab}\) (with
\(e_{1/2,-1/2}\equiv+1\)) is introduced here because it is
\(\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_b^*\) that transforms under the homogeneous Lorentz
group according to the \((1/2,0)\) representation. It follows that
\(\zeta_a(x)\) is a two-component spinor field that also belongs to the
\((1/2,0)\) representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group.\footnote{At
this point we are not assuming anything about the masses or interactions of
the particles described by these fields, but it may be noted that, as
explained in Section 5.9, a \((1/2,0)\) free field can create massless
particles only of helicity \(+1/2\), in agreement with the result
\eqref{eq:25.5.15} that the massless spinless one-particle state
\(\ket{0,0}\) that is annihilated by \(\WeylQ_a\) is in a supermultiplet
with a state of helicity \(+1/2\).}

From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.1}--\eqref{eq:26.1.2} and the anticommutation
relation \eqref{eq:25.2.31}, we find
\[
  \{\WeylQ_b,\zeta_a\}
  =-i\sum_c e_{ac}[\{\WeylQ_b,\WeylQ_c^*\},\phi(x)]
  =2i(\sigma^\mu e)_{ba}[P_\mu,\phi]\,,
\]
and so
\begin{equation}
  \{\WeylQ_b,\zeta_a(x)\}
  =-2(\sigma^\mu e)_{ba}\partial_\mu\phi(x)\,.
  \tag{26.1.3}\label{eq:26.1.3}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.1.2} and the anticommutation relation
\eqref{eq:25.2.32} gives
\[
  -i\sum_c e_{ac}\{\WeylQ_b^*,\zeta_c\}
  =\{\WeylQ_b^*,[\WeylQ_a^*,\phi]\}
  =-\{\WeylQ_a^*,[\WeylQ_b^*,\phi]\}
  =i\sum_c e_{bc}\{\WeylQ_a^*,\zeta_c\}\,,
\]
so \(\sum_c e_{ac}\{\WeylQ_b^*,\zeta_c\}\) is antisymmetric, and therefore
proportional to the antisymmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(e_{ab}\):
\begin{equation}
  i\{\WeylQ_b^*,\zeta_a(x)\}=2\delta_{ab}\mathcal{F}(x)\,.
  \tag{26.1.4}\label{eq:26.1.4}
\end{equation}
Lorentz invariance requires that the coefficient \(\mathcal{F}(x)\) is a
scalar field.

We must now go one step further, and calculate the commutators of the
supersymmetry generators with \(\mathcal{F}(x)\). Using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.4}, \eqref{eq:26.1.2}, and \eqref{eq:25.2.32}, we have
\[
  \delta_{ab}[\WeylQ_c^*,\mathcal{F}]
  =\tfrac12 i[\WeylQ_c^*,\{\WeylQ_b^*,\zeta_a\}]
  =\tfrac12 i[\{\WeylQ_c^*,\zeta_a\},\WeylQ_b^*]
  =-\delta_{ac}[\WeylQ_b^*,\mathcal{F}]\,.
\]
By taking \(a=b\ne c\), we find that this commutator vanishes:
\begin{equation}
  [\WeylQ_c^*,\mathcal{F}(x)]=0\,.
  \tag{26.1.5}\label{eq:26.1.5}
\end{equation}

Finally, using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.4}, \eqref{eq:25.2.31}, and
\eqref{eq:26.1.3}, we have
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta_{ab}[\WeylQ_c,\mathcal{F}]
  &=\tfrac12 i[\WeylQ_c,\{\WeylQ_b^*,\zeta_a\}]
    =\tfrac12 i[\{\WeylQ_c,\WeylQ_b^*\},\zeta_a]
      -\tfrac12 i[\WeylQ_b^*,\{\WeylQ_c,\zeta_a\}]
  \\
  &=-\sigma^\mu_{cb}\partial_\mu\zeta_a
    +i(\sigma^\mu e)_{ca}[\WeylQ_b^*,\partial_\mu\phi]
  \\
  &=-\sigma^\mu_{cb}\partial_\mu\zeta_a
    +\sum_d e_{bd}(\sigma^\mu e)_{ca}\partial_\mu\zeta_d\,.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Contracting with \(\delta_{ab}\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
  [\WeylQ_c,\mathcal{F}(x)]
  =-\sum_a\sigma^\mu_{ca}\partial_\mu\zeta_a(x)\,.
  \tag{26.1.6}\label{eq:26.1.6}
\end{equation}

Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.1}--\eqref{eq:26.1.6} show that the fields
\(\phi(x)\), \(\zeta_a(x)\), and \(\mathcal{F}(x)\) furnish a complete
representation of the supersymmetry algebra. These fields are not Hermitian,
so their complex conjugates furnish another supermultiplet:
\begin{equation}
  [\WeylQ_a^*,\phi^*(x)]=0\,,
  \tag{26.1.7}\label{eq:26.1.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  -i\sum_b e_{ab}[\WeylQ_b,\phi^*(x)]=\zeta_a^*(x)\,,
  \tag{26.1.8}\label{eq:26.1.8}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \{\WeylQ_b^*,\zeta_a^*(x)\}
  =2(e\sigma^\mu)_{ab}\partial_\mu\phi^*(x)\,,
  \tag{26.1.9}\label{eq:26.1.9}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  -i\{\WeylQ_b,\zeta_a^*(x)\}
  =2\delta_{ab}\mathcal{F}^*(x)\,,
  \tag{26.1.10}\label{eq:26.1.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  [\WeylQ_c,\mathcal{F}^*(x)]=0\,,
  \tag{26.1.11}\label{eq:26.1.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  [\WeylQ_c^*,\mathcal{F}^*(x)]
  =\sum_a\sigma^\mu_{ac}\partial_\mu\zeta_a^*(x)\,.
  \tag{26.1.12}\label{eq:26.1.12}
\end{equation}

We can express these commutation and anticommutation relations as
transformation rules under a supersymmetry transformation, which shifts any
bosonic or fermionic field operator \(\mathcal{O}(x)\) by the infinitesimal
amount
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mathcal{O}(x)
  \equiv
  \left[\sum_a\left(\epsilon_a^*\WeylQ_a
    +\epsilon_a\WeylQ_a^*\right),\mathcal{O}(x)\right]\,,
  \tag{26.1.13}\label{eq:26.1.13}
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon_a\) is an infinitesimal fermionic c-number spinor. (Because
\(\epsilon_a\) and \(\epsilon_a^*\) anticommute with \(\WeylQ_a\) and
\(\WeylQ_a^*\), the quantity
\(\epsilon_a^*\WeylQ_a+\epsilon_a\WeylQ_a^*\) is anti-Hermitian, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.1.13} gives
\((\delta\mathcal{O})^\dagger=\delta\mathcal{O}^\dagger\).) The commutation
and anticommutation rules \eqref{eq:26.1.1}--\eqref{eq:26.1.6} are
equivalent to the transformation rules
\begin{equation}
  \delta\phi(x)
  =-i\sum_{ab}\epsilon_a e_{ab}\zeta_b(x)\,,
  \tag{26.1.14}\label{eq:26.1.14}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta\zeta_a(x)
  =-2\sum_b\epsilon_b^*(\sigma^\mu e)_{ba}\partial_\mu\phi(x)
    -2i\epsilon_a\mathcal{F}(x)\,,
  \tag{26.1.15}\label{eq:26.1.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mathcal{F}(x)
  =-\sum_{ab}\epsilon_b^*\sigma^\mu_{ba}\partial_\mu\zeta_a(x)\,.
  \tag{26.1.16}\label{eq:26.1.16}
\end{equation}

This may be put into a four-component Dirac notation by introducing an
infinitesimal, Majorana\footnote{With the phase convention we will be using
here, a Majorana four-component spinor is formed from a \((1/2,0)\)
two-component spinor \(u_a\) as
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}u\\-eu^*\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.1.17} fits this definition, with \(u=-i\epsilon\).
Equivalently, a Majorana spinor can be formed from a two-component
\((0,1/2)\) spinor \(v_a\) as
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}ev^*\\v\end{pmatrix}.
\]
An example is provided by Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.34}. Properties of Majorana
spinors are considered in detail in the appendix to this chapter.},
four-component, spinor transformation parameter
\begin{equation}
  \alpha\equiv-i
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \epsilon_a\\
    \displaystyle\sum_b e_{ab}\epsilon_b^*
  \end{pmatrix},
  \tag{26.1.17}\label{eq:26.1.17}
\end{equation}
so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.1.13} becomes
\begin{equation}
  i\delta\mathcal{O}(x)\equiv
  [\bar{\alpha}\MajoranaQ,\mathcal{O}(x)]\,.
  \tag{26.1.18}\label{eq:26.1.18}
\end{equation}

The transformation rules \eqref{eq:26.1.14}--\eqref{eq:26.1.16} and their
complex conjugates may be put into a convenient covariant form by introducing
a set of real bosonic fields \(A\), \(B\), \(F\), and \(G\), defined by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{A+iB}{\sqrt{2}}\equiv\phi\,,
  \qquad
  \frac{F-iG}{\sqrt{2}}\equiv\mathcal{F}\,,
  \tag{26.1.19}\label{eq:26.1.19}
\end{equation}
and a four-component Majorana spinor \(\psi\), defined by
\begin{equation}
  \psi\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \zeta_a\\
    \displaystyle-\sum_b e_{ab}\zeta_b^*
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{26.1.20}\label{eq:26.1.20}
\end{equation}
Let's also recall the relation between the \(4\times4\) Dirac matrices and the
\(2\times2\) matrices \(\sigma_\mu\):
\[
  \gamma_\mu=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&-ie\sigma_\mu^T e\\
    i\sigma_\mu&0
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The transformation rules now take the form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \delta A&=\bar{\alpha}\psi\,,
    &\qquad
    \delta B&=-i\bar{\alpha}\gamma_5\psi\,,
    \\
    \delta\psi
    &=\partial_\mu(A+i\gamma_5B)\gamma^\mu\alpha
      +(F-i\gamma_5G)\alpha\,,
    \\
    \delta F&=\bar{\alpha}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi\,,
    &\qquad
    \delta G&=-i\bar{\alpha}\gamma_5\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi\,.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.1.21}\label{eq:26.1.21}
\end{equation}
A direct but tedious calculation shows that this transformation leaves
invariant the action
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  I=\int d^4x\biggl\{
    &-\frac12\partial_\mu A\,\partial^\mu A
     -\frac12\partial_\mu B\,\partial^\mu B
     -\frac12\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi
  \\
    &+\frac12(F^2+G^2)
     +m\left[FA+GB-\frac12\bar{\psi}\psi\right]
  \\
    &+g\left[F(A^2+B^2)+2GAB
      -\bar{\psi}(A+i\gamma_5B)\psi\right]\biggr\}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.1.22}\label{eq:26.1.22}
\end{equation}
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.21} and \eqref{eq:26.1.22} agree with the transformation
rules \eqref{eq:24.2.8} and Lagrangian density \eqref{eq:24.2.9} found in the
original work of Wess and Zumino. In the next three sections we will explore a
convenient technique for checking the supersymmetry of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.1.22} and for deriving more general supersymmetric actions.

Where the fermion field \(\psi(x)\) satisfies the free-field Dirac equation
\((\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu+m)\psi=0\), these transformation rules show that
\(F+mA\) and \(G+mB\) are invariant, and therefore commute with
\(\WeylQ_a\) and \(\WeylQ_a^*\), and hence also with \(P_\mu\). This does not
prove that \(F=-mA\) and \(G=-mB\), but without changing any of the
commutation and anticommutation rules
\eqref{eq:26.1.1}--\eqref{eq:26.1.6} or transformation rules
\eqref{eq:26.1.21}, we can redefine the fields \(F\) and \(G\) by subtracting
the constants \(F+mA\) and \(B+mG\), respectively, so that the new fields
\(F\) and \(G\) are given by \(F=-mA\) and \(G=-mB\), and therefore
\(\mathcal{F}=-m\phi^*\). This is not true in the presence of interactions,
but even in the interacting case \(\mathcal{F}(x)\), \(F(x)\), and \(G(x)\)
are typically auxiliary fields that can be expressed in terms of the other
fields of the supermultiplet, as is the case for the action
\eqref{eq:26.1.22}.
