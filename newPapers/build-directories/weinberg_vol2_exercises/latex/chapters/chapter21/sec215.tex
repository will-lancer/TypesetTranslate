\subsection{Electroweak--Strong Unification}
\label{sec:21.5}

We saw in \hyperref[sec:15.2]{Section 15.2} that a gauge theory will have an independent coupling constant for each simple or $U(1)$ subgroup of the gauge group. Thus the electroweak theory which is based on the gauge group $SU(2)\times U(1)$, has two independent couplings, $g$ and $g'$. In order to reduce the number of free parameters it was suggested~\hyperref[ch21-ref-32]{[32]} that the $SU(2)\times U(1)$ gauge group might be embedded in a simple $SU(3)$ gauge group, which would give $g'=g/\sqrt{3}$, but this has been ruled out experimentally. After the advent of quantum chromodynamics, theorists confronted a gauge group $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$, which has three independent gauge coupling constants: the coupling $g_s$ of quantum chromodynamics, and the couplings $g$ and $g'$ of the electroweak interactions. To reduce these to just a single free parameter, it was proposed to embed $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ in various simple Lie groups:\footnote{The group $SU(4)\times SU(4)$ is made simple by inclusion of a discrete symmetry operator that interchanges the two $SU(4)$s.} $SU(4)\times SU(4)$~\hyperref[ch21-ref-33]{[33]}, $SU(5)$~\hyperref[ch21-ref-34]{[34]}, or $SO(10)$~\hyperref[ch21-ref-35]{[35]}. Such models are often known as grand-unified theories.

Fortunately, the consequences of these and a large class of other models for the ratios of the $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ coupling constants are independent of the details of the individual models~\hyperref[ch21-ref-36]{[36]}. This class of models is characterized by the fact that the observed generations of quarks and leptons are the only fermions in the models, or at least the only fermions that are not neutral under $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$.

As shown in \hyperref[sec:15.2]{Section 15.2}, for any simple compact Lie group there is a conventional choice of generators $T_\alpha$ with totally antisymmetric structure constants, which in each reducible or irreducible representation $D$ satisfy the normalization condition:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Tr}\{T_\alpha T_\beta\}=N_D\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
\tag{21.5.1}\label{eq:21.5.1}
\end{equation}
We are assuming that all left-handed fermions form $n_g$ generations:
\[
\begin{array}{cccccc}
\begin{pmatrix}\nu_e\\ e\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},
&
\begin{pmatrix}\nu_\mu\\ \mu\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},
&
\begin{pmatrix}\nu_\tau\\ \tau\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},
&
\ldots\,;
\\[7pt]
\overline{e_R},
&
\overline{\mu_R},
&
\overline{\tau_R},
&
\ldots\,;
\\[7pt]
\begin{pmatrix}u\\ d\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},
&
\begin{pmatrix}c\\ s\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},
&
\begin{pmatrix}t\\ b\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},
&
\ldots\,;
\\[7pt]
\overline{u_R},
&
\overline{c_R},
&
\overline{t_R},
&
\ldots\,;
\\[7pt]
\overline{d_R},
&
\overline{s_R},
&
\overline{b_R},
&
\ldots\,.
\end{array}
\]
The $SU(3)$ generator $\frac{1}{2}g_s\lambda_3$ has eigenvalues: $+\frac{1}{2}g_s$ for the red quark doublets and the white antiquark singlets; $-\frac{1}{2}g_s$ for the white quark doublets and the red antiquark singlets; and zero for all other left-handed fermions; so its square has the trace
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{1}{2}g_s\lambda_3\right)^2
=4n_g\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}g_s\right)^2
+4n_g\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}g_s\right)^2
=2n_gg_s^2.
\tag{21.5.2}\label{eq:21.5.2}
\end{equation}
The $SU(2)$ generator $t_3$ has eigenvalues: $\frac{1}{2}g$ for the red, white, and blue quarks of charge $\frac{2}{3}$ and the neutrinos; $-\frac{1}{2}g$ for the red, white, and blue quarks of charge $-\frac{1}{3}$ and the charged leptons; and zero for all other fermions, so its square has the trace
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Tr}(t_3)^2
=[3n_g+n_g]\cdot\left[
\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)^2
+\left(-\frac{1}{2}g\right)^2
\right]
=2n_gg^2.
\tag{21.5.3}\label{eq:21.5.3}
\end{equation}
Finally, the $U(1)$ generator $y=t_3-q$ has eigenvalues: $\frac{1}{2}g'$ for neutrinos and charged leptons; $-g'$ for charged antileptons; $-\frac{1}{6}g'$ for quarks; $\frac{2}{3}g'$ for antiquarks of charge $-\frac{2}{3}$; and $-\frac{1}{3}g'$ for antiquarks of charge $+\frac{1}{3}$, so its square has the trace
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Tr}y^2
={}&2n_g\left(\frac{1}{2}g'\right)^2
+n_g(-g')^2
+6n_g\left(-\frac{1}{6}g'\right)^2
\notag\\
&+3n_g\left(\frac{2}{3}g'\right)^2
+3n_g\left(-\frac{1}{3}g'\right)^2
\notag\\
={}&\frac{10}{3}n_gg'^2.
\tag{21.5.4}\label{eq:21.5.4}
\end{align}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.1} requires the traces \eqref{eq:21.5.2}--\eqref{eq:21.5.4} to be all equal, so in this class of models the embedding of $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ in a simple Lie group imposes the coupling constant relations
\begin{equation}
g_s^2=g^2=\frac{5}{3}g'^2.
\tag{21.5.5}\label{eq:21.5.5}
\end{equation}

Now, Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.5} is in gross disagreement with the observed values of the coupling constants. The ratio $g'^2/g^2=\frac{3}{5}$ implies an electroweak mixing angle with $\sin^2\theta=g'^2/(g^2+g'^2)=\frac{3}{8}$, while the experimental value is $\sin^2\theta=0.231$. Even worse, the strong coupling $g_s^2$ is of course much larger than $g^2$ or $g'^2$.

The solution~\hyperref[ch21-ref-36]{[36]} to this problem is that coupling-constant relations like Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.5} apply only to the couplings measured at an energy scale comparable to the typical mass~\hyperref[ch21-ref-47]{[47]} $M$ of the gauge bosons that become massive in the spontaneous breakdown of the simple gauge group to $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$. If the energy $E$ at which the couplings are measured is very much less than $M$, then there will be large radiative corrections proportional to $\ln(M/E)$.

As emphasized in Chapter 18, there are no large logarithms in the relation between couplings measured at nearby energies $\mu$ and $\mu-d\mu$, so by integrating this relation from $M$ down to $E$ we can calculate the couplings at energies $E\ll M$ without encountering large logarithms. For this to be done it is only necessary that the couplings should stay small over this whole range. For the $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ couplings with $n_g$ fermion generations, Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.7.2} gives\footnote{The second term in the brackets in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.5.6} and \eqref{eq:21.5.7} is given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:18.7.2} and \eqref{eq:18.7.3} as $-n_f/6$, where $n_f$ are the numbers of fermions in the defining representation of the respective $SU(N)$ gauge groups. But this was calculated under the assumption that left- and right-handed fermions are in the same representation of the gauge group. If we count only left-handed fermions (and antifermions), then the second term in the brackets in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.5.6} and \eqref{eq:21.5.7} should be $-n_f/12$. For $SU(3)$ there are two left-handed quark triplets and two left-handed antiquark triplets per generation, so $n_f=4n_g$, while for $SU(2)$ there are three left-handed quark doublets and one left-handed lepton doublet per generation, so again $n_f=4n_g$. For $U(1)$, the beta-function is $g'^3/24\pi^2$ times the sum of the squares of the $U(1)$ charges for left-handed fermions and antifermions (compare Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.2.38}), which according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.4} is $(g'/24\pi^2)\cdot(10n_gg'^2/3)$.}
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}g_s(\mu)
=-\frac{g_s^3(\mu)}{4\pi^2}
\left(\frac{11}{4}-\frac{n_g}{3}\right),
\tag{21.5.6}\label{eq:21.5.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}g(\mu)
=-\frac{g^3(\mu)}{4\pi^2}
\left(\frac{11}{6}-\frac{n_g}{3}\right),
\tag{21.5.7}\label{eq:21.5.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}g'(\mu)
=-\frac{g'^3(\mu)}{4\pi^2}
\left(-\frac{5n_g}{9}\right).
\tag{21.5.8}\label{eq:21.5.8}
\end{equation}
The solutions of these equations are
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{g_s^2(\mu)}
=\frac{1}{g_s^2(M)}
-\frac{1}{8\pi^2}
\left(11-\frac{4n_g}{3}\right)
\ln\left(\frac{M}{\mu}\right),
\tag{21.5.9}\label{eq:21.5.9}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{g^2(\mu)}
=\frac{1}{g^2(M)}
-\frac{1}{8\pi^2}
\left(\frac{22}{3}-\frac{4n_g}{3}\right)
\ln\left(\frac{M}{\mu}\right),
\tag{21.5.10}\label{eq:21.5.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{g'^2(\mu)}
=\frac{1}{g'^2(M)}
-\frac{1}{8\pi^2}
\left(-\frac{20n_g}{9}\right)
\ln\left(\frac{M}{\mu}\right).
\tag{21.5.11}\label{eq:21.5.11}
\end{equation}
Also, Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.5} should be interpreted to mean that
\begin{equation}
g_s^2(M)=g^2(M)=\frac{5}{3}g'^2(M).
\tag{21.5.12}\label{eq:21.5.12}
\end{equation}
We can therefore eliminate the couplings \eqref{eq:21.5.12} \emph{and} the number of generations by subtracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.10} from Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.9}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{g_s^2(\mu)}-\frac{1}{g^2(\mu)}
=-\frac{11}{24\pi^2}\ln\left(\frac{M}{\mu}\right)
\tag{21.5.13}\label{eq:21.5.13}
\end{equation}
and by subtracting $3/5$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.11} from Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.5.10}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{g^2(\mu)}-\frac{3}{5g'^2(\mu)}
=-\frac{11}{12\pi^2}\ln\left(\frac{M}{\mu}\right).
\tag{21.5.14}\label{eq:21.5.14}
\end{equation}
Taking the ratio of these two equations gives a formula for $\sin^2\theta\equiv g'^2/(g^2+g'^2)$:
\begin{equation}
\sin^2\theta
=\frac{1}{6}
+\frac{5e^2(m_Z)}{9g_s^2(m_Z)}.
\tag{21.5.15}\label{eq:21.5.15}
\end{equation}

In this formula we have set $\mu$ equal to the typical energy of the processes used to measure $\sin^2\theta$, that is, $\mu\approx m_Z$. This has the advantage that we will be using the renormalization group equations \eqref{eq:21.5.6}--\eqref{eq:21.5.8} only above $m_Z$, where they are not strongly affected by the spontaneous breakdown of $SU(2)\times U(1)$. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.5.13} and \eqref{eq:21.5.14} can also be combined to give a formula for the unification scale $M$:
\begin{equation}
\ln\left(\frac{M}{m_Z}\right)
=\frac{4\pi^2}{11e^2}
\left(1-\frac{8e^2(m_Z)}{3g_s^2(m_Z)}\right),
\tag{21.5.16}\label{eq:21.5.16}
\end{equation}
where again, to avoid the effects of electroweak symmetry breaking on the renormalization group equations, we have taken $\mu$ as of order $m_Z$.

We saw in \hyperref[sec:18.2]{Section 18.2} that the value of $e(\mu)$ at $\mu\approx m_Z$ is given by $e(m_Z)^2/4\pi=(128.87\pm0.12)^{-1}$. This is the charge defined in the conventional (Gell-Mann--Low) manner, in terms of the vacuum polarization. For purposes of comparison with $g_s$, $g'$, and $g$, it is better to use the coupling defined (as in \hyperref[sec:18.6]{Section 18.6}) by modified minimum subtraction: $e(m_Z)^2/4\pi=(127.9\pm0.1)^{-1}$. The greatest uncertainty in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.5.13} and \eqref{eq:21.5.14} is in the value of $g_s^2(m_Z)$. As discussed in \hyperref[sec:18.7]{Section 18.7}, extrapolation of $g_s$ from lower energy data gives $g_s^2(m_Z)/4\pi=0.118\pm0.006$, while direct measurement from the rate of $Z^0$ decay into hadrons gives $g_s^2(m_Z)/4\pi=0.120\pm0.0025$. For $g_s^2(m_Z)/4\pi=0.118$ and $e(m_Z)^2/4\pi=1/128$, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:21.5.15} and \eqref{eq:21.5.16} give $\sin^2\theta=0.203$ and $M\approx1.1\cdot10^{15}$ GeV.

As mentioned in \hyperref[sec:21.3]{Section 21.3}, there is no reason to expect that baryon and lepton number should be conserved by the suppressed non-renormalizable terms in the effective Lagrangian that describes physics at ordinary energies, so we may expect the presence of an $(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1))$-conserving four fermion (three quarks and a lepton) interaction, with a coefficient that on dimensional grounds would be of order $M^{-2}$. The proton lifetime was first estimated on this basis, and found to be of order $10^{32}$ years~\hyperref[ch21-ref-36]{[36]}. Such baryon- and lepton-non-conserving four-fermion interactions are produced in models like those of Refs.~\hyperref[ch21-ref-33]{[33]}--\hyperref[ch21-ref-35]{[35]} from the exchange of gauge bosons with masses of order $M$. More generally, once the standard model explained why baryon- and lepton-non-conserving processes are naturally suppressed, there ceased to be any reason to believe that either baryon or lepton number are exactly conserved.

We have seen that the prediction \eqref{eq:21.5.15} comes quite close to the measured value 0.23 of $\sin^2\theta$, but the accuracy of the measurements and calculations has become good enough to make clear that they are not in precise agreement. The extra particles in supersymmetric theories appear to remove this discrepancy, and lead to a value of $M\approx2\cdot10^{16}$ GeV, about an order of magnitude larger~\hyperref[ch21-ref-36a]{[36a]}. It is very interesting that this $M$ is not so very different from the energy $10^{18}$ GeV at which the gravitational interactions become strong. The larger value of $M$ also has the effect of increasing the proton lifetime, which is proportional to $M^4$.
