\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The $\pi^0$ Decay Problem}{The pi0 Decay Problem}}
\label{sec:22.1}

By the mid-1960s the picture of the pion as a Goldstone boson associated
with a spontaneously broken $SU(2)\otimes SU(2)$ symmetry of the strong
interactions had scored a number of successes, outlined here in Chapter 19.
However, this picture also had a few outstanding failures. The most
disturbing had to do with the rate of the dominant decay mode of the neutral
pion, $\pi^0\to2\gamma$. It was the solution of this problem that led to the
discovery of symmetry-breaking anomalies.

After integrating out all heavy and trapped particles, we would expect the
effective Lagrangian for $\pi^0\to2\gamma$ to be given by the unique gauge-
and Lorentz-invariant term with no more than two derivatives:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\pi\gamma\gamma}
 =g\pi^0\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\rho}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\lambda},
 \tag{22.1.1}\label{eq:22.1.1}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is an unknown constant with the dimensions $[\mathit{mass}]^{-1}$.
The methods of Section 3.4 can then be used to calculate the rate for
$\pi^0\to2\gamma$
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma(\pi^0\to2\gamma)=\frac{m_\pi^3g^2}{\pi}.
 \tag{22.1.2}\label{eq:22.1.2}
\end{equation}
One might naively expect $g$ to be of order
\begin{equation}
 g\approx\frac{e^2}{8\pi^2F_\pi},
 \tag{22.1.3}\label{eq:22.1.3}
\end{equation}
where $F_\pi\simeq190\ \mathrm{MeV}$ is used as a typical strong interaction
mass scale, and a factor $1/8\pi^2$ is inserted because the graphs
responsible for $\pi^0\to2\gamma$ would have to include at least one loop.
For instance, in 1949, using the pre-QCD theory of pions and nucleons with
interaction Lagrangian
$iG_{\pi N}\vec\pi\cdot\overline N\,2\vec t\gamma_5N$,
Steinberger~\hyperref[ch22-ref-1]{[1]} calculated that the contribution to
$g$ from triangle graphs with a single proton loop is
\begin{equation}
 g_\triangle=\frac{e^2G_{\pi N}}{32\pi^2m_N}.
 \tag{22.1.4}\label{eq:22.1.4}
\end{equation}
This is numerically not very different from Eq.~\eqref{eq:22.1.3}, because
the Goldberger--Treiman relation (see Section 19.4) gives
$G_{\pi N}=2m_Ng_A/F_\pi$.

This estimate of the amplitude for $\pi^0\to2\gamma$ leaves out the special
constraints imposed by the $SU(2)\otimes SU(2)$ symmetry. The electromagnetic
interaction violates most of this symmetry, but at least formally it has no
effect on the $U(1)\cdot U(1)$ subgroup generated by the electrically
neutral generators of $SU(2)\otimes SU(2)$. Acting on quarks, the
infinitesimal pseudoscalar element of this subgroup has the effect:
\begin{equation}
 \delta u=i\epsilon\gamma_5u,\qquad
 \delta d=-i\epsilon\gamma_5d,
 \tag{22.1.5}\label{eq:22.1.5}
\end{equation}
so that the electric current is invariant:
\begin{equation}
 \delta\left[
  \frac{2}{3}\overline u\gamma^\mu u
  -\frac{1}{3}\overline d\gamma^\mu d
 \right]=0.
 \tag{22.1.6}\label{eq:22.1.6}
\end{equation}
(This argument was made by Sutherland~\hyperref[ch22-ref-2]{[2]} and
Veltman~\hyperref[ch22-ref-3]{[3]} before the advent of quantum
chromodynamics, with the proton and neutron in place of the $u$ and $d$
quarks.) Because the $\pi^0$ is the Goldstone boson associated with this
symmetry, a non-derivative interaction like \eqref{eq:22.1.1} can arise only
from the breaking of this symmetry by quark masses, and so must be
proportional to $m_\pi^2\propto m_u+m_d$. With this in mind, we would expect
the constant $g$ to be suppressed~\hyperref[ch22-ref-2]{[2]},
\hyperref[ch22-ref-3]{[3]} by an extra factor $m_\pi^2/m_N^2$:
\begin{equation}
 g\approx\frac{e^2}{8\pi^2F_\pi}
 \left(\frac{m_\pi^2}{m_N^2}\right).
 \tag{22.1.7}\label{eq:22.1.7}
\end{equation}
(In place of $m_N$ we might have used the chiral-symmetry-breaking scale
$2\pi F_\pi=1200\ \mathrm{MeV}$ discussed in Section 19.3, with little
change in our results). There are also chiral-invariant effective
$\pi^0\gamma\gamma$ interactions involving the derivative of the $\pi^0$
field. Lorentz invariance requires that these involve at least two
additional derivatives. Using the homogeneous Maxwell equation
$\partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda}-\partial_\nu F_{\mu\lambda}
=-\partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu}$ and integrating by parts, we see that there
is only one independent chiral-invariant coupling with just two extra
derivatives, given by inserting a d'Alembertian acting on the $\pi^0$ field
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:22.1.1}. On the pion mass shell this is just the same as an
interaction \eqref{eq:22.1.1} with an extra factor $m_\pi^2$, leading to the
same estimate \eqref{eq:22.1.7}. It was sometimes said that chiral symmetry
would forbid the decay $\pi^0\to2\gamma$, but it is more accurate to say
that chiral symmetry would make the rate for this process go as $m_\pi^7$
instead of $m_\pi^3$ for $m_\pi\to0$.

The difficulty is that the observed rate for $\pi^0\to2\gamma$ is much
larger than would be expected from Eq.~\eqref{eq:22.1.7}, and is in fact much
closer to what would be inferred from the naive result
Eq.~\eqref{eq:22.1.3}. To be specific, from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:22.1.7} and
\eqref{eq:22.1.2} we would expect a decay rate
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma(\pi^0\to2\gamma)
 \approx\frac{m_\pi^7\alpha^2}{4\pi^3F_\pi^2m_N^4}
 =1.9\cdot10^{13}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1},
 \tag{22.1.8}\label{eq:22.1.8}
\end{equation}
while using Eq.~\eqref{eq:22.1.3} in place of Eq.~\eqref{eq:22.1.7} would
give
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma(\pi^0\to2\gamma)
 \approx\frac{m_\pi^3\alpha^2}{4\pi^3F_\pi^2}
 =4.4\cdot10^{16}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}.
 \tag{22.1.9}\label{eq:22.1.9}
\end{equation}
The observed rate is
$\Gamma(\pi^0\to2\gamma)=(1.19\pm0.08)\cdot10^{16}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$,
which is in satisfactory agreement with the naive rough estimate
\eqref{eq:22.1.9}, and almost three orders of magnitude larger than the
`improved' result \eqref{eq:22.1.8}! One is forced to the conclusion that
something anomalous here is invalidating the chiral symmetry that led us to
introduce the additional factor $m_\pi^2/m_N^2$ in $g$. Similar problems
arise in trying to understand the rates of some other processes, such as
$\eta^0\to\gamma+\gamma$.

In 1969 the source of this anomaly was traced by Bell and
Jackiw~\hyperref[ch22-ref-4]{[4]} to the violation of chiral symmetry by the
regulator that is needed in order to derive consequences of the conservation
of the neutral axial vector current for one-loop Feynman diagrams. Their
result was confirmed, generalized, and extended to higher orders by
Adler~\hyperref[ch22-ref-5]{[5]}. It was subsequently realized in 1979 by
Fujikawa~\hyperref[ch22-ref-6]{[6]} that in the path-integral formulation of
field theory the chiral-symmetry-breaking anomaly enters only in the measure
used to define the path integral over fermion fields. As we shall see in the
next section, this approach makes it simple to evaluate the amplitude for
$\pi^0\to2\gamma$ produced by this anomaly to all orders of perturbation
theory. After that, we shall return to the direct calculation of anomalies in
more general theories, and discuss various applications.
