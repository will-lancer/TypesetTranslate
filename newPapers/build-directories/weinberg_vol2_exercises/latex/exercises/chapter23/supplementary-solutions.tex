\begin{enumerate}
  \SupplementarySolution{1}{Topology, Defect Codimension, and Causal Formation}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)]
    Under the stated fixed boundary condition, all directions at
    \(\lvert\mathbf x\rvert\to\infty\) represent the same chosen vacuum.
    Adjoining one point at infinity therefore gives the one-point
    compactification
    \[
      \mathbb R^d\cup\{\infty\}\simeq S^d,
    \]
    and the configuration is a based map
    \(\Phi:S^d\to\mathcal M\), with \(\Phi(\infty)\) the chosen vacuum.
    A continuous path through finite-energy nonsingular configurations is
    exactly a based homotopy, so it cannot change the class
    \([\Phi]\in\pi_d(\mathcal M)\).

    The class can change only if one of the hypotheses fails: the field
    may violate the fixed boundary data, leave the restricted space of
    maps into \(\mathcal M\), or become singular.  Each possibility exits
    the configuration space whose connected components are being
    classified.  This is the origin of topological stability.
    \item[(b)]
    Take a small \(q\)-dimensional ball transverse to the defect.  Its
    boundary links the core and is \(S^{q-1}\).  Away from the core the
    field lies in \(\mathcal M\), so its restriction to the linking sphere
    is a map
    \[
      S^{q-1}\longrightarrow\mathcal M.
    \]
    The defect is topologically nontrivial precisely when this map is a
    nonzero element of \(\pi_{q-1}(\mathcal M)\).

    In three dimensions the possibilities are
    \[
    \begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{defect}&q&\text{classification}\\ \hline
      \text{domain wall}&1&\pi_0(\mathcal M)\\
      \text{string}&2&\pi_1(\mathcal M)\\
      \text{monopole}&3&\pi_2(\mathcal M).
    \end{array}
    \]
    Two disconnected vacua have nontrivial \(\pi_0\) and permit walls.
    Since \(\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb Z\), an \(S^1\) vacuum permits strings.
    Since \(\pi_2(S^2)=\mathbb Z\), an \(S^2\) vacuum permits monopoles.
    The other two entries in each of these simple examples are trivial.
    \item[(c)]
    Finite energy requires the two limits
    \(\phi(+\infty)\) and \(\phi(-\infty)\) independently to belong to
    \(\{-v,+v\}\).  A convenient orientation-sensitive charge is
    \[
      Q=\frac{\phi(+\infty)-\phi(-\infty)}{2v}.
    \]
    Opposite signs describe a wall or antiwall with \(Q=\pm1\), whereas
    equal signs give the topologically trivial sector \(Q=0\).  For the
    wall with \(Q=1\), complete the square:
    \[
    \begin{split}
      E={}&\frac12\int \dd x\,
        \left[\phi'
         -\sqrt{\frac\lambda2}(v^2-\phi^2)\right]^2\\
        &+\sqrt{\frac\lambda2}
         \int_{\phi(-\infty)}^{\phi(+\infty)}
          \dd\phi\,(v^2-\phi^2).
    \end{split}
    \]
    For \(Q=1\), the last integral gives the bound
    \[
      E\ge \frac{2\sqrt2}{3}\sqrt\lambda\,v^3.
    \]
    Saturation requires
    \(\phi'=\sqrt{\lambda/2}(v^2-\phi^2)\), whose solution is
    \[
      \boxed{\phi(x)=v\tanh\left[
        v\sqrt{\frac\lambda2}(x-x_0)\right],\qquad
        \sigma=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}\sqrt\lambda\,v^3.}
    \]
    Reversing \(x-x_0\) gives the antiwall and reverses \(Q\) without
    changing the tension.
    \item[(d)]
    Single-valuedness gives
    \(\Phi(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi(\varphi)\), hence
    \(e^{2\pi \ii n}=1\) and \(n\in\mathbb Z\).  Equivalently,
    \[
      n=\frac1{2\pi \ii}\oint\frac{\dd\Phi}{\Phi}
    \]
    is the degree of the map from a linking circle to the vacuum circle.
    It can change continuously only if \(\Phi=0\) somewhere on that
    circle or the boundary condition fails.

    With the conventional complex-scalar gradient energy
    \(E=\int \dd^2x\,\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2\),
    \(\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2=v^2n^2/r^2\) outside the core.  Thus
    \[
      \frac EL
        \simeq 2\pi v^2n^2
         \int_{r_c}^{L}\frac{\dd r}{r}
        =2\pi v^2n^2\ln\frac{L}{r_c}.
    \]
    A convention with an additional \(1/2\) in the kinetic term halves
    this coefficient.  The logarithm reflects the \(1/r\) angular
    gradient persisting arbitrarily far from a global string.
    \item[(e)]
    A correlation domain has volume of order \(\xi^3\).  Neighboring
    domains may choose different points of \(\mathcal M\), and the field
    then interpolates between them.  Around a junction, the interpolating
    values can define a map from a linking sphere into \(\mathcal M\).
    If that map represents a nontrivial homotopy class, no smooth
    deformation confined to \(\mathcal M\) can fill the linked region.
    The order parameter must leave \(\mathcal M\) in a core, trapping a
    defect.

    Dimensional counting gives the initial densities.  A correlation
    cell can contain wall area \(O(\xi^2)\), string length \(O(\xi)\), or
    \(O(1)\) monopole cores.  Dividing by its volume gives
    \[
      \boxed{\frac AV\sim p_{\mathrm w}\xi^{-1},\qquad
        \frac LV\sim p_{\mathrm s}\xi^{-2},\qquad
        \frac{N_{\mathrm m}}V\sim p_{\mathrm m}\xi^{-3}.}
    \]
    The dimensionless probabilities \(p_{\mathrm w},p_{\mathrm s}\), and
    \(p_{\mathrm m}\) depend on the vacuum manifold, interpolation rule,
    and correlations.  The powers of \(\xi\) follow solely from the
    dimensions of walls, strings, and point defects.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{2}{Derrick Scaling and \(\phi^6\) Kinks}
  Put
  \[
    T=\frac12\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\phi')^2\dd x,
    \qquad
    V=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}U(\phi)\,\dd x.
  \]
  For the scaled configuration \(\phi_\lambda(x)=\phi(\lambda x)\),
  changing variables gives
  \[
    E[\phi_\lambda]=\lambda T+\lambda^{-1}V.
  \]
  A finite-energy solution of the Euler--Lagrange equation is stationary
  under this admissible variation, so
  \[
    0=\left.\frac{\dd E[\phi_\lambda]}{\dd\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}
      =T-V.
  \]
  Therefore
  \[
    \boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
      \frac12(\phi')^2\dd x
      =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}U(\phi)\,\dd x.}
  \]

  The static field equation is \(\phi''=\dd U/\dd\phi\).  Integrating the
  supplied right-hand side gives
  \[
    U(\phi)=\frac12\phi^6-\phi^4+\frac12\phi^2+C
      =\frac12\phi^2(1-\phi^2)^2+C.
  \]
  The polynomial preceding \(C\) is nonnegative and vanishes at
  \(\phi=-1,0,+1\), so the required choice is \(C=0\).  Hence
  \[
    \boxed{U^{-1}(0)=\{-1,0,+1\}.}
  \]
  There are two adjacent-vacuum kink species,
  \(-1\to0\) and \(0\to1\), and their two oppositely oriented
  antikinks.  Thus there are two unoriented types, or four oriented
  topological sectors.  A finite-width solution cannot pass directly
  from \(-1\) to \(+1\): it approaches the intermediate vacuum
  \(\phi=0\) only at infinite \(x\).

  On the interval \(0\le\phi\le1\),
  \[
    \sqrt{2U}=\phi(1-\phi^2).
  \]
  Completing the square gives
  \[
    E=\frac12\int \dd x\,
      [\phi'-\phi(1-\phi^2)]^2
      +\int_{\phi(-\infty)}^{\phi(+\infty)}
        \phi(1-\phi^2)\,\dd\phi.
  \]
  The \(0\to1\) kink therefore obeys
  \[
    \boxed{\phi'=\phi(1-\phi^2).}
  \]
  Separating variables,
  \[
    \log\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{1-\phi^2}}=x-x_0,
  \]
  and hence
  \[
    \boxed{\phi_{0,1}(x)
      =\frac1{\sqrt{1+e^{-2(x-x_0)}}}.}
  \]
  The other kink is
  \(\phi_{-1,0}(x)=-\phi_{0,1}(-x)\), with an independent choice of
  center; spatial reflection gives the antikinks.

  Saturation of the bound gives the kink mass
  \[
    \boxed{M=\int_0^1\phi(1-\phi^2)\,\dd\phi=\frac14.}
  \]
  Each adjacent-vacuum kink has this mass.  The limiting
  \(-1\to+1\) configuration consists of two infinitely separated kinks
  and has mass \(1/2\).

  \SupplementarySolution{3}{Monopole Topology and Gauge-Invariant Magnetic Charge}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)]
    Finite potential energy imposes
    \(\lvert\phi\rvert\to v\), and finite covariant-gradient energy
    imposes \(D_i\phi\to0\).  Thus the normalized scalar is well-defined
    on the sphere at infinity:
    \[
      \widehat\phi:S^2_\infty\longrightarrow
        \{\widehat\phi^a\widehat\phi^a=1\}\simeq S^2.
    \]
    Its degree is
    \[
      n=\frac1{8\pi}\int_{S^2_\infty}
        \epsilon^{abc}\widehat\phi^a\,
        \dd\widehat\phi^b\wedge \dd\widehat\phi^c
        \in\mathbb Z.
    \]
    A smooth finite-energy deformation supplies a homotopy between the
    initial and final maps, and degree is invariant under homotopy.  To
    change \(n\), \(\phi\) would have to vanish on the sphere at infinity
    or cease to approach a vacuum there, violating the finite-energy
    boundary conditions.
    \item[(b)]
    Both \(F_{\mu\nu}=F_{\mu\nu}^aT^a\) and
    \(D_\mu\widehat\phi=(D_\mu\widehat\phi)^aT^a\) transform in the
    adjoint representation.  The invariant scalar product and the
    invariant tensor \(\epsilon^{abc}\) are preserved by the adjoint
    \(SO(3)\) rotation.  Each term in \(\mathcal F_{\mu\nu}\), and hence
    their difference, is therefore gauge invariant.

    In unitary gauge \(\widehat\phi^a=\delta^{a3}\).  Although its ordinary
    derivative vanishes, its covariant derivative contains the charged
    gauge fields:
    \[
      (D_\mu\widehat\phi)^a
        =e\epsilon^{ab3}A_\mu^b.
    \]
    The second term in \(\mathcal F_{\mu\nu}\) cancels the
    \(e(A_\mu^1A_\nu^2-A_\mu^2A_\nu^1)\) part of \(F_{\mu\nu}^3\).
    What remains is
    \[
      \boxed{\mathcal F_{\mu\nu}
        =\partial_\mu A_\nu^3-\partial_\nu A_\mu^3.}
    \]
    Thus the definition is globally gauge invariant while locally
    reducing to the electromagnetic field strength of the unbroken
    \(U(1)\).
    \item[(c)]
    At infinity \(D_i\widehat\phi=0\).  Eliminating the gauge connection
    in favor of \(\widehat\phi\) gives the topological part of the
    magnetic field,
    \[
      B_i=\frac1{2e}\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon^{abc}
        \widehat\phi^a\partial_j\widehat\phi^b
        \partial_k\widehat\phi^c,
    \]
    with the overall sign correlated with the orientation convention in
    the degree.  Integrating and using part~(a) gives
    \[
      \boxed{g_m=\int_{S^2_\infty}\mathbf B\cdot \dd\mathbf S
        =\frac{4\pi n}{e}.}
    \]
    If the smallest electric charge carried by a doublet is
    \(q_{\min}=e/2\), then
    \[
      q_{\min}g_m=2\pi n,
    \]
    which is exactly Dirac quantization.  The smooth non-Abelian core
    replaces the singular Dirac string, but the asymptotic flux obeys the
    same quantization law.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{4}{The Bogomolny Bound and Monopole Scales}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)]
    For either sign,
    \[
    \begin{split}
      E={}&\frac12\int \dd^3x\,
         (B_i^a\mp D_i\phi^a)^2
         \ \pm\int \dd^3x\,B_i^aD_i\phi^a\\
        ={}&\frac12\int \dd^3x\,
         (B_i^a\mp D_i\phi^a)^2
         \ \pm\int_{S^2_\infty}\dd S_i\,\phi^aB_i^a.
    \end{split}
    \]
    The Bianchi identity gives \(D_iB_i=0\), which was used in the second
    line.  At infinity \(\phi^a\to v\widehat\phi^a\), so the surface term
    is \(4\pi vn/e\).  Choosing its sign to be positive yields
    \[
      \boxed{E\ge\frac{4\pi v}{e}\lvert n\rvert.}
    \]
    For \(n>0\), equality is attained by
    \[
      \boxed{B_i^a=D_i\phi^a;}
    \]
    for \(n<0\), the sign is reversed.
    \item[(b)]
    The monopole energy has mass dimension one.  Once a factor \(v/e\) is
    extracted, the only remaining dimensionless parameter is
    \(\lambda/e^2\).  The magnetic flux supplies the conventional factor
    \(4\pi\), so
    \[
      M_{\mathrm{mon}}=\frac{4\pi v}{e}
        f\left(\frac{\lambda}{e^2}\right).
    \]
    At \(\lambda=0\), the Bogomolny solution saturates the unit-charge
    bound, giving
    \[
      \boxed{f(0)=1.}
    \]
    The charged gauge field approaches its vacuum value on the scale
    \(m_W^{-1}=(ev)^{-1}\), while the scalar magnitude approaches \(v\)
    on the scale
    \(m_H^{-1}=(\sqrt{2\lambda}\,v)^{-1}\).  At the BPS point the scalar
    is massless and its tail is power-law rather than exponential.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{5}{A Biased Kink and a Static Antikink--Kink Pair}
  Differentiating the potential gives
  \[
    U'(\phi)=2(1-\phi^2)(\epsilon-\phi),
    \qquad
    U''(\phi)=2(3\phi^2-2\epsilon\phi-1).
  \]
  Thus \(U'(\pm1)=0\), while
  \[
    U''(+1)=4(1-\epsilon)>0,\qquad
    U''(-1)=4(1+\epsilon)>0
  \]
  for \(|\epsilon|<1\).  Both points are local minima.  Their energy
  densities are
  \[
    U(+1)=\frac{4\epsilon}{3},\qquad
    U(-1)=-\frac{4\epsilon}{3}.
  \]

  When \(\epsilon=0\),
  \(U_0=\frac12(1-\phi^2)^2\).  For a kink increasing from
  \(-1\) to \(+1\), the Bogomolny completion is
  \[
    E=\frac12\int \dd x\,[\phi'-(1-\phi^2)]^2
      +\int_{-1}^{1}(1-\phi^2)\,\dd\phi.
  \]
  Hence
  \[
    \phi'=1-\phi^2,\qquad
    \boxed{\phi_K(x;X)=\tanh(x-X),\qquad E_K=\frac43.}
  \]
  The antikink is \(-\tanh(x-X)\).

  For small positive \(\epsilon\), initially take
  \(\phi(x,t)=\tanh(x-X(t))\) with \(\dot X=0\).  The field equation is
  \[
    \phi_{tt}-\phi_{xx}+U_0'(\phi)
      +2\epsilon(1-\phi^2)=0.
  \]
  At the initial instant the unperturbed static equation cancels the
  middle two terms, while
  \(\phi_{tt}=-\ddot X\,\phi_K'\) and
  \(\phi_K'=1-\phi_K^2\).  Therefore
  \[
    -\ddot X(1-\phi_K^2)
      +2\epsilon(1-\phi_K^2)=0,
    \qquad
    \boxed{\ddot X=2\epsilon.}
  \]
  The same result follows from the pressure difference
  \[
    \Delta U=U(+1)-U(-1)=\frac{8\epsilon}{3}
  \]
  divided by the zeroth-order kink mass \(4/3\).  The kink accelerates
  toward the false \(+1\) region, enlarging the true \(-1\) region.

  A static configuration approaching \(+1\) at both ends has the first
  integral
  \[
    \frac12(\phi')^2=U(\phi)-U(+1).
  \]
  Now
  \[
    U(\phi)-U(+1)
      =(\phi-1)^2\left[
        \frac12(\phi+1)^2-\frac{2\epsilon}{3}(\phi+2)\right].
  \]
  Besides the double root at \(+1\), the bracket has a turning point
  \[
    \phi_*=-1+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}
      +2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon^2}{9}},
  \]
  which lies just above \(-1\) for small positive \(\epsilon\).
  Integrating the first-order equation from \(+1\) down to \(\phi_*\)
  and reflecting about the turning point constructs a static
  antikink--kink configuration.  It is the unstable critical bubble of
  the lower-energy \(-1\) phase inside the metastable \(+1\) phase.

  At large separation \(s\), the outward pressure force on either wall is
  \(8\epsilon/3\).  The supplied attractive force has magnitude
  \(32e^{-2s}\).  Static balance therefore requires
  \[
    32e^{-2s}=\frac{8\epsilon}{3},
    \qquad
    \boxed{s\simeq\frac12\log\frac{12}{\epsilon}.}
  \]
  Since \(\epsilon\ll1\), this indeed gives \(s\gg1\), the regime in
  which the force estimate is valid.

  \SupplementarySolution{6}{Instanton Topology, Self-Duality, and the BPST Modulus}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)]
    With \(F=\dd A+A\wedge A\), direct expansion and cyclicity of the trace
    give
    \[
      \operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F)
        =\dd\,\operatorname{tr}\left(
          A\wedge \dd A+\frac23A\wedge A\wedge A\right).
    \]
    Thus the four-dimensional integral is a Chern--Simons integral over
    \(S^3_\infty\).

    Finite action requires \(F_{\mu\nu}\to0\) sufficiently rapidly.
    Consequently the connection at infinity is pure gauge,
    \(A\to U^{-1}\dd U\), up to the convention-dependent factor of \(i\).
    Substitution in the boundary integral gives the normalized winding
    number
    \[
      \frac1{24\pi^2}\int_{S^3_\infty}
         \operatorname{tr}(U^{-1}\dd U)^3\in\mathbb Z.
    \]
    Since \(\pi_3(SU(2))=\mathbb Z\), this integer is \(Q\), with its sign
    fixed by the orientation and gauge-field conventions.
    \item[(b)]
    In Euclidean four-space \(\widetilde{\widetilde F}=F\).  Positivity
    gives
    \[
    \begin{split}
      0&\le\int \dd^4x\,
         \operatorname{tr}(F_{\mu\nu}\mp\widetilde F_{\mu\nu})^2\\
       &=2\int \dd^4x\,\operatorname{tr}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
         \mp2\int \dd^4x\,
            \operatorname{tr}F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F_{\mu\nu}.
    \end{split}
    \]
    Using the definition of \(Q\) therefore yields
    \[
      S_E\ge\frac{8\pi^2}{g^2}\lvert Q\rvert.
    \]
    Equality holds for \(F=\widetilde F\) when \(Q>0\) and
    \(F=-\widetilde F\) when \(Q<0\).

    The Bianchi identity is \(D_\mu\widetilde F_{\mu\nu}=0\).
    For a self-dual or anti-self-dual field it becomes
    \(D_\mu F_{\mu\nu}=0\), which is precisely the Euclidean Yang--Mills
    equation.  Thus saturation of the topological bound implies the
    second-order field equation.
    \item[(c)]
    The stated identity for the 't Hooft symbols gives
    \[
      F_{\mu\nu}^aF_{\mu\nu}^a
        =\frac{192\rho^4}{(x^2+\rho^2)^4}.
    \]
    In four-dimensional polar coordinates,
    \(\dd^4x=2\pi^2r^3\dd r\), and
    \[
      \int_{\mathbb R^4}\frac{\dd^4x}{(x^2+\rho^2)^4}
        =\frac{\pi^2}{6\rho^4}.
    \]
    Consequently
    \[
      \int \dd^4x\,F_{\mu\nu}^aF_{\mu\nu}^a=32\pi^2,\qquad
      \int \dd^4x\,\operatorname{tr}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}=16\pi^2.
    \]
    The field is self-dual, so
    \[
      Q=\frac{16\pi^2}{16\pi^2}=1,\qquad
      \boxed{S_E=\frac{8\pi^2}{g^2}.}
    \]
    The cancellation of \(\rho\) in the integral is the classical
    scale-invariance of four-dimensional Yang--Mills theory.
    \item[(d)]
    Write \(u=r^2/\rho^2\).  Apart from the common normalization, the
    cumulative action inside a four-ball is
    \[
      \frac{S(R)}{S(\infty)}
        =\frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{R^2/\rho^2}
            \dd u\,\frac{u}{(1+u)^4}}
          {\displaystyle\int_0^\infty
            \dd u\,\frac{u}{(1+u)^4}}
        =1-\frac3{(1+U)^2}+\frac2{(1+U)^3},
      \quad U=\frac{R^2}{\rho^2}.
    \]
    At \(U=1\) this fraction is \(1/2\), so
    \[
      \boxed{R_{1/2}=\rho.}
    \]
    Varying \(\rho\) changes the physical region carrying the action but
    leaves the normalized integral and the winding number unchanged.
    Since the classical theory has no intrinsic length scale, no value of
    \(\rho\) is preferred; it is therefore a collective coordinate.
    Quantum running of \(g\) breaks this scale invariance in the
    semiclassical measure.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{7}{Hodge Duality, the Yang--Mills Bound, and an ASDYM Lax Pair}
  The Hodge operator is characterized by
  \[
    \alpha\wedge\star\beta
      =\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g\operatorname{vol}
  \]
  for forms \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) of the same degree.  On
  \(p\)-forms in four-dimensional Euclidean space,
  \[
    \star^2=(-1)^{p(4-p)},
  \]
  and consequently \(\star^2=1\) on two-forms.  Every two-form has the
  unique decomposition
  \[
    \omega=\omega_++\omega_-,\qquad
    \omega_\pm=\frac12(\omega\pm\star\omega),
    \qquad \star\omega_\pm=\pm\omega_\pm.
  \]
  The two eigenspaces are orthogonal: if \(H\) is self-dual and \(G\)
  is anti-self-dual, then
  \[
    \langle H,G\rangle_g
      =\langle\star H,\star G\rangle_g
      =-\langle H,G\rangle_g=0.
  \]
  It follows that
  \[
    \boxed{H\wedge G=-H\wedge\star G=0.}
  \]

  To make the normalization of the bound explicit, take the matrices in
  \(\mathfrak{su}(n)\) to be anti-Hermitian and use the positive
  invariant form \(-\operatorname{tr}(XY)\).  Define
  \[
    S_{\mathrm{YM}}
      =-\frac1{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2}
        \int_{\mathbb R^4}\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge\star F),
    \qquad
    c_2=\frac1{8\pi^2}
        \int_{\mathbb R^4}\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F).
  \]
  Writing \(F=F_++F_-\), the mixed wedge products vanish and
  \[
  \begin{split}
    g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2S_{\mathrm{YM}}
      &=\lVert F_+\rVert^2+\lVert F_-\rVert^2,\\
    8\pi^2c_2
      &=-\lVert F_+\rVert^2+\lVert F_-\rVert^2.
  \end{split}
  \]
  Therefore
  \[
    \boxed{S_{\mathrm{YM}}
      \geq\frac{8\pi^2}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2}|c_2|.}
  \]
  Equality holds for an ASD field when \(c_2>0\) and for an SD field
  when \(c_2<0\) with these orientation and trace conventions.  Reversing
  either convention reverses the sign assigned to \(c_2\), but not the
  bound.

  Now set \(x^4=\tau\) and choose the orientation for which the ASD
  equations read
  \[
    F_{\tau i}=\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}F_{jk}.
  \]
  In temporal gauge,
  \[
    F_{\tau i}=\partial_\tau A_i
      -\partial_iA_\tau+[A_\tau,A_i]
      =\partial_\tau A_i,
  \]
  so this gives
  \[
    \boxed{\partial_\tau A_i
      =\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}F_{jk},\qquad i=1,2,3.}
  \]
  (The question's displayed qualifier \(k=1,2,3\) is naturally read as
  the range of the free spatial index \(i\).)

  Let \(D_\mu=\partial_\mu+A_\mu\).  One Lax pair, with complex spectral
  parameter \(\lambda\), is
  \[
  \begin{split}
    L_0(\lambda)
      &=D_1+\ii D_2-\lambda(D_3-\ii D_\tau),\\
    L_1(\lambda)
      &=D_3+\ii D_\tau+\lambda(D_1-\ii D_2),
  \end{split}
  \qquad
    L_0(\lambda)\Psi=L_1(\lambda)\Psi=0,
  \]
  where \(D_\tau=\partial_\tau\) in temporal gauge.  Expanding the
  compatibility condition
  \([L_0(\lambda),L_1(\lambda)]=0\) in powers of \(\lambda\), the
  constant and quadratic terms give
  \[
    F_{\tau1}=F_{23},\qquad F_{\tau2}=F_{31},
  \]
  and the linear term gives \(F_{\tau3}=F_{12}\).  These are precisely
  the three evolution equations above.

  \SupplementarySolution{8}{Anti-Self-Dual Yang--Mills in Complex Coordinates}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)]
    For equal-degree forms, the Hodge operator is defined by
    \[
      \alpha\wedge\star\beta
        =\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_\eta\operatorname{vol}.
    \]
    Since \(\star^2=1\) on two-forms in four-dimensional Euclidean
    space, write
    \(\omega=\omega_++\omega_-\), where
    \(\star\omega_\pm=\pm\omega_\pm\).  The SD and ASD summands are
    orthogonal and their wedge product vanishes.  Hence
    \[
    \begin{split}
      \omega\wedge\omega
        &=\omega_+\wedge\omega_+
          +\omega_-\wedge\omega_-,\\
      \star\omega\wedge\star\omega
        &=(\omega_+-\omega_-)\wedge(\omega_+-\omega_-)
         =\omega\wedge\omega.
    \end{split}
    \]

    For anti-Hermitian \(\mathfrak{su}(2)\) matrices, use
    \[
      S_{\mathrm{YM}}=-\int\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge\star F),
      \qquad
      c_2=\frac1{8\pi^2}
        \int\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F).
    \]
    If \(F=-\star F\), then
    \[
      \boxed{S_{\mathrm{YM}}
        =\int\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F)=8\pi^2c_2.}
    \]
    Thus the stated equality follows with the source's normalization;
    the opposite trace or orientation convention changes the sign used
    in defining \(c_2\).

    \item[(ii)]
    Put \(D_a=\partial_a+A_a\).  A spectral-parameter Lax pair is
    \[
      \boxed{(D_w-\lambda D_{\bar z})\Psi=0,
      \qquad
      (D_z+\lambda D_{\bar w})\Psi=0.}
    \]
    Its compatibility condition is
    \[
    \begin{split}
      0={}&[D_w-\lambda D_{\bar z},
             D_z+\lambda D_{\bar w}]\\
       ={}&F_{wz}
          +\lambda(F_{w\bar w}+F_{z\bar z})
          +\lambda^2F_{\bar w\bar z}.
    \end{split}
    \]
    Since this must hold for every \(\lambda\), its three coefficients
    are exactly equations~(1).

    \item[(iii)]
    Under
    \(A_a\mapsto g^{-1}A_ag+g^{-1}\partial_ag\), the conditions
    \(A_w'=A_z'=0\) amount to
    \[
      (\partial_w+A_w)g=0,
      \qquad
      (\partial_z+A_z)g=0.
    \]
    Their integrability condition is
    \([D_w,D_z]g=F_{wz}g=0\), which is the first equation in~(1).
    Thus the desired gauge exists locally.  It exists globally on
    \(\mathbb R^4\) for a smooth connection on the trivial bundle, with
    the usual choice of gauge at a base point.

    \item[(iv)]
    In this gauge the second equation becomes
    \[
      \partial_{\bar w}A_{\bar z}
       -\partial_{\bar z}A_{\bar w}
       +[A_{\bar w},A_{\bar z}]=0.
    \]
    This is the integrability condition for a group-valued function
    \(J\) satisfying
    \[
      A_{\bar w}=J^{-1}\partial_{\bar w}J,
      \qquad
      A_{\bar z}=J^{-1}\partial_{\bar z}J.
    \]
    Consequently
    \[
      A=J^{-1}\partial_{\bar w}J\,\dd\bar w
        +J^{-1}\partial_{\bar z}J\,\dd\bar z.
    \]
    Since \(A_w=A_z=0\), the remaining curvature components are
    \[
      F_{w\bar w}=\partial_w(J^{-1}\partial_{\bar w}J),
      \qquad
      F_{z\bar z}=\partial_z(J^{-1}\partial_{\bar z}J).
    \]
    The third equation in~(1) therefore reduces to Yang's equation
    \[
      \boxed{\partial_w(J^{-1}\partial_{\bar w}J)
       +\partial_z(J^{-1}\partial_{\bar z}J)=0.}
    \]

    \item[(v)]
    For Maxwell theory take \(G=U(1)\) and write, locally,
    \(J=e^{\ii\chi}\) with \(\chi\) real.  Commutators vanish and Yang's
    equation becomes
    \[
      \ii\bigl(\partial_w\partial_{\bar w}\chi
        +\partial_z\partial_{\bar z}\chi\bigr)=0.
    \]
    With \(\dd s^2=|\dd z|^2+|\dd w|^2\),
    \[
      \Delta=4\left(
        \partial_z\partial_{\bar z}
       +\partial_w\partial_{\bar w}\right).
    \]
    Hence the anti-self-dual Maxwell equations in this gauge are
    equivalent to
    \[
      \boxed{\Delta\chi=0,}
    \]
    or equivalently to the Laplace equation for the locally defined
    Lie-algebra-valued logarithm \(\log J=\ii\chi\).
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{11}{Euclidean Bounces and Lorentzian Bubble Evolution}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)]

    \[
      S_E=2\pi^2\int_0^\infty \dd\rho\,\rho^3
        \left[\frac12(\phi')^2+V(\phi)\right].
    \]
    The Euler--Lagrange equation is
    \[
      \boxed{\phi''+\frac3\rho\phi'=V'(\phi),}
    \]
    with
    \[
      \phi'(0)=0,\qquad
      \phi(\rho)\longrightarrow\phi_f
        \quad(\rho\to\infty).
    \]
    It describes a particle moving in the inverted potential \(-V\) with
    time \(\rho\) and friction \(3/\rho\).  Starting very near the true
    vacuum delays the roll until the friction is weak and therefore
    overshoots \(\phi_f\).  Starting closer to the barrier rolls while
    the friction is strong and undershoots.  Continuity between these
    initial data selects the bounce.
    \item[(b)]
    Under \(\phi_R(x)=\phi(x/R)\),
    \[
      B(R)=R^2T+R^4U.
    \]
    Stationarity of the bounce at \(R=1\) gives
    \[
      2T+4U=0,\qquad T=-2U.
    \]
    Thus
    \[
      \boxed{B=T+U=\frac T2=-U>0.}
    \]
    Since \(T>0\), the integrated potential difference must satisfy
    \(U<0\).  Therefore the bounce cannot remain only in regions where
    \(V(\phi)\ge V(\phi_f)\); it must occupy enough four-volume on the
    lower-potential side of the barrier to make the total \(U\) negative.
    \item[(c)]
    Analytic continuation gives
    \[
      \phi(r,t)=\phi_b\left(\sqrt{r^2-t^2}\right).
    \]
    On the time-symmetric slice,
    \[
      \phi(r,0)=\phi_b(r),\qquad
      \dot\phi(r,t)
        =-\frac{t}{\sqrt{r^2-t^2}}\,
         \phi_b'\left(\sqrt{r^2-t^2}\right),
    \]
    and hence \(\dot\phi(r,0)=0\).  The continued bounce therefore
    supplies a critical bubble profile with no initial wall velocity.

    Euclidean rotations preserve \(r^2+\tau^2\).  After
    \(\tau\to \ii t\), the preserved combination is \(r^2-t^2\), and the
    symmetry acting on the solution is \(O(3,1)\).  The vanishing initial
    time derivative is why the bubble is said to materialize at rest;
    its subsequent expansion follows from the Lorentzian field equation.
    \item[(d)]
    Continuing the Euclidean wall \(\rho^2=r^2+\tau^2=R^2\) gives
    \[
      r^2-t^2=R^2,\qquad
      \boxed{r(t)=\sqrt{R^2+t^2}.}
    \]
    Therefore
    \[
      v_{\mathrm w}=\frac{\dd r}{\dd t}=\frac t r,\qquad
      \gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v_{\mathrm w}^2}}=\frac rR.
    \]
    A worldline \(r^2-t^2=R^2\) is the standard uniformly accelerated
    hyperbola, with proper acceleration
    \[
      \boxed{a_{\mathrm{proper}}=\frac1R.}
    \]
    At late times
    \(r=t+R^2/(2t)+O(t^{-3})\) and
    \(v_{\mathrm w}\to1\), so the wall approaches the light cone without
    crossing it.
    \item[(e)]

        =4\pi r^2\sigma\frac rR

      \boxed{E_{\mathrm{wall}}
        =\frac{4\pi}{3}\epsilon r^3=E_{\mathrm{vol}}.}

    \(+E_{\mathrm{wall}}\).  Their sum remains zero.  The vacuum energy

    area and kinetic energy of the accelerating wall.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{12}{A Three-Vacuum \(\phi^8\) Kink with Unequal Tails}
  Varying the field gives
  \[
    \boxed{\phi_{tt}-\phi_{xx}+U'(\phi)=0.}
  \]
  The corresponding total energy is
  \[
    E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd x\left[
      \frac12\phi_t^2+\frac12\phi_x^2+U(\phi)\right].
  \]
  For a field whose boundary terms vanish,
  \[
  \begin{split}
    \frac{\dd E}{\dd t}
      &=\int \dd x\,[\phi_t\phi_{tt}+\phi_x\phi_{xt}
                    +U'(\phi)\phi_t]\\
      &=\int \dd x\,\phi_t[\phi_{tt}-\phi_{xx}+U'(\phi)]
        +[\phi_x\phi_t]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=0.
  \end{split}
  \]

  The specified potential is even and has three degenerate vacua,
  \(\phi=-1,0,+1\).
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)]
    The two nontrivial discrete transformations are
    \[
      \boxed{\alpha=-1,\qquad\beta=-1,}
    \]
    namely field reflection and spatial reflection.  Translations of the
    argument are understood.  If
    \(K(x)=\phi_{(0,1)}(x)\), then the four oriented adjacent-vacuum
    solutions can be written
    \[
    \begin{array}{c|c}
      \text{sector}&\text{profile}\\ \hline
      0\longrightarrow1&K(x)\\
      1\longrightarrow0&K(-x)\\
      -1\longrightarrow0&-K(-x)\\
      0\longrightarrow-1&-K(x).
    \end{array}
    \]
    Thus there are two kink sectors and their two antikinks.  A direct
    \(-1\)-to-\(+1\) profile separates into two kinks because the
    intermediate vacuum is reached only at infinite distance.

    \item[(ii)]
    A convenient superpotential is
    \[
      \boxed{W(\phi)=\frac{\phi^3}{3}-\frac{\phi^5}{5},
      \qquad W'(\phi)=\phi^2(1-\phi^2).}
    \]
    It obeys \(U=\tfrac12(W')^2\).  In the \(0\)-to-\(1\) sector,
    \[
      E=\frac12\int \dd x\,[\phi'-W'(\phi)]^2
        +W(1)-W(0),
    \]
    so the Bogomolny equation and its saturated energy are
    \[
      \boxed{\phi'=\phi^2(1-\phi^2),
      \qquad E_{(0,1)}=\frac13-\frac15=\frac{2}{15}.}
    \]

    \item[(iii)]
    Separating the Bogomolny equation gives
    \[
      x-A=\int^\phi\frac{\dd\varphi}{\varphi^2(1-\varphi^2)}.
    \]

    \[
      \frac{\dd}{\dd\phi}\left[
        \frac12\ln\left(\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}\right)
          -\frac1\phi\right]
        =\frac1{\phi^2(1-\phi^2)},
    \]
    the required implicit profile follows:
    \[
      \boxed{\frac12\ln\left(\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}\right)
        -\frac1\phi=x-A.}
    \]

    \item[(iv)]
    As \(\phi\to0^+\), the left-hand side is
    \(-1/\phi+O(\phi)\).  Hence
    \[
      \phi\sim\frac1{A-x}\qquad(x\to-\infty).
    \]
    At the other end put \(\delta=1-\phi\).  Then
    \[
      \frac12\ln\left(\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}\right)
        -\frac1\phi
      =-\frac12\ln\delta+\frac12\ln2-1+O(\delta).
    \]
    Comparing with \(x-A\) gives
    \[
      \delta\sim2e^{2A-2}e^{-2x}=e^{-2(x-b)},
      \qquad
      \boxed{b=A-1+\frac12\ln2.}
    \]
    The kink is consequently highly asymmetric: its approach to the
    quartic vacuum at \(\phi=0\) is algebraic, whereas its approach to the
    quadratic vacuum at \(\phi=1\) is exponential.

  \SupplementarySolution{14}{Peierls Dimerization and the Polyacetylene Wall Mode}

      \[
        H_D=-iv_F\sigma_2\partial_x
          +M_1(x)\sigma_1+M_2(x)\sigma_3 .
      \]
      The vector \((M_1,M_2)\) can rotate by \(\pi\) at fixed nonzero
      length, and no single matrix anticommutes with the full Hamiltonian
      throughout the interpolation.  On the lattice the hopping phases
      similarly break the real spectral symmetry that pinned the wall
      eigenvalue.  The bound level may move away from midgap and merge into
      a band, so a zero mode is no longer protected.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{15}{The Complete \(\phi^4\) Kink}
  Multiplying \(\phi''=V'(\phi)\) by \(\phi'\) gives
  \[
    \frac12\phi'^2-V(\phi)=C.
  \]
  Finite energy sets \(C=0\).  With
  \(W'=\sqrt{\lambda/2}(v^2-\phi^2)\),
  \[
    E=\int \dd x\,\frac12(\phi'-W')^2+W(+v)-W(-v).
  \]
  Saturation requires
  \(\phi'=\sqrt{\lambda/2}(v^2-\phi^2)\), hence
  \[
    \boxed{\phi_K(x)=v\tanh\!\left[
      v\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2}}(x-x_0)\right]},
    \qquad
    \boxed{M_K=\frac{2\sqrt{2\lambda}}{3}v^3}.
  \]
  Since \(1-\tanh z\sim2e^{-2z}\), the tail decays with inverse length
  \(\sqrt{2\lambda}\,v\), equal to the vacuum particle mass.

  More generally, if \(V=\frac12(W')^2\), differentiation of
  \(\phi'=W'(\phi)\) gives
  \[
    \phi''=W''W'=\frac{\dd V}{\dd\phi},
  \]
  so every Bogomolny solution obeys the static Euler--Lagrange equation.
  With \(x\) regarded as mechanical time, that equation is Newton's law
  for a particle with coordinate \(\phi\) moving in the inverted potential
  \(-V(\phi)\).  The finite-energy kink is the zero-mechanical-energy
  trajectory joining two adjacent maxima of \(-V\).

  If a third degenerate minimum lies between the endpoints, the first-order
  flow reaches it only at infinite \(x\): near a nondegenerate minimum,
  \(\dd(\phi-\phi_*)/\dd x\) is proportional to \(\phi-\phi_*\).  A single smooth
  finite-width solution therefore cannot pass through it.  The putative
  skipping configuration separates into two kinks with an arbitrarily long
  interval of the intermediate vacuum.

  \SupplementarySolution{16}{Gauge-Invariant Vortex Winding and Radial Gauge}
  On a large circle a gauge transformation changes the scalar phase by
  \(\alpha(R,\vartheta)\).  If \(e^{\ii\alpha}\) extends smoothly over the
  enclosed disk, its restriction to the boundary is null-homotopic.
  Equivalently,
  \[
    \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
      \dd\vartheta\,\partial_\vartheta\alpha=0.
  \]
  Hence it cannot change the winding \(n\).  A transformation with nonzero
  boundary winding would be singular or multivalued somewhere in the
  plane and is not an allowed smooth gauge transformation of the vortex.

  Define
  \[
    \alpha(r,\vartheta)
      =-q\int_0^r \dd s\,A_r(s,\vartheta).
  \]
  Then \(A_r\mapsto A_r+q^{-1}\partial_r\alpha=0\).  Smoothness of the
  original Cartesian gauge field at the origin makes this transformation
  regular there, and periodicity of \(A_r\) makes it single-valued in
  \(\vartheta\).

  By contrast, setting \(A_\vartheta\) to zero around a closed circle would
  require a periodic \(\alpha\) satisfying
  \[
    0=\oint A_i\,\dd x^i
      +\frac1q\oint \dd\alpha .
  \]
  The second integral vanishes for a single-valued gauge transformation,
  whereas the first approaches the nonzero vortex flux
  \(2\pi n/q\).  Thus nontrivial holonomy, equivalently nonzero flux,
  obstructs imposing \(A_\vartheta=0\) globally.

  \SupplementarySolution{17}{The Critical Abelian-Higgs Bogomolny Bound}
  Covariant differentiation obeys the product rule because the gauge
  connections on \(\phi\) and \(\phi^*\) cancel:
  \[
    \partial_i(\phi^*D_j\phi)
      =(D_i\phi)^*D_j\phi+\phi^*D_iD_j\phi.
  \]
  Direct expansion of \(D_i=\partial_i-\ii qA_i\) also gives
  \[
    [D_i,D_j]\phi=-\ii q(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i)\phi
      =-\ii qF_{ij}\phi .
  \]
  Using
  \[
    |D_1\phi\pm \ii D_2\phi|^2
      =|D_i\phi|^2\mp qB|\phi|^2+\text{divergence},
  \]
  the critical energy becomes
  \[
    E=\int \dd^2x\left[
      |(D_1\pm \ii D_2)\phi|^2
      +\frac12\{B\mp q(v^2-|\phi|^2)\}^2\right]
      \pm qv^2\Phi .
  \]
  Choosing the sign of the flux gives
  \[
    E\geq qv^2|\Phi|=2\pi v^2|n|.
  \]
  Equality holds when
  \[
    (D_1\pm \ii D_2)\phi=0,\qquad
    B=\pm q(v^2-|\phi|^2).
  \]
  Applying the opposite covariant derivative to the first equation and
  using \([D_1,D_2]=-\ii qB\), together with the second equation, reproduces
  the scalar equation; differentiating the magnetic equation similarly
  gives Ampere's equation.  The bound is exactly linear in \(|n|\), so a
  separated collection of unit vortices has the same energy as a coincident
  \(n\)-vortex.  The scalar attraction and magnetic repulsion cancel,
  yielding no static force at critical coupling.

  \SupplementarySolution{18}{Bright Solitons in Slowly Varying and Harmonic Potentials}
  Write the symplectic term as
  \[
    \langle \ii\phi,\dot\phi\rangle
      =\frac{\ii}{2}(\phi\dot{\bar\phi}-\bar\phi\dot\phi).
  \]
  Treating \(\phi\) and \(\bar\phi\) as independent fields, variation
  with respect to \(\bar\phi\), followed by an integration by parts,
  gives
  \[
    -\ii\phi_t-\frac12\phi_{xx}-|\phi|^2\phi
      +\epsilon V(\epsilon x)\phi=0.
  \]
  Thus the Euler--Lagrange equation is precisely (1).

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)]
    Set
    \[
      k=\sqrt{2|E|},\qquad f_E(x)=k\operatorname{sech}(kx).
    \]
    The elementary identity
    \[
      f_E''=k^2f_E-2f_E^3
    \]
    implies
    \[
      -\frac12f_E''-f_E^3=-\frac{k^2}{2}f_E=Ef_E.
    \]
    Therefore \(\phi=e^{-\ii Et}f_E\) solves (1) when \(\epsilon=0\).

    \item[(ii)]
    Put \(y=x-X(t)\) and
    \(\phi=f_E(y)e^{\ii\theta(t)+iu(t)y}\).  Direct differentiation gives
    \[
    \begin{split}
      \ii\phi_t={}&e^{\ii\theta+iuy}
       \left[-\ii\dot X f_E'
        -(\dot\theta+\dot u\,y-u\dot X)f_E\right],\\
      -\frac12\phi_{xx}-|\phi|^2\phi
        ={}&e^{\ii\theta+iuy}
       \left[Ef_E-iu f_E'+\frac12u^2f_E\right].
    \end{split}
    \]
    The coefficients of \(f_E'\), \(yf_E\), and \(f_E\) agree exactly
    when
    \[
      \boxed{\dot X=u,\qquad\dot u=0,\qquad
        \dot\theta=-E+\frac12u^2.}
    \]

    The norm of the profile is
    \[
      \mathcal N(E)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_E^2\dd x=2k.
    \]
    The other useful integrals are
    \[
      \int(f_E')^2\dd x=\frac23k^3,
      \qquad
      \int f_E^4\dd x=\frac43k^3.
    \]
    Since \(|\phi_x|^2=(f_E')^2+u^2f_E^2\),
    \[
      \boxed{H=-\frac13k^3+ku^2,
      \qquad P=2ku=\mathcal N u.}
    \]
    Eliminating \(u\) gives either of the equivalent relations
    \[
      \boxed{H=H_0(E)+\frac{P^2}{2\mathcal N(E)}
        =-\frac{\mathcal N^3}{24}
          +\frac{P^2}{2\mathcal N},
      \qquad H_0(E)=\frac13E\mathcal N.}
    \]
    For fixed internal profile, the bright soliton therefore has the
    ordinary particle dispersion with inertial mass equal to its conserved
    norm \(\mathcal N\), in addition to its negative binding energy.

    \item[(iii)]
    Let \(g_E(y)\) be any real localized solution of
    \[
      Eg_E=-\frac12g_E''-g_E^3+\frac12y^2g_E
    \]
    and take
    \[
      \phi(x,t)=g_E(x-X(t))
        e^{\ii\theta(t)+iu(t)(x-X(t))}.
    \]
    Repeating the preceding substitution, but using
    \(x^2=(y+X)^2\), gives
    \[
      \boxed{\dot X=u,\qquad
        \dot u=-X,\qquad
        \dot\theta=-E+\frac12u^2-\frac12X^2.}
    \]
    Hence \(\ddot X+X=0\).  The center and momentum parameter obey
    Hamilton's equations for
    \[
      H_{\mathrm{cl}}(X,u)=\frac12u^2+\frac12X^2,
    \]
    while the phase accumulates the internal frequency \(-E\) plus the
    classical center-of-mass Lagrangian
    \((u^2-X^2)/2\).  The nonlinear shape \(g_E\) is carried rigidly by
    the same harmonic orbit as a point particle.

    \item[(iv)]
    Use the four-parameter ansatz from part~(ii), now allowing all four
    parameters to depend on time.  Because \(f_E\) is real and even,
    the terms proportional to \(\dot E\) and
    \(\dot u\int y f_E^2\dd y\) make no contribution to the symplectic
    integral.  Define
    \[
    \begin{split}
      \mathcal N(E)&=2\sqrt{2|E|},\\
      H_0(E)&=-\frac13(2|E|)^{3/2},\\
      \mathcal V_\epsilon(E,X)
        &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd y\,
          V\bigl(\epsilon(X+y)\bigr)f_E(y)^2.
    \end{split}
    \]
    Substitution into the source action gives the effective Lagrangian
    \[
      \boxed{L_{\mathrm{eff}}
        =\mathcal N(E)(\dot\theta-u\dot X)
         +H_0(E)+\frac12\mathcal N(E)u^2
         +\epsilon\mathcal V_\epsilon(E,X).}
    \]
    The \(\theta\) equation conserves \(\mathcal N\), and hence
    \(\dot E=0\).  The \(u\)- and \(X\)-equations give
    \[
      \boxed{\dot X=u,\qquad
        \dot u=-\frac{\epsilon}{\mathcal N}
          \frac{\partial\mathcal V_\epsilon}{\partial X}.}
    \]
    Finally, variation with respect to \(E\) yields
    \[
      \boxed{\dot\theta=-E+\frac12u^2
        -\epsilon\frac{\partial_E\mathcal V_\epsilon}
          {\mathcal N'(E)},}
    \]
    where the identity
    \(-H_0'(E)/\mathcal N'(E)=-E\) was used.

    Since the external potential varies on the scale \(\epsilon^{-1}\),
    parity of \(f_E^2\) gives
    \[
      \mathcal V_\epsilon(E,X)
        =\mathcal N(E)V(\epsilon X)+O(\epsilon^2).
    \]
    To leading nontrivial order, the collective-coordinate dynamics is
    therefore
    \[
      \boxed{\dot E=0,\qquad \dot X=u,\qquad
        \dot u=-\epsilon^2V'(\epsilon X),\qquad
        \dot\theta=-E+\frac12u^2-\epsilon V(\epsilon X).}
    \]
    In particular,
    \(\ddot X=-\partial_X[\epsilon V(\epsilon X)]\): the soliton center
    follows the classical Hamiltonian
    \(u^2/2+\epsilon V(\epsilon X)\), while its norm and internal
    parameter remain fixed at this order.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{19}{Two-Vortex Moduli-Space Scattering}
  At critical coupling the static energy has a Bogomolny bound.  In the
  sector of flux \(2\pi N\), the moduli space \(\mathcal M_N\) is the set
  of all solutions of the first-order vortex equations modulo gauge
  transformations.  A solution is determined, up to gauge, by the
  unordered zeros \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_N\) of its Higgs field.  Thus
  \[
    \mathcal M_N\simeq\operatorname{Sym}^N(\mathbb C),
  \]
  a smooth complex \(N\)-manifold; elementary symmetric polynomials in
  the \(Z_a\), rather than the individually labeled zeros, are regular
  global coordinates.

  Promote the moduli \(q^a\) slowly to functions of time and solve Gauss's
  law, equivalently impose background gauge on each zero mode.  The
  field-theory kinetic energy then has the form
  \[
    T=\frac12g_{ab}(q)\dot q^a\dot q^b,
  \]
  where \(g\) is the \(L^2\) inner product of the gauge and Higgs zero
  modes.  This is the natural smooth, complete K\"ahler metric on
  \(\mathcal M_N\).  With no potential on the critical moduli space, its
  geodesics approximate low-speed vortex motion.

  For \(N=1\), translation invariance makes
  \(\mathcal M_1\simeq\mathbb C\) a flat plane, with an overall metric
  coefficient equal to the one-vortex mass.  For \(N=2\), separate the
  center of mass \(Z=(Z_1+Z_2)/2\) and relative coordinate
  \(w=(Z_1-Z_2)/2\).  Interchange of the vortices sends \(w\mapsto-w\),
  so the single-valued relative coordinate is
  \[
    \zeta=w^2.
  \]
  Accordingly
  \[
    \mathcal M_2\simeq\mathbb C_{\mathrm{cm}}
      \times\mathcal M_2^0,
  \]
  where \(\mathcal M_2^0\) is asymptotic to the flat cone
  \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z_2\).  The exact metric rounds off the apparent
  conical tip: \(\zeta=0\), the coincident two-vortex, is a smooth point.

  In a head-on center-of-mass collision the geodesic passes smoothly
  through \(\zeta=0\) and continues to the opposite side of the
  \(\zeta\)-plane.  Thus \(\zeta\mapsto-\zeta\), so
  \(w\mapsto iw\):
  \[
    \boxed{\text{two vortices scatter through }90^\circ\text{ head on}.}
  \]
  For increasing \(|b|\), the trajectory samples less of the rounded
  tip and its deflection decreases monotonically to zero.  Because
  multiplying the initial velocity merely reparametrizes a geodesic, the
  scattering curve is independent of speed within the geodesic
  approximation.  In the following signed-angle convention the two
  branches at \(b=0\) represent the same unlabeled right-angle event.

  \SupplementarySolution{20}{The Rational-Map Approximation to the \(B=7\) Skyrmion}
  Compactifying physical space gives
  \(\mathbb R^3\cup\{\infty\}\simeq S^3\), and a finite-energy Skyrme
  field \(U:S^3\to SU(2)\simeq S^3\) has integer baryon number equal to
  its degree.  In the rational-map ansatz one writes
  \[
    U(r,z)=\exp\!\left[\ii f(r)\,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}_{R(z)}\cdot\boldsymbol\tau\right],
    \qquad
    \widehat{\mathbf n}_{R}
      =\frac{(2\operatorname{Re}R,2\operatorname{Im}R,1-|R|^2)}
             {1+|R|^2},
  \]
  where \(z\) is the stereographic coordinate on the angular two-sphere,
  \(R(z)=p(z)/q(z)\) is a degree-\(N\) rational map, and
  \(f(0)=\pi\), \(f(\infty)=0\).  The angular degree and radial winding
  factorize, so
  \[
    \boxed{B=N.}
  \]

  In standard dimensionless Skyrme units the energy reduces to
  \[
    E=4\pi\int_0^\infty \dd r\left[
      r^2f'^2+2N(f'^2+1)\sin^2f
      +\mathcal I\frac{\sin^4f}{r^2}\right],
  \]
  up to the conventional overall normalization, where
  \[
    \mathcal I=\frac1{4\pi}\int_{S^2}
      \left(\frac{1+|z|^2}{1+|R|^2}
        \left|\frac{\dd R}{\dd z}\right|\right)^4\dd\Omega.
  \]
  One first minimizes \(\mathcal I\) over degree-\(N\) maps and then
  solves the remaining radial equation for \(f\).  This preserves the
  topology exactly and usually captures the symmetry and shell structure
  of the numerical Skyrmion while reducing a three-dimensional variational
  problem to a finite-dimensional angular minimization and one radial ODE.

  For the map in the question let
  \(\omega=e^{2\pi \ii/5}\).  Since \(\omega^5=1\),
  \[
    R(\omega z)=\omega^2R(z).
  \]
  A spatial rotation through \(2\pi/5\) is therefore compensated by a
  target-sphere rotation through \(4\pi/5\), establishing the combined
  \(C_5\) symmetry.  Two further direct identities are
  \[
    R\!\left(-\frac1z\right)=-\frac1{R(z)},
    \qquad
    R(\bar z)=\overline{R(z)}.
  \]
  The first supplies a two-fold rotation perpendicular to the five-fold
  axis, and the second supplies the corresponding reflection.  Together
  with the \(C_5\) generator these combined spatial and target
  transformations generate \(D_{5\dd}\).  They are, in fact, contained in
  the full icosahedral symmetry of the map.

  Write
  \[
    p(z)=z^7-7z^2,\qquad q(z)=7z^5+1.
  \]
  With the convention \(\mathcal W=p'q-pq'\),
  \[
  \begin{split}
    \mathcal W(z)
      &=(7z^6-14z)(7z^5+1)
        -(z^7-7z^2)(35z^4)\\
      &=\boxed{14z(z^{10}+11z^5-1).}
  \end{split}
  \]
  Put \(y=z^5\).  The two values of \(y\) are
  \[
    y_\pm=\frac{-11\pm5\sqrt5}{2}.
  \]
  If \(\varphi_g=(1+\sqrt5)/2\) is the golden ratio, then
  \(y_+=\varphi_g^{-5}\) and \(y_-=-\varphi_g^5\).  The eleven finite
  zeros are therefore
  \[
    \boxed{z=0,\qquad
      z=\varphi_g^{-1}e^{2\pi \ii k/5},\qquad
      z=\varphi_g e^{(2k+1)\pi \ii/5},\qquad k=0,\ldots,4.}
  \]
  A degree-seven rational map has \(2B-2=12\) critical points on
  \(\mathbb{CP}^1\), counted with multiplicity.  Because the displayed
  polynomial has degree eleven, the twelfth zero is at
  \(z=\infty\).
