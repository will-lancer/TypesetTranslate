\section*{Notation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Notation}

% Source: physical PDF pp. 21-23, printed pp. xx-xxii.

The big issue in choosing notation for a book on supersymmetry is whether to
use a two-component or a four-component notation for spinors.  This edition
uses the two-component Weyl notation throughout the four-dimensional
chapters.  Its purpose is only to change the spinor language: the metric,
phases, normalizations, factor ordering, and physical content remain those of
the original text.

An undotted spinor \(\psi_\alpha\) transforms according to
\((1/2,0)\), while its complex conjugate
\(\bar\psi_{\dot\alpha}\) transforms according to \((0,1/2)\).  A field that
was represented by a four-component Majorana spinor in the original text is
therefore represented here by the pair
\((\psi_\alpha,\bar\psi_{\dot\alpha})\), subject to complex conjugation rather
than treated as two independent fields.  The supersymmetry generators,
superspace coordinates, and superderivatives are correspondingly written
\[
  Q_\alpha,\quad\bar Q_{\dot\alpha},\qquad
  \theta^\alpha,\quad\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha},\qquad
  D_\alpha,\quad\bar D_{\dot\alpha}.
\]
The appendix to Chapter 26 summarizes the two-component spinor identities
needed for superfields.

Here are some other features of the notation used in this book:

Latin indices \(i,j,k\), and so on generally run over the three spatial
coordinate labels, usually taken as \(1,2,3\). Where specifically indicated,
they run over values \(1,2,3,4\), with \(x^4=\ii t\).

Greek indices \(\mu,\nu\), etc.\ from the middle of the Greek alphabet
generally run over the four spacetime coordinate labels \(0,1,2,3\), with
\(x^0\) the time coordinate. Where it is necessary to distinguish between
spacetime coordinates in a general coordinate system and in a locally inertial
system, indices \(\mu,\nu\), etc.\ are used for the former and \(a,b\), etc.\
for the latter.

Undotted Greek indices \(\alpha,\beta,\ldots\) from the beginning of the
Greek alphabet run over the components of \((1/2,0)\) spinors, and dotted
indices \(\dot\alpha,\dot\beta,\ldots\) run over the components of
\((0,1/2)\) spinors.  Upper-case letters \(A,B,\ldots\) label generators of a
symmetry algebra.  In Chapter 31, lower-case Latin indices \(a,b,\ldots\)
label local Lorentz vectors. Internal Lie-algebra indices in Chapter 24 use
the lower-case Latin family \(a,b,c,\ldots\). If an adjoint index occurs
alongside a lower-case Latin component index, the non-colliding family
\(r,s,t,\ldots\) is used for the adjoint index.

When a quantity carries both a gauge-adjoint index and spacetime indices,
their opposing scripts are set together, as in
\(F^a_{\mu\nu}\) and \(A_a^\mu\);
an empty group is not inserted between the scripts.

Repeated indices are generally summed, unless otherwise indicated.

The spacetime metric \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\) is diagonal, with
\(\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)\).

The d'Alembertian is defined as
\(\Box\equiv\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial^2/\partial x^\mu\partial x^\nu
=\nabla^2-\partial^2/\partial t^2\), where \(\nabla^2\) is the Laplacian
\(\partial^2/\partial x^i\partial x^i\).

A positive energy associated with a momentum mode is denoted
\(\omega_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{k}^2+m^2}\), or \(\omega_k\) when the
momentum label is not bold. The notation \(E_k\) is reserved for a separately
defined quantity or field, not an on-shell mode energy.

The `Levi--Civita tensor' \(\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\) is defined as the
totally antisymmetric quantity with \(\epsilon^{0123}=+1\).

The two-component sigma matrices are
\[
  \sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}
  =(\mathbf{1},\sigma^1,\sigma^2,\sigma^3),
  \qquad
  \bar\sigma^{\mu\,\dot\alpha\alpha}
  =(\mathbf{1},-\sigma^1,-\sigma^2,-\sigma^3),
\]
so that, with our mostly-plus metric,
\[
  \sigma^\mu\bar\sigma^\nu+\sigma^\nu\bar\sigma^\mu
  =-2\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf{1},
  \qquad
  \bar\sigma^\mu\sigma^\nu+\bar\sigma^\nu\sigma^\mu
  =-2\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf{1}.
\]
The components of \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) are the usual Pauli matrices
\[
  \sigma_1=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 1\\
    1 & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \sigma_2=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & -\ii\\
    i & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \sigma_3=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0\\
    0 & -1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
and spinor indices are raised and lowered with
\[
  \epsilon_{12}=+1,\qquad
  \epsilon^{12}=-1,\qquad
  \psi^\alpha=\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\psi_\beta,\qquad
  \psi_\alpha=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\psi^\beta,
\]
with identical rules for dotted indices.  Our abbreviations for invariant
contractions are
\[
  \psi\chi\equiv\psi^\alpha\chi_\alpha,\qquad
  \bar\psi\bar\chi
  \equiv\bar\psi_{\dot\alpha}\bar\chi^{\dot\alpha}.
\]
For comparison with the original four-component notation, these definitions
reproduce its explicit matrices through
\[
  \gamma^\mu=-\ii
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&\sigma^\mu\\
    \bar\sigma^\mu&0
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
This comparison identity fixes phases but is not used as the working spinor
notation in Chapters 24--31.

The step function \(\theta(s)\) has the value \(+1\) for \(s>0\) and \(0\) for
\(s<0\).

The complex conjugate, transpose, and Hermitian adjoint of a matrix or vector
\(A\) are denoted \(A^*\), \(A^{\mathsf T}\), and
\(A^\dagger=(A^*)^{\mathsf T}\), respectively. We use a dagger for the
Hermitian adjoint of an operator and an asterisk for the complex conjugate of a
number. For a vector or matrix of operators, the dagger also denotes the
transpose of the matrix formed from the Hermitian adjoints of its components.
\(+\mathrm{H.c.}\) or \(+\mathrm{c.c.}\) at the end of an expression indicates
the addition of the Hermitian adjoint or complex conjugate of the foregoing
terms. A bar on a two-component spinor denotes the conjugate dotted spinor;
it is not a Dirac adjoint.

States use Dirac notation. In particular, asymptotic states are written
\(\InKet{\alpha}\) and \(\OutKet{\beta}\); the helper macros place the
\(\mathrm{in}\) and \(\mathrm{out}\) labels outside the ket or bra.

Units are used with \(\hbar\) and the speed of light taken to be unity.
Throughout, \(-e\) is the rationalized charge of the electron, so that the
fine structure constant is \(\alpha=e^2/4\pi\simeq1/137\). Temperatures are in
energy units, with the Boltzmann constant taken equal to unity.

Numbers in parenthesis at the end of quoted numerical data give the
uncertainty in the last digits of the quoted figure. Where not otherwise
indicated, experimental data are taken from `Review of Particle Physics,' The
Particle Data Group, \emph{European Physics Journal C} \textbf{3}, 1 (1998).
