\subsection{The Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model}

In the previous section we identified two different ways that the breakdown
of supersymmetry at a high energy scale \(M_S\) could be communicated to
the known quarks and leptons: through gauge or gravitational superfields.
The supersymmetry-breaking terms in the resulting low-energy effective
Lagrangian will then be suppressed by powers of gauge couplings or the
Newton constant. Most of these terms will therefore be rather small, with
the exception that, along with factors of gauge couplings or the Newton
constant, on dimensional grounds the mass terms and other
superrenormalizable terms in the effective Lagrangian will be proportional
to one or more factors of the supersymmetry-breaking scale \(M_S\), which
is quite large compared with known particle masses. We can conclude then
that, to a fair approximation for gauge-mediated supersymmetry breaking
and a very good approximation for gravitationally mediated supersymmetry
breaking, the main effect of supersymmetry breaking will be in the
superrenormalizable terms of the effective Lagrangian of the supersymmetric
standard model. This version of the standard
model~\hyperref[ch28-ref-10]{[10]}, which is supersymmetric except for
superrenormalizable terms, is usually known as the \emph{minimal
supersymmetric standard model}.

With \(R\) parity or \(B-L\) conserved, the most general
superrenormalizable Lagrangian density allowed by
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge symmetry takes
the form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}={}&
  -\sum_{ij}M_{ij}^{2Q}
    \bigl(\mathcal{Q}_i^\dagger\mathcal{Q}_j\bigr)
  -\sum_{ij}M_{ij}^{2\bar U}
    \bigl(\overline{\mathcal{U}}_i^\dagger
          \overline{\mathcal{U}}_j\bigr)
  -\sum_{ij}M_{ij}^{2\bar D}
    \bigl(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_i^\dagger
          \overline{\mathcal{D}}_j\bigr)
  \\
  &-\sum_{ij}M_{ij}^{2L}
    \bigl(\mathcal{L}_i^\dagger\mathcal{L}_j\bigr)
  -\sum_{ij}M_{ij}^{2\bar E}
    \bigl(\overline{\mathcal{E}}_i^\dagger
          \overline{\mathcal{E}}_j\bigr)
  \\
  &-2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[
    m_{\mathrm{gluino}}\lambda_3^\alpha\lambda_{3\alpha}\right]
  -2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[
    m_{\mathrm{wino}}\lambda_2^\alpha\lambda_{2\alpha}\right]
  -2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[
    m_{\mathrm{bino}}\lambda_1^\alpha\lambda_{1\alpha}\right]
  \\
  &-\sum_{ij}A_{ij}^{D}h_{ij}^{D}
    \bigl(\mathcal{Q}_i^{\mathrm T}e\mathcal{H}_1\bigr)
    \overline{\mathcal{D}}_j
  -\sum_{ij}A_{ij}^{E}h_{ij}^{E}
    \bigl(\mathcal{L}_i^{\mathrm T}e\mathcal{H}_1\bigr)
    \overline{\mathcal{E}}_j
  \\
  &-\sum_{ij}A_{ij}^{U}h_{ij}^{U}
    \bigl(\mathcal{Q}_i^{\mathrm T}e\mathcal{H}_2\bigr)
    \overline{\mathcal{U}}_j
  -\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{D}h_{ij}^{D}
    \bigl(\mathcal{Q}_i^{\mathrm T}\mathcal{H}_2^*\bigr)
    \overline{\mathcal{D}}_j
  \\
  &-\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{E}h_{ij}^{E}
    \bigl(\mathcal{L}_i^{\mathrm T}\mathcal{H}_2^*\bigr)
    \overline{\mathcal{E}}_j
  -\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{U}h_{ij}^{U}
    \bigl(\mathcal{Q}_i^{\mathrm T}\mathcal{H}_1^*\bigr)
    \overline{\mathcal{U}}_j
  \\
  &-B\mu\bigl(\mathcal{H}_2^{\mathrm T}e\mathcal{H}_1\bigr)
  +\mathrm{H.c.}
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.4.1}\label{eq:28.4.1}
\end{equation}
Script letters are used here to denote the scalar components of
left-chiral superfields. Sums over \(SU(2)\) and color indices are
understood, as necessary for invariance under
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\), with \(e\) the usual
antisymmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(i\sigma_2\). All coefficients
may be complex, and each gaugino mass is correspondingly an arbitrary
complex Weyl mass.

We follow the custom here of writing the coefficients of the terms
involving scalar fields but not their adjoints as equal to the
coefficients of the corresponding supersymmetric \(\mathcal{F}\)-terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.1.7} times factors \(A_{ij}^{D}\), \(A_{ij}^{E}\),
\(A_{ij}^{U}\), and \(B\). This is motivated by the consideration that
the smallness of the Yukawa couplings of the light quarks in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.1.7} reflects a number of approximate chiral symmetries
that if extended to the whole supermultiplet would also make the
corresponding trilinear terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.4.1} small, while the
appearance of the \(\mu\)-term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.1.7} violates a
possible Peccei--Quinn symmetry~\hyperref[ch28-ref-4]{[4]} that if
approximately valid would make both \(\mu\) and \(B\mu\) small. Similar
considerations suggest the form in which we write the coefficients of the
terms that involve both scalars and their complex conjugates. Also, in
Section 31.4 we will describe contributions to \(Ah\) and \(B\mu\) that
really are proportional to \(h\) and \(\mu\), respectively. However, here
we are leaving it an open question whether the \(Ah\), \(Ch\), and
\(B\mu\) coefficients in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.4.1} are necessarily small
when the corresponding \(h\) and \(\mu\) coefficients in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.1.7} are small.

The \(Ch\)-terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.4.1} have generally been omitted in
discussions of the minimal supersymmetric standard model. This is partly
because, as discussed in Section 27.7, terms like these that involve the
\(\phi\)-components of left-chiral scalar superfields and also their
complex conjugates can potentially produce quadratic divergences and
thereby raise fine-tuning problems. But we saw in Section 27.7 that the
quadratic divergences occur only in `tadpole' graphs in which a scalar
field line disappears into the vacuum, and in the minimum supersymmetric
standard model there are no scalars that are neutral under all gauge
symmetries, and hence no scalar tadpoles. The \(Ch\)-terms are absent in
the theories of gravitationally mediated supersymmetry breaking discussed
in Section 31.6, and small in the theories of gauge-mediated supersymmetry
breaking described in Section 28.6, but there is no reason to suppose that
this will always be the case.

Even though they are not supersymmetric, superrenormalizable interactions
like those in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.4.1} are shown in Section 27.7 to not
produce supersymmetry-violating ultraviolet-divergent corrections to the
coefficients of the supersymmetric \(d=4\) interactions. The condition of
supersymmetry that is imposed on the dimensionless couplings of the
minimal supersymmetric standard model therefore does not get in the way
of the cancellation of ultraviolet divergences by renormalization of
coupling constants. It was this property, rather than any theory of
supersymmetry breaking in a high energy hidden sector, that motivated the
introduction of the minimal supersymmetric standard model in
Reference~\hyperref[ch28-ref-10]{10}.

The best reason today for studying the implications of the supersymmetric
standard model is that, as already mentioned, theories in which
supersymmetry is spontaneously broken at a high energy scale are naturally
described by the minimal supersymmetric standard model at much lower
energies. We can explore the phenomenological implications of the
supersymmetric standard model, and be reasonably confident that the
results will be relevant whatever detailed model of supersymmetry breaking
and its mediation turns out to be correct.

Even without the \(Ch\)-terms, if all of the other coefficients in the
Lagrangian are constrained only by gauge symmetries and \(R\) parity
conservation, the minimal supersymmetric standard model will contain over
100 free parameters~\hyperref[ch28-ref-11]{[11]}. Here `minimal' means
nothing more than that the theory contains only a minimal menu of
superfields. Sometimes the term `minimal supersymmetric standard model' is
reserved for models that also satisfy restrictions on the coefficients of
the superrenormalizable terms, motivated either by some underlying theory
or by empirical constraints. For instance, the minimal supersymmetric
standard model is sometimes optimistically assumed to satisfy the
universality conditions
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  M_{ij}^{2Q}=M_{ij}^{2\bar D}=M_{ij}^{2\bar U}
  =M_{ij}^{2L}=M_{ij}^{2\bar E}=M^2\delta_{ij}\,,
  \\
  m_{\mathrm{gluino}}=m_{\mathrm{wino}}=m_{\mathrm{bino}}\,,
  \\
  A_{ij}^{D}=A_{ij}^{E}=A_{ij}^{U}=A\,,
  \qquad
  C_{ij}^{D}=C_{ij}^{E}=C_{ij}^{U}=0\,.
  \end{gathered}
  \tag{28.4.2}\label{eq:28.4.2}
\end{equation}
Often these conditions are imposed at the scale
\(M_X\approx10^{16}\ \mathrm{GeV}\) of coupling constant unification,
with corrections produced only by the renormalization group flow to lower
energies. We will not be making such assumptions here.

In analyzing the phenomenological implications of the minimal
supersymmetric standard model, we must deal not only with the search for
new particles, but also with two classes of severe empirical constraint on
processes involving known particles: the experimental upper bounds on
various flavor non-conserving processes, and on various modes of CP
non-conservation.

\subsubsection*{Flavor Changing Processes}

We saw in Section 21.3 that there is an automatic suppression of
flavor-changing processes like \(K^0\)-\(\bar K^0\) oscillations and
\(K^0\longrightarrow\mu^+\mu^-\) in the non-supersymmetric standard
model. This is due to the feature of this theory, that it is only the mass
splittings of the quarks that prevent them from being defined so that each
flavor is separately conserved, so the amplitude of these flavor-changing
process must be proportional to several factors of small quark masses.
Also, in this theory lepton flavor is automatically conserved, so that
processes like \(\mu\longrightarrow e\gamma\) are absolutely forbidden.
These satisfactory results are put at risk by the presence of squarks and
sleptons in supersymmetric extensions of the standard model, because there
is in general no reason to expect that the squark and slepton mass matrices
will be diagonal in the same basis as the quark and lepton mass matrices.
This does not introduce flavor changing in the interaction of these
particles with gauge bosons, which are flavor-independent, but it can
produce flavor-changing transitions in which squarks or sleptons turn into
quarks or leptons with the emission or absorption of gauginos. Of course,
there

The most stringent limits on squark mass splittings and/or mixing angles
are set by measurements of \(K^0\)-\(\bar K^0\)
transitions~\hyperref[ch28-ref-12]{[12]}. These transitions are produced
by operators in the effective low-energy Lagrangian density like
\(-(\bar s\bar\sigma^\mu d)(\bar s\bar\sigma_\mu d)\), which can be
produced by diagrams like Figure~\ref{fig:28.1}. The superpartners of the
quarks \(d_L\) and \(s_L\) are in general linear combinations
\(\sum_iV_{di}\mathcal{D}_i\) and \(\sum_iV_{si}\mathcal{D}_i\) of
squarks \(\mathcal{D}_i\) of definite mass, where \(V_{ji}\) is a
\(3\times3\) unitary matrix, so the two squark propagators in
this diagram contribute a factor
\[
  \sum_i\frac{V_{di}V_{si}^*}{k^2+M_i^2-i\epsilon}
  \mathbin{\cdot}
  \sum_j\frac{V_{dj}V_{sj}^*}{k^2+M_j^2-i\epsilon}\,,
\]
where \(k\) is the four-momentum circulating in the loop. Because
\(V_{ji}\) is unitary, this vanishes if the three squark masses \(M_i\)
are all equal. If the squark square masses differ from some common value
\(M_{\mathrm{squark}}^2\) by relatively small amounts \(\Delta M_i^2\),
then this becomes
\[
  \left(
    \frac{1}{k^2+M_{\mathrm{squark}}^2-i\epsilon}
  \right)^{\!4}
  \left(
    \sum_iV_{di}V_{si}^*\Delta M_i^2
  \right)^{\!2}.
\]
The amplitude for
\(d_L\bar{s}_L\longrightarrow s_L\bar{d}_L\) has dimensionality
mass\(^{-2}\), so after multiplying by the gluino propagators and four
factors of the strong coupling \(g_s\), and integrating over \(k\), we
must get an amplitude proportional to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{g_s^4}{\widetilde{M}^{\,6}}
  \left(
    \sum_iV_{di}V_{si}^*\Delta M_i^2
  \right)^{\!2},
  \tag{28.4.3}\label{eq:28.4.3}
\end{equation}
where \(\widetilde{M}\) is the larger of \(M_{\mathrm{squark}}\) and
\(m_{\mathrm{gluino}}\). This may be compared with the result for this
amplitude in the non-supersymmetric standard model, which is produced by
\(W\) exchange, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:28.2}. Ignoring the third
generation of quarks, which has only small transition amplitudes to the
first two generations, the amplitudes for \(d\longrightarrow u\),
\(d\longrightarrow c\), \(s\longrightarrow u\) and
\(s\longrightarrow c\) by \(W^-\) emission are, respectively,
\(\cos\theta_c\), \(-\sin\theta_c\), \(\sin\theta_c\), and
\(\cos\theta_c\), where \(\theta_c\) is the Cabibbo angle defined in
Section 21.3. Hence in place of the squark propagators here we have the
quark propagators, written in undotted--dotted block form,
\[
  \sin\theta_c\cos\theta_c
  \left(
    \frac{1}{k^2+m_u^2-i\epsilon}
    \begin{pmatrix}
      m_u\delta_\alpha{}^\beta
        &k_\mu\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}\\
      k_\mu\bar\sigma^{\mu\dot\alpha\beta}
        &m_u\delta^{\dot\alpha}{}_{\dot\beta}
    \end{pmatrix}
    -
    \frac{1}{k^2+m_c^2-i\epsilon}
    \begin{pmatrix}
      m_c\delta_\alpha{}^\beta
        &k_\mu\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}\\
      k_\mu\bar\sigma^{\mu\dot\alpha\beta}
        &m_c\delta^{\dot\alpha}{}_{\dot\beta}
    \end{pmatrix}
  \right),
\]
and in place of the strong coupling \(g_s\) here we have the \(SU(2)\)
coupling \(g\). Thus in the non-supersymmetric standard model the
amplitude for
\(d_L\bar{s}_L\longrightarrow s_L\bar{d}_L\) is proportional to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{g^4\sin^2\theta_c\cos^2\theta_c}{m_W^4}
  (m_c-m_u)^2\,,
  \tag{28.4.4}\label{eq:28.4.4}
\end{equation}
with a proportionality coefficient of the same order as that in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.4.3}. With a plausible guess on how to calculate the
\(K^0\)-\(\bar K^0\) transition amplitude from the amplitude for
\(d_L\bar{s}_L\longrightarrow s_L\bar{d}_L\), the amplitude
corresponding to Figure~\ref{fig:28.2} is known to give a result in good
agreement with experiment. (Indeed, Gaillard and
Lee~\hyperref[ch28-ref-13]{[13]} used this calculation to predict that
\(m_c\approx1.5\ \mathrm{GeV}\), before the \(c\) quark was discovered.)
It therefore seems reasonable to require that the squark exchange result
\eqref{eq:28.4.3} should be less than the quark exchange result
\eqref{eq:28.4.4}. This yields the condition
\begin{equation}
  \left|
    \sum_iV_{di}V_{si}^*\frac{\Delta M_i^2}{\widetilde{M}^{\,2}}
  \right|
  <
  \frac{g^2}{g_s^2}\sin\theta_c\cos\theta_c
  \frac{(m_c-m_u)\widetilde{M}}{m_W^2}\,.
  \tag{28.4.5}\label{eq:28.4.5}
\end{equation}
Taking \(g^2/4\pi=0.036\), \(g_s^2/4\pi=0.118\),
\(\sin\theta_c=0.22\), \(m_W=80.4\ \mathrm{GeV}\),
\(m_c=1.5\ \mathrm{GeV}\), and \(m_u\ll m_c\), we find
\begin{equation}
  \left|
    \sum_iV_{di}V_{si}^*\frac{\Delta M_i^2}{\widetilde{M}^{\,2}}
  \right|
  <1.5\mathbin{\cdot}10^{-3}
  \mathbin{\cdot}
  \bigl(\widetilde{M}/100\ \mathrm{GeV}\bigr).
  \tag{28.4.6}\label{eq:28.4.6}
\end{equation}
The squark masses are unlikely to be much less than
\(m_{\mathrm{gluino}}\), so we can conclude that either the squark masses
are split by no more than about one part in \(10^3\), or the non-diagonal
terms of the mixing matrix \(V_{ji}\) are less than about \(10^{-3}\), or
the squarks are heavier than about \(10\ \mathrm{TeV}\), or we have some
combination of nearly degenerate squarks, nearly zero mixing angles, and
heavy squarks. In itself, this result only constrains the superpartners
\(\mathcal{D}_i\) of the left-handed quarks of charge \(-e/3\), but
similar limits on the masses and mixing angles of the
\(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_i\) squarks can be obtained by considering the
amplitude for
\(d_R\bar{s}_R\longrightarrow s_R\bar{d}_R\). We can also obtain
somewhat weaker

Just as for the squarks, the sleptons of definite mass are expected to be
non-diagonal linear combinations of the superpartners of the leptons. This
leads to the decay process \(\mu\longrightarrow e+\gamma\) through

appears without needing to impose such symmetries.

information about known particles has to do with CP-violating effects,

electron~\hyperref[ch28-ref-15]{[15]}. In Section 21.3 we saw that these
effects are rather weak in the non-supersymmetric standard model with only
one scalar doublet, apart from a potential problem with the parameter
\(\theta\) of quantum chromodynamics, discussed in

To take one example, consider the quark chromoelectric dipole moments,
which in some models make the largest contribution to the electric dipole
moment of the neutron. The CP-violating chromoelectric dipole moment
operator is
\(-4\left[
    q^c\sigma_{\mu\nu}\lambda_aq
    -\bar q\bar\sigma_{\mu\nu}\lambda_a\bar q^{\,c}

\(f_a^{\mu\nu}\) is the \(SU(3)\) field-strength tensor, and
\(\lambda_a\) are the \(3\mathbin{\times}3\) generators of \(SU(3)\)).
Since each undotted tensor bilinear here pairs \(q\) with \(q^c\), and the
dotted term is its conjugate, in
order for a one-loop graphs to contribute to the chromoelectric dipole
moment an external left-handed \(u\) or \(d\) quark line must emit an
internal gluino line and turn into a \(\mathcal{U}\) or \(\mathcal{D}\)
squark line, which next turns into a
\(\overline{\mathcal{U}}^*\) or \(\overline{\mathcal{D}}^*\) squark
line, and then into a right-handed \(u\) or \(d\) quark line by absorbing
the internal gluino line, with the external gluon line attached either to
the internal gluino line or to one of the internal squark lines. (See
Figure~\ref{fig:28.4}.)

To calculate this, we need to know the mixing of the scalar components
\(\mathcal{U}_i\) (or \(\mathcal{D}_i\)) of the left-chiral quark
superfields \(Q_i\) with the complex conjugates
\(\overline{\mathcal{U}}_j^*\) (or
\(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_j^*\)) of the scalar components of the
left-chiral antiquark superfields \(\bar U_j\) (or \(\bar D_j\)),
produced by the spontaneous breakdown of
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\), and represented by the X in

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{Q}\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{H}}
  =
  -\left|
    \sum_{ij}h_{ij}^{U}\mathcal{U}_i\overline{\mathcal{U}}_j
    +\mu\mathcal{H}_1^0
  \right|^2
  -\left|
    \sum_{ij}h_{ij}^{D}\mathcal{D}_i\overline{\mathcal{D}}_j
    +\mu\mathcal{H}_2^0
  \right|^2.
  \tag{28.4.7}\label{eq:28.4.7}
\end{equation}
There is also a contribution from the \(A\)- and \(C\)-terms in

\begin{equation}

  \mathcal{L}'_{\mathcal{Q}\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{H}}
  ={}&
  -\sum_{ij}h_{ij}^{D}\mathcal{D}_i\overline{\mathcal{D}}_j
  \left[
    -A_{ij}^{D}\mathcal{H}_1^0+C_{ij}^{D}\mathcal{H}_2^{0*}
  \right]
  \\
  &-\sum_{ij}h_{ij}^{U}\mathcal{U}_i\overline{\mathcal{U}}_j
  \left[
    A_{ij}^{U}\mathcal{H}_2^0+C_{ij}^{U}\mathcal{H}_1^{0*}
  \right]
  -\mathrm{H.c.}
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.4.8}\label{eq:28.4.8}
\end{equation}
Replacing the neutral Higgs scalar fields with their expectation values
gives the quadratic terms
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{Q}\overline{\mathcal{Q}}}
  ={}&
  -2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{ij}
    m_{ij}^{U}\mathcal{U}_i\overline{\mathcal{U}}_j
    \left(
      \mu^*\cot\beta+A_{ij}^{U}+C_{ij}^{U}\cot\beta
    \right)
  \\

    m_{ij}^{D}\mathcal{D}_i\overline{\mathcal{D}}_j
    \left(
      \mu^*(\tan\beta)^*+A_{ij}^{D}-C_{ij}^{D}(\tan\beta)^*

  \tag{28.4.9}\label{eq:28.4.9}
\end{equation}
where \(m_{ij}^{U}=\langle\mathcal{H}_2^0\rangle h_{ij}^{U}\) and
\(m_{ij}^{D}=-\langle\mathcal{H}_1^0\rangle h_{ij}^{D}\) are the mass
matrices of the quarks of charge \(2e/3\) and \(-e/3\), and
\begin{equation}
  \tan\beta\equiv
  \frac{\langle\mathcal{H}_2^0\rangle}
       {\langle\mathcal{H}_1^0\rangle^*}.
  \tag{28.4.10}\label{eq:28.4.10}
\end{equation}

\(C\)s diagonal, Figure~\ref{fig:28.4} makes contributions to the

\begin{equation}
  d_u^{ce}
  =
  \frac{g_s^3}{16\pi^2}
  \operatorname{Im}\left[
    m_uA'_u
    I(m_{\mathcal{U}},m_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}},
      m_{\mathrm{gluino}})
  \right],
  \tag{28.4.11}\label{eq:28.4.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  d_d^{ce}
  =
  \frac{g_s^3}{16\pi^2}
  \operatorname{Im}\left[
    m_dA'_d
    I(m_{\mathcal{D}},m_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},
      m_{\mathrm{gluino}})
  \right],
  \tag{28.4.12}\label{eq:28.4.12}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  A'_u\equiv(\mu^*+C_u)\cot\beta+A_u\,,
  \qquad
  A'_d\equiv(\mu^*-C_d)(\tan\beta)^*+A_d\,,
  \tag{28.4.13}\label{eq:28.4.13}
\end{equation}
and \(I\) is a complicated dimensionless function of its arguments arising
from the integration over the virtual four-momentum. For
\(m_{\mathcal{Q}}\simeq m_{\overline{\mathcal{Q}}}\) and the gluino
field defined to make \(m_{\mathrm{gluino}}\) as well as
\(m_{\mathcal{Q}}\) real, the function \(I\) takes the form
\begin{equation}
  I(m_{\mathcal{Q}},m_{\mathcal{Q}},m_{\mathrm{gluino}})
  =
  m_{\mathrm{gluino}}^{-3}
  J\left(
    \frac{m_{\mathrm{gluino}}^2}
         {m_{\mathcal{Q}}^2-m_{\mathrm{gluino}}^2}
  \right),
  \tag{28.4.14}\label{eq:28.4.14}
\end{equation}
where~\hyperref[ch28-ref-21]{[21]}
\begin{equation}
  J(z)
  =
  2\left(-z^4+\frac43z^3+z^2\right)
    \ln\left(\frac{1+z}{z}\right)
  +2z^3-\frac{11}{3}z^2.
  \tag{28.4.15}\label{eq:28.4.15}
\end{equation}

The hard part of this sort of calculation always lies in estimating the
contribution of an operator like the chromoelectric dipole interaction to
hadronic matrix elements such as the electric dipole moment of the
neutron. We can anticipate that there will be renormalization group
corrections required because this operator is to be used at energies of
the order of the neutron mass, rather than the masses of the squarks and
gluino. More important is simply getting dimensional factors and factors
of \(4\pi\) right. For this purpose, it is usual to use a counting
rule~\hyperref[ch28-ref-22]{[22]} known as `naive dimensional analysis.'
A connected graph with \(V_i\) vertices of type \(i\) and \(I\) internal
lines will have a number \(L\) of loops given by
\(L=I-\sum_iV_i+1\). If there are \(N_i\) lines attached to a vertex of
type \(i\) and \(N\) external lines of the whole graph, then
\(2I+N=\sum_iV_iN_i\), so
\[
  L=1-\frac{N}{2}
  +\sum_iV_i\left(\frac{N_i}{2}-1\right).
\]
We expect a factor of order \(1/16\pi^2\) for each loop, so the coefficient
of an operator \(\mathcal{O}\) in the low-energy effective Lagrangian with
\(N\) field factors will contain an over-all factor
\[
  (4\pi)^{N-2}
  \prod_i(4\pi)^{(2-N_i)V_i}.
\]
If the operator \(\mathcal{O}\) has dimensionality \(d\) and the
interactions \(\mathcal{O}_i\) of type \(i\) have dimensionality \(d_i\),
then the coefficient of \(\mathcal{O}\) will have dimensionality
\(4-d-\sum_i(4-d_i)\), so this coefficient will also have a factor
\(M^{4-d}\prod_iM^{d_i-4}\), where \(M\) is some scale that is typical
of hadronic physics, such as the nucleon mass or the energy
\(2\pi F_\pi\simeq1200\ \mathrm{MeV}\), where the low-energy expansions
discussed in Section 19.5 begin to break down. Finally, the contribution
to the coefficient of \(\mathcal{O}\) from some graph will of course be
proportional to the couplings of all the operators \(\mathcal{O}_i\)
associated with the vertices in the graph. These remarks can be
conveniently summarized by defining a `reduced coupling': the reduced
coupling associated with any operator \(\mathcal{O}_i\) having \(N_i\)
field factors, dimensionality \(d_i\), and coupling constant \(g_i\) is
\begin{equation}
  g_i^{\mathrm{reduced}}
  \equiv
  g_i(4\pi)^{2-N_i}M^{d_i-4}.
  \tag{28.4.16}\label{eq:28.4.16}
\end{equation}
The above estimates suggest the rule of naive dimensional analysis: the
reduced coupling of any operator \(\mathcal{O}\) in the effective hadronic
Lagrangian is roughly equal to the product of the reduced couplings of the
interactions that contribute to this effective coupling.

The neutron electric dipole moment is the coefficient of an operator with
one photon and two neutron fields and dimensionality 5, so its reduced
coupling is \(Md_n^e/4\pi\). Likewise, the quark chromoelectric magnetic
moment has reduced coupling \(Md_q^{ce}/4\pi\). In addition to one factor
of this reduced coupling, the reduced coupling of the neutron electric
dipole operator must have a factor of the reduced coupling \(e/4\pi\) of
the electromagnetic coupling and an indeterminate number of factors of
the reduced strong coupling \(g_s/4\pi\), which at the low-energy scale
\(M\) are not very different from unity and will be ignored. Taking the
\(d\) quark contribution as representative of the contributions of both
\(u\) and \(d\) quarks, the result then is that
\begin{equation}
  d_n^e
  \approx
  \frac{ed_d^{ce}}{4\pi}
  \approx
  e\left(\frac{g_s}{4\pi}\right)^{\!3}
  \operatorname{Im}\left[m_dA'_d\right]
  I(m_{\mathcal{D}},m_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},
    m_{\mathrm{gluino}}).
  \tag{28.4.17}\label{eq:28.4.17}
\end{equation}
Further simplifying by setting
\(m_{\mathrm{gluino}}\simeq m_{\mathcal{D}}
\simeq m_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\), so that \(J=7/18\), and taking
\(g_s^2/4\pi\) at the scale of squark and gluino masses to have the same
value 0.12 as at \(m_Z\), and \(\lvert m_d\rvert\approx7\ \mathrm{MeV}\),
we have then
\begin{equation}
  \lvert d_n^e\rvert
  \approx
  0.5\mathbin{\cdot}10^{-23}\,e\ \mathrm{cm}\,
  \frac{\lvert A'_d\rvert\lvert\sin\varphi\rvert
        \mathbin{\cdot}(100\ \mathrm{GeV})^2}
       {m_{\mathrm{gluino}}^3},
  \tag{28.4.18}\label{eq:28.4.18}
\end{equation}
where \(\varphi\) is the phase of \(A'_d\), with the convention that the
gluino, quark, and squark masses are taken real. The contribution of the
electric dipole moment of the quark is somewhat larger, while the
contribution of the purely gluonic CP-odd operator is considerably
smaller~\hyperref[ch28-ref-23]{[23]}.

To avoid conflict with the experimental upper bound of
\(0.97\mathbin{\cdot}10^{-25}\,e\ \mathrm{cm}\), either the phases
associated with CP violation in the supersymmetric standard model must be
less than about \(10^{-2}\), or some of the new particles of this model
must be heavier than about \(1\ \mathrm{TeV}\). Similar conclusions have
been reached from calculations of the electric dipole moments of atoms
and molecules~\hyperref[ch28-ref-23]{[23]}. Even more stringent conditions
on CP-violating phases have been
derived~\hyperref[ch28-ref-24]{[24]} by considering the contribution of
Figure~\ref{fig:28.1} to the one precisely measured CP-violating effect,
the imaginary part of the amplitude for \(K^0\)-\(\bar K^0\) oscillation.
