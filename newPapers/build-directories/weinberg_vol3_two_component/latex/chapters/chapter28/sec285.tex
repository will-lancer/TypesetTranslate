\subsection{The Sector of Zero Baryon and Lepton Number}

Despite the large number of parameters of the supersymmetric standard
model, in some contexts it is surprisingly predictive. This is true in
particular when we consider the scalar fields whose vacuum expectation
values spontaneously break the \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge symmetry.
In this section we will consider these scalars, along with other fields of
zero baryon and lepton number: neutral scalars odd under charge conjugation,
charged scalars, and the fermionic superpartners of these scalars and of the
\(W^\pm\) and \(Z^0\).

It is a crucial requirement for supersymmetric versions of the standard
model that they should contain scalar doublet `Higgs' superfields with the
right mass and interaction parameters to account for the breakdown of the
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge group of the electromagnetic and weak
interactions. We saw in Section 28.1 that at least two left-chiral scalar
doublets are needed to give mass to the quarks of both charge \(2e/3\) and
\(-e/3\) and the charged leptons, while we found in Section 28.2 that two
doublets are just what is needed to bring the \(SU(3)\), \(SU(2)\), and
\(U(1)\) gauge couplings together at some very high energy. We have
therefore assumed that there are two left-chiral scalar \(SU(2)\) doublets
\begin{equation}
  H_1=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    H_1^0\\
    H_1^-
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  H_2=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    H_2^+\\
    H_2^0
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{28.5.1}\label{eq:28.5.1}
\end{equation}

These have \(SU(2)\) and \(U(1)\) \(D\)-terms \eqref{eq:27.4.7} given
(assuming zero Fayet--Iliopoulos constant \(\xi_{U(1)}\)) by
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol D
  =\frac{g}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\boldsymbol{\tau}\mathcal{H}_1\right)
  +\frac{g}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\boldsymbol{\tau}\mathcal{H}_2\right),
  \tag{28.5.2}\label{eq:28.5.2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  D_y
  =\frac{g'}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
  -\frac{g'}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right),
  \tag{28.5.3}\label{eq:28.5.3}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{H}_{1,2}\) are the scalar components of the superfield
doublets \(H_{1,2}\) and \(\tau_r\) are the Pauli matrices, with
\(\tau_r^2=\mathbf{1}\). As shown in Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.4.9}, in
renormalizable theories this gives the \(D\)-term contribution to the
scalar field potential
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V_D
  &=\frac12\boldsymbol D^2+\frac12D_y^2\\
  &=\frac{g^2}{8}
    \left[
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\boldsymbol{\tau}\mathcal{H}_1\right)
      +\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\boldsymbol{\tau}\mathcal{H}_2\right)
    \right]^2
    +\frac{g'^2}{8}
    \left[
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
      -\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)
    \right]^2 .
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.4}\label{eq:28.5.4}
\end{equation}
This can be put in a more convenient form by using the relation
\begin{equation}
  (\boldsymbol{\tau})_{i\ell}\mathbin{\cdot}
  (\boldsymbol{\tau})_{kj}
  =2\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}-\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{kj}.
  \tag{28.5.5}\label{eq:28.5.5}
\end{equation}
(To prove this, use rotational invariance to show that
\(\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}\) may be expressed as a linear combination of
\((\boldsymbol{\tau})_{i\ell}\mathbin{\cdot}
(\boldsymbol{\tau})_{kj}\) and \(\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{kj}\), and calculate
the coefficients by taking traces on the indices \(i,j\) and \(i,\ell\).)
In this way, we may rewrite the \(D\)-term part of the scalar field
potential as
\begin{equation}
  V_D
  =\frac{g^2}{2}\left|\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right|^2
  +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{8}
    \left[
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
      -\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)
    \right]^2 .
  \tag{28.5.6}\label{eq:28.5.6}
\end{equation}

As mentioned in Section 28.1, there is just one possible renormalizable
term in the superpotential for these two left-chiral doublets, of the form
\begin{equation}
  f(H_1,H_2)=\mu\left(H_1^{\mathsf T}eH_2\right),
  \tag{28.5.7}\label{eq:28.5.7}
\end{equation}
where \(\mu\) is a constant with the dimensions of a mass and \(e\) is the
antisymmetric matrix \(\ii\tau_2\). According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.4.9},
this gives an additional contribution to the scalar field potential
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V_\mu
  &=
    \sum_r\left|
      \frac{\partial f(\mathcal{H}_1,\mathcal{H}_2)}
           {\partial\mathcal{H}_{1r}}
    \right|^2
    +\sum_r\left|
      \frac{\partial f(\mathcal{H}_1,\mathcal{H}_2)}
           {\partial\mathcal{H}_{2r}}
    \right|^2\\
  &=|\mu|^2
    \left[
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
      +\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)
    \right].
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.8}\label{eq:28.5.8}
\end{equation}
For \(\mu\neq0\) the potential \(V_D+V_\mu\) evidently has a minimum value
of zero, reached at the unique point
\(\mathcal{H}_1=\mathcal{H}_2=0\). With just these terms in the potential,
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) as well as supersymmetry is not spontaneously
broken. (The case \(\mu=0\) is not much better; there is a continuous
infinity of vacuum states with supersymmetry unbroken and with
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) broken down to electromagnetic gauge
invariance with all possible strengths, including zero.) This is one more
example of the general difficulty, already seen in Section 28.3, of
formulating realistic theories in which supersymmetry is spontaneously
broken within the standard model.

Under the assumption of the previous section that supersymmetry is violated
in the effective Lagrangian only by superrenormalizable terms, the most
general such supersymmetry-breaking term involving the scalar doublets is
of the form
\[
  V_m
  =m_1^2\left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
  +m_2^2\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)
  +\operatorname{Re}\left\{
    B\mu\left(\mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2\right)
  \right\},
\]
where \(m_1^2\) and \(m_2^2\) are real parameters (not necessarily
positive) and \(B\mu\) is a parameter of arbitrary phase. We will adjust
the over-all phase of the superfields \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) so that \(B\mu\)
is real and positive, and so
\begin{equation}
  V_m
  =m_1^2\left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
  +m_2^2\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)
  +B\mu\operatorname{Re}
    \left(\mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2\right).
  \tag{28.5.9}\label{eq:28.5.9}
\end{equation}
The total scalar potential in the tree approximation is then
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V
  &=V_D+V_\mu+V_m\\
  &=\frac{g^2}{2}
    \left|\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right|^2
    +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{8}
    \left[
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
      -\left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)
    \right]^2\\
  &\quad
    +(m_1^2+|\mu|^2)
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1\right)
    +(m_2^2+|\mu|^2)
      \left(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2\right)\\
  &\quad
    +B\mu\operatorname{Re}
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2\right).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.10}\label{eq:28.5.10}
\end{equation}
Note in particular that \(\mu^2\), \(m_1^2\), and \(m_2^2\) only appear
in the combinations \(m_1^2+|\mu|^2\) and \(m_2^2+|\mu|^2\).

There is one condition on the supersymmetry-breaking parameters \(m_i^2\),
derived from the requirement that the potential should be bounded below.
For scalar fields going to infinity in generic directions, the potential
is dominated by the quartic terms \(V_D\), which are positive. There are
special directions in which \(V_D\) vanishes: those for which (up to an
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge transformation)
\[
  \mathcal{H}_1=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \phi\\
    0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{H}_2=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0\\
    \phi
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
with \(\phi\) an arbitrary complex quantity. For such directions,
\(V=(2|\mu|^2+m_1^2+m_2^2)|\phi|^2-B\mu\operatorname{Re}(\phi^2)\), so (since \(B\mu\)
has been defined to be positive) in order for this not to go to
\(-\infty\) as \(|\phi|\longrightarrow\infty\), it is necessary that
\begin{equation}
  2|\mu|^2+m_1^2+m_2^2\geq B\mu.
  \tag{28.5.11}\label{eq:28.5.11}
\end{equation}

We wish to look for a minimum of the potential at which electromagnetic
gauge invariance is not broken, so let us consider the behavior of the
potential as a function of the neutral scalar fields, with the charged
scalar fields set equal to zero. In this case Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.10}
gives the potential of the neutral scalars as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V^{\mathrm N}
  ={}&\frac{g^2+g'^2}{8}
    \left[
      |\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2-|\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2
    \right]^2
    +(m_1^2+|\mu|^2)|\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2\\
  &+(m_2^2+|\mu|^2)|\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2
    -B\mu\operatorname{Re}
      \left(\mathcal{H}_1^0\mathcal{H}_2^0\right).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.12}\label{eq:28.5.12}
\end{equation}
To find a stationary point, we expand \(V^{\mathrm N}\) around the constant
values \(\mathcal{H}_i^0=v_i\), writing
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}_i^0=v_i+\varphi_i.
  \tag{28.5.13}\label{eq:28.5.13}
\end{equation}
To second order in the \(\varphi_i\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.12} gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V_{\mathrm{quad}}^{\mathrm N}
  ={}&
    \frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(|v_1|^2-|v_2|^2)
    \left[
      2\operatorname{Re}(v_1^*\varphi_1-v_2^*\varphi_2)
      +|\varphi_1|^2-|\varphi_2|^2
    \right]\\
  &+\frac{g^2+g'^2}{2}
    \left[
      \operatorname{Re}(v_1^*\varphi_1-v_2^*\varphi_2)
    \right]^2\\
  &+(m_1^2+|\mu|^2)
    \left(2\operatorname{Re}v_1^*\varphi_1+|\varphi_1|^2\right)\\
  &+(m_2^2+|\mu|^2)
    \left(2\operatorname{Re}v_2^*\varphi_2+|\varphi_2|^2\right)\\
  &-B\mu\operatorname{Re}
    \left(v_1\varphi_2+v_2\varphi_1+\varphi_1\varphi_2\right)
    +\text{constant}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.14}\label{eq:28.5.14}
\end{equation}
For the \(v_i\) to be equilibrium values of the fields, the terms of first
order in the \(\varphi_i\) must vanish
\begin{equation}
  (m_1^2+|\mu|^2)v_1^*
  +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(|v_1|^2-|v_2|^2)v_1^*
  -\frac12B\mu v_2=0,
  \tag{28.5.15}\label{eq:28.5.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  (m_2^2+|\mu|^2)v_2^*
  +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(|v_2|^2-|v_1|^2)v_2^*
  -\frac12B\mu v_1=0.
  \tag{28.5.16}\label{eq:28.5.16}
\end{equation}
Without changing the over-all phase of the \(\varphi_i\), we may adjust
their relative phases so that \(v_1\) is real. Then
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.5.15} and \eqref{eq:28.5.16} show that \(v_2\) is also
real, so that these equations become
\begin{equation}
  (m_1^2+|\mu|^2)v_1
  +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(v_1^2-v_2^2)v_1
  -\frac12B\mu v_2=0,
  \tag{28.5.17}\label{eq:28.5.17}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  (m_2^2+|\mu|^2)v_2
  +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(v_2^2-v_1^2)v_2
  -\frac12B\mu v_1=0.
  \tag{28.5.18}\label{eq:28.5.18}
\end{equation}
These conditions may be used to express the mass parameters in the
potential in terms of the convenient quantities
\begin{equation}
  \tan\beta\equiv v_2/v_1,
  \tag{28.5.19}\label{eq:28.5.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  m_Z^2\equiv\frac12(g^2+g'^2)(v_1^2+v_2^2),
  \tag{28.5.20}\label{eq:28.5.20}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  m_A^2\equiv2|\mu|^2+m_1^2+m_2^2.
  \tag{28.5.21}\label{eq:28.5.21}
\end{equation}
(The parameter \(m_Z\) is the mass of the \(Z\) vector boson.\footnote{There
is a difference of a factor of 2 between the formulas for \(m_Z^2\) given
by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.5.20} and \eqref{eq:21.3.30}, due to the fact that
the scalar fields are normalized differently here and in Section 21.3.}
We will soon see that \(m_A\) is the mass of one of the physical scalars.)
Multiplying Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.5.17} and \eqref{eq:28.5.18}, respectively,
by \(v_2\) and \(v_1\) and taking the sum and difference gives
\begin{equation}
  B\mu=m_A^2\sin2\beta,
  \tag{28.5.22}\label{eq:28.5.22}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  m_1^2-m_2^2=-(m_A^2+m_Z^2)\cos2\beta,
  \tag{28.5.23}\label{eq:28.5.23}
\end{equation}
which with Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.21} yields
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  m_1^2+|\mu|^2
  &=\frac12m_A^2-\frac12(m_A^2+m_Z^2)\cos2\beta,\\
  m_2^2+|\mu|^2
  &=\frac12m_A^2+\frac12(m_A^2+m_Z^2)\cos2\beta.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.24}\label{eq:28.5.24}
\end{equation}
With linear terms cancelling, the quadratic part \eqref{eq:28.5.14} of
the neutral scalar potential may then be written
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V_{\mathrm{quad}}^{\mathrm N}
  ={}&
    \frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(v_1^2-v_2^2)
      \left[|\varphi_1|^2-|\varphi_2|^2\right]
    +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{2}
      \left[\operatorname{Re}(v_1\varphi_1-v_2\varphi_2)\right]^2\\
  &+(m_1^2+|\mu|^2)|\varphi_1|^2
    +(m_2^2+|\mu|^2)|\varphi_2|^2
    -B\mu\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_1\varphi_2)
    +\text{constant}\\
  ={}&
    \frac12m_Z^2\cos2\beta
      \left[|\varphi_1|^2-|\varphi_2|^2\right]
    +m_Z^2
      \left[
        \operatorname{Re}(\cos\beta\,\varphi_1-\sin\beta\,\varphi_2)
      \right]^2\\
  &+\frac12m_A^2\left(|\varphi_1|^2+|\varphi_2|^2\right)
    -\frac12(m_A^2+m_Z^2)\cos2\beta
      \left[|\varphi_1|^2-|\varphi_2|^2\right]\\
  &-m_A^2\sin2\beta\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_1\varphi_2)
    +\text{constant}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.25}\label{eq:28.5.25}
\end{equation}

We see from Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.25} that the real and imaginary parts of
the \(\varphi_i\) are decoupled. (This is because the potential
\eqref{eq:28.5.12} is invariant under a charge conjugation or CP
transformation \(\varphi_i\longrightarrow\varphi_i^*\).) The mass-squared
matrix of the imaginary parts of the \(\varphi_i\) is
\begin{equation}
  M_{\operatorname{Im}\varphi}^2
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \frac12m_A^2(1-\cos2\beta) & \frac12m_A^2\sin2\beta\\
    \frac12m_A^2\sin2\beta & \frac12m_A^2(1+\cos2\beta)
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{28.5.26}\label{eq:28.5.26}
\end{equation}
The determinant vanishes, so one eigenvalue is zero, and the other equals
the trace, which is just \(m_A^2\). The zero-mass scalar is of course the
neutral Goldstone boson associated with the spontaneous breakdown of
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) to electromagnetic gauge invariance, and as
discussed in Chapter 21 it is eliminated by the Higgs mechanism. As
promised, \(m_A\) is the mass of one of the physical scalars, the
non-Goldstone boson with \(C\) negative. This shows that for the field value
\(\varphi_i=v_i\) to be at least a local minimum of the potential, the
parameter \(m_A^2\) defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.21} must be positive.
The condition \eqref{eq:28.5.11} for good behavior at large field strengths
shows that Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22} has a solution here for \(\beta\) in the
range \(0\leq\beta\leq\pi/2\).

In particular, if \(B\mu=0\) and \(0<\beta<\pi/2\), then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22} shows that \(m_A=0\). In this case, the particle
\(A\) is the Goldstone boson of a \(U(1)\) Peccei--Quinn
symmetry~\hyperref[ch28-ref-4]{[4]} of the potential \eqref{eq:28.5.12}
under an equal phase change of \(\mathcal{H}_1^0\) and
\(\mathcal{H}_2^0\), which for \(v_1\neq0\) and \(v_2\neq0\) is
spontaneously broken without leaving any combination of this and the
electroweak \(U(1)\) symmetry unbroken. This is the original version of the
axion~\hyperref[ch28-ref-25]{[25]}, which as we saw in Section 23.6 acquires
only a small mass from the Yukawa interactions of the scalars with the
quarks and is experimentally ruled out. Thus we can conclude that \(B\mu\)
definitely does not vanish.

The elements of the mass-squared matrix for the real scalars are given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.25} as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  (M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{11}
  &=\frac12m_A^2(1-\cos2\beta)
    +\frac12m_Z^2(1+\cos2\beta),\\
  (M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{12}
  &=(M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{21}
    =-\frac12(m_A^2+m_Z^2)\sin2\beta,\\
  (M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{22}
  &=\frac12m_A^2(1+\cos2\beta)
    +\frac12m_Z^2(1-\cos2\beta).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.27}\label{eq:28.5.27}
\end{equation}
Solving the secular equation gives the eigenvalues
\begin{equation}
  m_H^2
  =\frac12\left[
    m_A^2+m_Z^2
    +\sqrt{(m_A^2+m_Z^2)^2-4m_A^2m_Z^2\cos^22\beta}
  \right],
  \tag{28.5.28}\label{eq:28.5.28}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  m_h^2
  =\frac12\left[
    m_A^2+m_Z^2
    -\sqrt{(m_A^2+m_Z^2)^2-4m_A^2m_Z^2\cos^22\beta}
  \right].
  \tag{28.5.29}\label{eq:28.5.29}
\end{equation}

To calculate the masses of the charged scalars, we evaluate the potential
\(V\) with the neutral scalars set equal to their vacuum expectation values:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}_1=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    v_1\\
    \mathcal{H}_1^-
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{H}_2=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathcal{H}_2^+\\
    v_2
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{28.5.30}\label{eq:28.5.30}
\end{equation}
Using this in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.10} then gives the quadratic part of the
charged scalar potential
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V_{\mathrm{quad}}^{\mathrm C}
  ={}&
    \frac{g^2}{2}
      \left|v_2(\mathcal{H}_1^-)^*+v_1\mathcal{H}_2^+\right|^2
    +\frac{g^2+g'^2}{4}(v_1^2-v_2^2)
      \left(|\mathcal{H}_1^-|^2-|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2\right)\\
  &+(m_1^2+|\mu|^2)|\mathcal{H}_1^-|^2
    +(m_2^2+|\mu|^2)|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2
    +B\mu\mathcal{H}_1^-\mathcal{H}_2^+.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.31}\label{eq:28.5.31}
\end{equation}
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22} and \eqref{eq:28.5.24}, this may be written
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  V_{\mathrm{quad}}^{\mathrm C}
  =\frac12(m_W^2+m_A^2)
    \bigl[
      &|\mathcal{H}_1^-|^2(1-\cos2\beta)
      +|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2(1+\cos2\beta)\\
      &+2\sin2\beta\,\mathcal{H}_1^-\mathcal{H}_2^+
    \bigr],
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.32}\label{eq:28.5.32}
\end{equation}
where \(m_W\) is the charged gauge boson mass:
\begin{equation}
  m_W^2=\frac12g^2(|v_1|^2+|v_2|^2).
  \tag{28.5.33}\label{eq:28.5.33}
\end{equation}
The charged scalar mass matrix is then
\begin{equation}
  M_{\mathrm C}^2
  =\frac12(m_W^2+m_A^2)
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1-\cos2\beta & \sin2\beta\\
    \sin2\beta & 1+\cos2\beta
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{28.5.34}\label{eq:28.5.34}
\end{equation}
This has determinant zero, so it has one eigenvalue equal to zero and the
other eigenvalue equal to the trace
\begin{equation}
  m_C^2=m_W^2+m_A^2.
  \tag{28.5.35}\label{eq:28.5.35}
\end{equation}
The zero-mass charged scalar is of course the other Goldstone boson
associated with the spontaneous breakdown of
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\), and like the neutral Goldstone boson found
earlier it is eliminated by the Higgs mechanism.

Even without knowing the parameters \(m_A\) and \(\beta\), these results
tell us a lot about the relative magnitudes of the scalar boson masses. We
can rewrite Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.5.28} and \eqref{eq:28.5.29} in the form
\begin{equation}
  m_H^2
  =\frac12\left[
    m_A^2+m_Z^2
    +\sqrt{(m_A^2-m_Z^2)^2+4m_A^2m_Z^2\sin^22\beta}
  \right],
  \tag{28.5.36}\label{eq:28.5.36}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  m_h^2
  =\frac12\left[
    m_A^2+m_Z^2
    -\sqrt{(m_A^2-m_Z^2)^2+4m_A^2m_Z^2\sin^22\beta}
  \right].
  \tag{28.5.37}\label{eq:28.5.37}
\end{equation}
We see that \textit{the heavier neutral scalar mass \(m_H\) is larger than
the larger of \(m_Z\) and \(m_A\), while the lighter neutral scalar mass
\(m_h\) is smaller than the smaller of \(m_Z\) and \(m_A\).} If the large
ratio of top to bottom quark masses is due to a large ratio
\(v_2/v_1=\tan\beta\) of scalar field vacuum expectation values, rather
than a large ratio of Yukawa couplings, then we expect \(\beta\) to be near
\(\pi/2\), in which case these inequalities become approximate equalities.
Further, Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.35} shows that \textit{the charged scalar mass
is greater than both \(m_A\) and \(m_W\).}

These results are quantitatively modified by various radiative corrections
\textit{within} the standard model (as opposed to radiative effects that
produce the input parameters \(m_i^2\) in theories of gauge-mediated
supersymmetry breaking). The most important corrections arise from the
presence in the scalar potential \(V\) of terms arising from graphs
consisting of a single loop of top or bottom quarks interacting any number
of times with external scalar field lines. This is because the top and
bottom quarks have by far the strongest couplings to \(\mathcal{H}_2\) and
\(\mathcal{H}_1\), respectively. (It is prudent to include bottom as well as
top quark loops here because, as mentioned above, the larger mass of the top
quark may be due to a large ratio \(v_2/v_1\), rather than to a large ratio
of Yukawa couplings, but even in this case we will see that the dominant
corrections are due to top quark loops.)

Let us first consider the neutral scalars, at least one of which would be
lighter than the \(Z\) boson in the absence of radiative corrections. The
effect of these top and bottom loops is to contribute a term of form
\(U_t(|\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2)+U_b(|\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2)\) to \(V^{\mathrm N}\).
We will absorb any terms in \(U_b\) or \(U_t\) that are linear in
\(|\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2-v_1^2\) or \(|\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2-v_2^2\) into the
input parameters \(m_1^2\) and \(m_2^2\), so that
\begin{equation}
  U_b'(v_1^2)=U_t'(v_2^2)=0.
  \tag{28.5.38}\label{eq:28.5.38}
\end{equation}
Our earlier results \eqref{eq:28.5.24} and \eqref{eq:28.5.22} for
\(m_1^2+|\mu|^2\), \(m_2^2+|\mu|^2\) and \(B\mu\) are then unchanged.
Also, the mass matrix for the \(C\)-odd neutral scalars is still given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.26}. On the other hand, the elements of the
mass-squared matrix for the \(C\)-even neutral scalars are now given by
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  (M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{11}
  &=\frac12m_A^2(1-\cos2\beta)
    +\frac12m_Z^2(1+\cos2\beta)+\Delta_b,\\
  (M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{12}
  &=(M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{21}
    =-\frac12(m_A^2+m_Z^2)\sin2\beta,\\
  (M_{\operatorname{Re}\varphi}^2)_{22}
  &=\frac12m_A^2(1+\cos2\beta)
    +\frac12m_Z^2(1-\cos2\beta)+\Delta_t,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.39}\label{eq:28.5.39}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_b=2v_1^2U_b''(v_1^2),
  \qquad
  \Delta_t=2v_2^2U_t''(v_2^2).
  \tag{28.5.40}\label{eq:28.5.40}
\end{equation}
The solutions of the secular equation are then
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  m_H^2=\frac12\biggl[
    &m_A^2+m_Z^2+\Delta_t+\Delta_b\\
    &+\sqrt{
      \left((m_A^2-m_Z^2)\cos2\beta+\Delta_t-\Delta_b\right)^2
      +(m_A^2+m_Z^2)^2\sin^22\beta
    }
  \biggr],
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.41}\label{eq:28.5.41}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  m_h^2=\frac12\biggl[
    &m_A^2+m_Z^2+\Delta_t+\Delta_b\\
    &-\sqrt{
      \left((m_A^2-m_Z^2)\cos2\beta+\Delta_t-\Delta_b\right)^2
      +(m_A^2+m_Z^2)^2\sin^22\beta
    }
  \biggr].
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.42}\label{eq:28.5.42}
\end{equation}
In considering searches for these particles, it is important to note that
the light Higgs mass \(m_h\) increases as the unknown mass \(m_A\)
increases, reaching a finite upper bound for
\(m_A\longrightarrow\infty\)
\begin{equation}
  m_h^2\leq m_h^2(m_A\longrightarrow\infty)
  =m_Z^2\cos^22\beta+\Delta_t\sin^2\beta+\Delta_b\cos^2\beta.
  \tag{28.5.43}\label{eq:28.5.43}
\end{equation}

To calculate \(\Delta_b\) and \(\Delta_t\), we recall from Section 16.2
that the potentials \(U_b\) and \(U_t\) are given by
\begin{equation}
  U_b(|\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2)
  =-\frac{3}{16\pi^2}|\lambda_b\mathcal{H}_1^0|^4
    \left[
      \ln\frac{|\lambda_b\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2}{M_{sb}^2}
      -\frac32
    \right]
    +\text{linear terms},
  \tag{28.5.44}\label{eq:28.5.44}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  U_t(|\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2)
  =-\frac{3}{16\pi^2}|\lambda_t\mathcal{H}_2^0|^4
    \left[
      \ln\frac{|\lambda_t\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2}{M_{st}^2}
      -\frac32
    \right]
    +\text{linear terms},
  \tag{28.5.45}\label{eq:28.5.45}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_t=m_t/v_2\) and \(\lambda_b=m_b/v_1\) are the Yukawa
couplings of the top and bottom quarks; \(M_{st}\) and \(M_{sb}\) are the
masses of the stop and sbottom (the scalar superpartners of the top and
bottom quarks); these masses and the terms \(-3/2\) inside the square
brackets are chosen to satisfy the condition that supersymmetry-breaking
corrections due to stop and sbottom loops would cancel the corrections due
to top and bottom loops if the masses were equal; and the `linear terms'
are linear in \(|\mathcal{H}_2^0|^2\) or \(|\mathcal{H}_1^0|^2\), with
coefficients adjusted to satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.38}. (The factor 3
takes account of the three quark colors.) Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.40}
gives
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_b
  =-\frac{3}{4\pi^2}|\lambda_b|^4v_1^2
    \ln\left(\frac{\lambda_b^2v_1^2}{M_{sb}^2}\right)
  =\frac{3\sqrt{2}\,m_b^4G_F}{2\pi^2\cos^2\beta}
    \ln\left(\frac{M_{sb}^2}{m_b^2}\right),
  \tag{28.5.46}\label{eq:28.5.46}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_t
  =-\frac{3}{4\pi^2}|\lambda_t|^4v_2^2
    \ln\left(\frac{\lambda_t^2v_2^2}{M_{st}^2}\right)
  =\frac{3\sqrt{2}\,m_t^4G_F}{2\pi^2\sin^2\beta}
    \ln\left(\frac{M_{st}^2}{m_t^2}\right),
  \tag{28.5.47}\label{eq:28.5.47}
\end{equation}
where \(G_F=1.17\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{GeV}^{-2}\) is the Fermi coupling
constant, given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:21.3.34} as
\(G_F=g^2/(4\sqrt{2}m_W^2)\). Taking \(m_b=4.3\ \mathrm{GeV}\),
\(m_t=180\ \mathrm{GeV}\), \(M_{st}\sim M_{sb}\sim1\ \mathrm{TeV}\), and
\(m_Z=91.2\ \mathrm{GeV}\) gives
\(\Delta_b\sim1.1\times10^{-6}m_Z^2/\cos^2\beta\) and
\(\Delta_t\sim1.1m_Z^2/\sin^2\beta\). We see that even if \(\tan\beta\)
is as large as \(m_t/m_b\), the top-quark correction \(\Delta_t\) will
still be much larger than \(\Delta_b\).

The effect of \(\Delta_t\) is to \textit{increase} both \(m_H\) and \(m_h\).
Taking this and other radiative corrections into
account~\hyperref[ch28-ref-26]{[26]}, for \(\tan\beta>10\) the upper bound
\eqref{eq:28.5.43} on the lightest neutral scalar mass is raised by
radiative corrections from just below \(m_Z\) to between \(100\ \mathrm{GeV}\)
and \(110\ \mathrm{GeV}\) for stop masses between \(300\ \mathrm{GeV}\)
and \(1\ \mathrm{TeV}\). For comparison, an experimental \textit{lower}
bound~\hyperref[ch28-ref-27]{[27]} of \(62.5\ \mathrm{GeV}\) on \(m_h\),
\(m_H\), and \(m_A\) is set by the absence of an \(hA\) or \(HA\) final
state in \(e^+e^-\) collisions at 130 to \(172\ \mathrm{GeV}\). Also,
calculations of radiative corrections involving Higgs scalars are
consistent with precision measurements of electroweak phenomena for \(m_h\)
in the range~\hyperref[ch28-ref-27a]{[27a]} of 27 to \(140\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

Radiative corrections are less important for the charged scalars. Since the
attachment of charged scalar lines allows transitions between top and
bottom quarks, the correction to the scalar field potential here takes a
more general form, constrained by \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) to be
\begin{equation}
  \Delta V
  =U\left(
    \mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2,
    \mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1,
    \mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1,
    \mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2,
    \mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2
  \right).
  \tag{28.5.48}\label{eq:28.5.48}
\end{equation}
(Quark loops do not actually produce any dependence on
\(\mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2\).) Every appearance of a
\(\mathcal{H}_1\) or \(\mathcal{H}_2\) doublet is accompanied with a factor
\(\lambda_b\) or \(\lambda_t\), respectively, so terms involving
\(\mathcal{H}_1\) will be suppressed, as we have already seen in
calculating the neutral scalar masses. To a good approximation, then, the
correction to the effective potential is of the form
\begin{equation}
  \Delta V
  \simeq U(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2,0,0,0)
  =U(|v_2+\varphi_2|^2+|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2,0,0,0).
  \tag{28.5.49}\label{eq:28.5.49}
\end{equation}
By going back to the case where the charged fields vanish, we see that the
function \(U\) must be just the same as what was earlier called \(U_t\).
Any terms in \(U\) of first order in an expansion in powers of
\(|v_2+\varphi_2|^2+|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2-v_2^2\) would simply serve to
redefine the constant \(m_2^2\), and are eliminated by the convention
\eqref{eq:28.5.38}. Terms in \(U\) of second order in
\(|v_2+\varphi_2|^2+|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2-v_2^2\) are genuine radiative
corrections, but although they contain terms of second order in
\(|\varphi_2|^2\) which do affect the neutral scalar masses, they contain
no terms of second order in \(|\mathcal{H}_2^+|^2\) which could shift the
non-Goldstone charged scalar mass. Fortunately, radiative corrections are
not needed to avoid a conflict with experiment, because in the absence of
an upper bound on \(m_A\) there is no theoretical upper bound on the
charged scalar mass \eqref{eq:28.5.35}. An experimental lower
bound~\hyperref[ch28-ref-28]{[28]} \(m_C\geq59\ \mathrm{GeV}\) is provided
by the non-observation of the process
\(e^+e^-\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^+\mathcal{H}^-\) at 181 to
\(184\ \mathrm{GeV}\). There is a much more stringent lower bound on
\(m_C\) provided by the rate of the process \(b\longrightarrow s\gamma\)
(measured in decays like \(B\longrightarrow K^*\gamma\)), which can occur
by transitions to an intermediate \(\mathcal{H}^-u\) or
\(\mathcal{H}^-c\) state, with the photon radiated from the virtual quark
or \(\mathcal{H}^-\). The present agreement between theory and experiment
for this process sets a lower bound~\hyperref[ch28-ref-29]{[29]} of about
\(150\ \mathrm{GeV}\) on \(m_C\) (and higher for \(\tan\beta<1\)). Using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.35}, this gives an important lower bound
\(m_A>125\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

There are two conditions on the masses \(m_i^2\) that must be satisfied by
any model of supersymmetry breaking in order to give a successful account
of electroweak symmetry breaking. One of them is provided by the
requirement that the potential is bounded below, which as we have seen
requires that
\[
  2|\mu|^2+m_1^2+m_2^2>B\mu.
\]
Since \(B\mu\) is defined to be positive, this ensures that the \(C\)-odd
neutral scalar squared mass \eqref{eq:28.5.21} is positive. The other
condition is provided by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22} and
\eqref{eq:28.5.24}, which for arbitrary values of \(\beta\) require that
\begin{equation}
  4(m_1^2+|\mu|^2)(m_2^2+|\mu|^2)\leq(B\mu)^2.
  \tag{28.5.50}\label{eq:28.5.50}
\end{equation}
We can easily see from Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.10} that this condition ensures
that the matrix of second derivatives of the potential has a negative
eigenvalue at \(\mathcal{H}_1=\mathcal{H}_2=0\), so this
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\)-invariant point is one of unstable
equilibrium, and therefore \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) must be
spontaneously broken. If \(\beta\) is very near \(\pi/2\), then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.24} tells us that this condition is satisfied by having
\(m_1^2+|\mu|^2\) positive and \(m_2^2+|\mu|^2\) \textit{negative}. As we
will see in the next section, the renormalization group flow of the
parameters in the scalar field Lagrangian provides a mechanism for driving
\(m_2^2+|\mu|^2\) negative.

Even with a minimal set of superfields, in supersymmetry theories there are
several pairs of particles with different \(SU(2)\times U(1)\)
transformation properties, but the same charge, color, and baryon and
lepton number, which become mixed when \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) is
spontaneously broken. We have already seen an example of this in the
previous section, in which we had to deal with the mixing of the scalar
superpartners of the left-handed quarks with the complex conjugates of the
scalar superpartners of the left-handed antiquarks. A similar mixing occurs
between the higgsinos and gauginos, both charged and neutral; the particles
of definite mass are not higgsinos or gauginos, but mixtures known as
\textit{charginos} and \textit{neutralinos}. Let us consider the charginos,
which provide a useful bound on \(\mu\). According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.4.8}, there are off-diagonal supersymmetric mass terms in
the Lagrangian density
\[
  -\operatorname{Re}\left[
    \mu\left(h_1^-h_2^+\right)
    +\ii\sqrt{2}m_W\cos\beta
      \left(w^-h_2^+\right)
    +\ii\sqrt{2}m_W\sin\beta
      \left(w^+h_1^-\right)
  \right],
\]
To this, we should add a term for the wino mass generated by gauge
interactions with the supersymmetry-breaking sector
\[
  -m_{\mathrm{wino}}\operatorname{Re}
    \left(w^+w^-\right).
\]
The squared chargino masses are then the eigenvalues of the matrix
\(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm C}^\dagger\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm C}\), where
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm C}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    m_{\mathrm{wino}} & \ii\sqrt{2}m_W\sin\beta\\
    \ii\sqrt{2}m_W\cos\beta & \mu
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \tag{28.5.51}\label{eq:28.5.51}
\end{equation}
These two eigenvalues are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  m_{\mathrm{chargino}}^2
  =\frac12\biggl[
    &m_{\mathrm{wino}}^2+2m_W^2+|\mu|^2\\
    &\mathrel{\pm}\Bigl(
      (m_{\mathrm{wino}}^2-|\mu|^2)^2
      +4m_W^4\cos^22\beta\\
    &\hspace{4.7em}
      +4m_W^2\bigl(
        m_{\mathrm{wino}}^2+|\mu|^2
        -2m_{\mathrm{wino}}\operatorname{Re}\mu\sin2\beta
      \bigr)
    \Bigr)^{1/2}
  \biggr].
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.5.52}\label{eq:28.5.52}
\end{equation}
We expect the wino mass \(m_{\mathrm{wino}}\) to be much larger than
\(m_W\). If it is also much larger than \(|\mu|\), then the heavier
chargino is mostly a wino, with mass \(m_{\mathrm{wino}}\), while the
lightest chargino is mostly a higgsino, with mass \(|\mu|\). In any case,
\(|\mu|\) is greater than the lightest chargino mass, which the
non-appearance of gauginos in \(e^+\)-\(e^-\) annihilation tells us is
greater than about \(60\ \mathrm{GeV}\), and probably greater than \(m_W\).
The search for neutralinos in \(e^+\)-\(e^-\) annihilation (with
assumptions like \eqref{eq:28.4.2}) has set a lower bound of
\(27\ \mathrm{GeV}\) on the mass of the lightest
neutralino~\hyperref[ch28-ref-29a]{[29a]}.
