\subsection{Gauge Mediation of Supersymmetry Breaking}

In this section we will consider the possibility that the breakdown of
supersymmetry is transmitted to the known particles through interactions
of the ordinary \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge
bosons and their superpartners~\hyperref[ch28-ref-30]{[30]}. It is assumed
here that supersymmetry is dynamically broken in a sector of superfields,
\emph{not} including the superfields of the observed quarks and leptons,
and that some of the chiral superfields in the symmetry-breaking sector,
known as the \emph{messenger superfields}, have non-vanishing
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) quantum numbers. In order
for the messenger particles to be able to get large (say, of order
\(1\ \mathrm{TeV}\)) masses without breaking
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\), it is necessary for them
to furnish a real (or pseudoreal) representation of
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\), which automatically also
means that they do not introduce any new anomalies. Although most
treatments of gauge-mediated supersymmetry breaking in the literature also
make specific assumptions about the interactions of the messenger
superfields with the other superfields responsible for supersymmetry
breaking, the most important predictions of this class of theories in fact
do not depend on these assumptions. We shall therefore put off assuming
anything about the interactions of the messenger superfields with the other
superfields of the symmetry-breaking sector. We will, however, make another
assumption about the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) properties of the

messenger superfields, which has a strong phenomenological motivation. In
order for the messenger particles not to interfere with the unification of
couplings discussed in Section 28.2, we assume that they have the same
ratios for the total traces of all squared
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge generators as the
ordinary quarks and leptons. This condition will automatically be satisfied
if the messenger superfields (together perhaps with some
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-neutral chiral
superfields) furnish a complete representation of some simple group \(G\)
that contains \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\), of which
the quarks and leptons (again, together perhaps with some
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-neutral chiral
superfields) also form a complete representation. (For instance, these
left-chiral superfields might form \(N\) \(SU(2)\) singlet \(SU(3)\)
triplets with charge \(e/3\) and \(N\) \(SU(2)\) doublet \(SU(3)\) singlets
with charges 0 and \(-e\), which together form \(N\) representations
\(\mathbf{5}\) of \(SU(5)\), together with an equal number of left-chiral
superfields in the complex-conjugate representations of
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\), which form \(N\)
representations \(\bar{\mathbf{5}}\).) However, for our present purposes we
will neither need to assume that \(G\) is an actual symmetry group of the
theory, nor adopt any particular choice of \(G\) or of the representations
furnished by the messenger particles.

This interaction of the messenger superfields both with the other chiral
and/or gauge superfields of the supersymmetry-breaking sector and with the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge superfields can be
expected to produce a breakdown of supersymmetry in the propagators of the
component fields of the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge superfields. To
lowest order in the \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)
couplings, the leading contribution to the propagators comes from the
diagram shown in Figure~\ref{fig:28.5}, in which a pair of gauge, gaugino,
or auxiliary \(D\)-field lines is attached to a loop of messenger fields,
which also may have any number of interactions with
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-neutral fields of the
supersymmetry-breaking sector. The supersymmetry-breaking corrections
\(\Delta_{ic}\) to the propagators of the gauge superfields (with
\(i=1,2,3\) for \(SU(3)\), \(SU(2)\), and \(U(1)\), and
\(c=V,\lambda,D\) labelling the different components of each gauge
superfield) therefore have the forms
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \Delta_{3c}(q)
      &=(g_s^2/16\pi^2)\sum_n T_{3n}\Pi_{cn}(q)\,,\\
    \Delta_{2c}(q)
      &=(g^2/16\pi^2)\sum_n T_{2n}\Pi_{cn}(q)\,,\\
    \Delta_{1c}(q)
      &=(g'^2/16\pi^2)\sum_n T_{1n}\Pi_{cn}(q)\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.1}\label{eq:28.6.1}
\end{equation}
where \(n\) labels the different messenger superfields; \(\Pi_{cn}(q)\)
are more-or-less complicated functions of the four-momentum \(q\);
\(T_{3n}\) and \(T_{2n}\) are the traces of the squares of any generator of
\(SU(3)\) and \(SU(2)\), respectively, in the representations furnished by
the \(n\)th messenger superfield (normalized so that in the defining
representations \(T_3=T_2=1/2\)); and \(T_{1n}\) is the sum of the squares
of the electroweak hypercharges of the \(n\)th messenger superfield. One
immediate consequence is that the gauginos acquire masses of the same
form:\footnote{Recall that the bino is the superpartner of the \(U(1)\)
gauge field \(B_\mu\) that appears in the Lagrangian of the standard model.
We are not yet taking \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) breaking into account,
so the gaugino, squark, and slepton masses calculated here should be
understood as parameters appearing in the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-invariant effective
Lagrangian of the standard mode.}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    m_{\mathrm{gluino}}
      &=(g_s^2/16\pi^2)\sum_n T_{3n}M_{gn}\,,\\
    m_{\mathrm{wino}}
      &=(g^2/16\pi^2)\sum_n T_{2n}M_{gn}\,,\\
    m_{\mathrm{bino}}
      &=(g'^2/16\pi^2)\sum_n T_{1n}M_{gn}\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.2}\label{eq:28.6.2}
\end{equation}
where the \(M_{gn}\) are masses that characterize the different messenger
superfields. As already mentioned, in order to preserve the unification of
couplings at very high energy we assume that the sums of the \(T_n\) have
the same ratios as for the observed quarks and leptons:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_n T_{3n}=\sum_n T_{2n}=\sum_n 3T_{1n}/5=T\,.
  \tag{28.6.3}\label{eq:28.6.3}
\end{equation}
The breakdown of supersymmetry in these propagators is then communicated
to the squarks and sleptons of the supersymmetric standard model through
the diagrams shown in Figure~\ref{fig:28.6}, in which an

\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge boson or gaugino
or auxiliary \(D\) field is emitted and reabsorbed by the squark or
slepton. We are calculating the effective low-energy theory in which
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-breaking is not yet taken
into account, so there is no mixing among the \(SU(3)\), \(SU(2)\), and
\(U(1)\) propagators, and each propagator acts like a unit matrix on the
gauge indices. Thus the squared mass given to any squark or slepton will be
proportional to a sum over the squares of all the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) generators (including
coupling constants) in the representation furnished by that squark or
slepton. The sums of the squared \(SU(2)\) and \(SU(3)\) generators in the
defining representations are
\[
  \sum_{a=1}^{3}(g\sigma_a/2)^2
  =\frac{3g^2}{4}\,\mathbf{1}\,,
  \qquad
  \sum_{\alpha=1}^{8}(g_s\lambda_\alpha/2)^2
  =\frac{4g_s^2}{3}\,\mathbf{1}\,,
\]
where \(\sigma_a\) are the Pauli isospin matrices (5.4.18) and
\(\lambda_\alpha\) are the Gell-Mann matrices (19.7.2). For \(U(1)\) the
generator is just the weak hypercharge (21.3.7), including a factor \(g'\).
The squared squark and slepton masses therefore have the forms
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    M_Q^2={}&2\sum_n M_{sn}^2
      \left[
        \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{3n}
        +\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{2n}
        +\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{1n}
      \right],\\
    M_{\bar U}^2={}&2\sum_n M_{sn}^2
      \left[
        \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{3n}
        +\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{1n}
      \right],\\
    M_{\bar D}^2={}&2\sum_n M_{sn}^2
      \left[
        \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{3n}
        +\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{1n}
      \right],\\
    M_L^2={}&2\sum_n M_{sn}^2
      \left[
        \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{2n}
        +\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{1n}
      \right],\\
    M_{\bar E}^2={}&2\sum_n M_{sn}^2
      \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{1n}\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.4}\label{eq:28.6.4}
\end{equation}
where \(Q\), \(\bar U\), \(\bar D\), \(L\), and \(\bar E\) are the scalar
superpartners of the left-handed quark doublets, the left-handed
antiquarks of charge \(-2e/3\) and \(+e/3\), the left-handed lepton
doublets, and the left-handed charged antileptons, and \(M_{sn}\) are some
new masses that characterize the \(n\)th messenger superfields. (The
factor 2 is extracted from \(M_{sn}^2\) for future convenience.) The
squark and slepton masses that are produced in this way are automatically
the same in all three generations, thus avoiding the problem with
flavor-changing processes discussed in Section 28.4.

We expect all \(M_{gn}\) and \(M_{sn}\) to be roughly of the same order of
magnitude, so that the gluino and squarks will have comparable masses,
while the wino, bino, and sleptons will be much lighter, with masses
suppressed by squares of electroweak coupling constants.

We can go considerably further than this with some reasonable dynamical
assumptions. Suppose that the effects of supersymmetry breaking on the
messenger superfields may be modeled by including these superfields along
with a set of
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-neutral chiral
superfields \(S_n\) (not necessarily all distinct) in a superpotential
\begin{equation}
  f(\Phi,\bar\Phi,S)=\sum_n\lambda_nS_n\Phi_n\bar\Phi_n\,,
  \tag{28.6.5}\label{eq:28.6.5}
\end{equation}
where \(\bar\Phi_n\) and \(\Phi_n\) are left-chiral messenger superfields
in complex-conjugate representations of
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) and \(\lambda_n\) are a
set of coupling coefficients. (Here and below we suppress the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\) indices that are summed over in calculating
scalar products like \(\Phi_n\bar\Phi_n\).) The superfields \(S_n\) are
supposed to have non-vanishing vacuum expectation values
\(\mathcal{S}_n\) and \(\mathcal{F}_n\) for their scalar and auxiliary
components, respectively. It is the non-zero values of \(\mathcal{F}_n\)
that in these models introduce supersymmetry breaking in the masses of the
\(\Phi_n\) and \(\bar\Phi_n\) particles. Section 26.4 shows that, with
gauge couplings neglected, the squared masses of the spinor components of
\(\Phi_n\) (and \(\bar\Phi_n\)) are the eigenvalues of the matrix
\(\mathcal{M}_n^\dagger\mathcal{M}_n\), with \(\mathcal{M}_n\) defined by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.11}, which gives
\[
  \mathcal{M}_n=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&\lambda_n\mathcal{S}_n\\
    \lambda_n\mathcal{S}_n&0
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
so that the messenger fermions have masses
\(\lvert\lambda_n\mathcal{S}_n\rvert\). To find the mass terms for the
scalar components \(\phi_n\) and \(\bar\phi_n\) of the \(\Phi_n\) and
\(\bar\Phi_n\) superfields, we note that integrating out the auxiliary
fields of the \(\Phi_n\) and \(\bar\Phi_n\) yields a potential
\[
  \sum_n\left|
    \frac{\partial f(\phi,\bar\phi,\mathcal{S})}{\partial\phi_n}
  \right|^2
  +\sum_n\left|
    \frac{\partial f(\phi,\bar\phi,\mathcal{S})}{\partial\bar\phi_n}
  \right|^2
  =\sum_n\lvert\lambda_n\mathcal{S}_n\rvert^2
    \left[\lvert\phi_n\rvert^2+\lvert\bar\phi_n\rvert^2\right],
\]
to which we now must add the contribution of the auxiliary component of
the \(S_n\), given by the second term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.4} as:
\[
  2\operatorname{Re}\sum_n
  \left[
    \lambda_n\mathcal{F}_n
    \frac{\partial f(\phi,\bar\phi,\mathcal{S})}
         {\partial\mathcal{S}_n}
  \right]
  =2\operatorname{Re}\sum_n
  \left[\mathcal{F}_n\lambda_n\phi_n\bar\phi_n\right].
\]
The complex scalar fields of definite mass are then
\((\phi_n\pm e^{-i\alpha_n}\bar\phi_n)/\sqrt{2}\), where \(\alpha_n\) is
the phase of \(\lambda_n\mathcal{F}_n\), with squared masses
\(\lvert\lambda_n\mathcal{S}_n\rvert^2
\mathbin{\pm}\lvert\lambda_n\mathcal{F}_n\rvert\). (Note that this
pattern, of a pair of complex scalars with squared masses equidistant above
and below a Dirac fermion squared mass, is just what we would expect
from the sum rule \eqref{eq:27.5.11}.) Since these squared masses must be
positive, it follows that
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\mathcal{F}_n\rvert
  \leq\lvert\lambda_n\rvert\lvert\mathcal{S}_n\rvert^2\,.
  \tag{28.6.6}\label{eq:28.6.6}
\end{equation}
The gaugino masses in models based on Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.5} are given by
diagrams of the form shown in Figure~\ref{fig:28.5}, but now with just a
single loop, not including what are shown as dotted lines in
Figure~\ref{fig:28.5}. A detailed calculation gives the coefficients
\(M_{gn}\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.2}
as~\hyperref[ch28-ref-31]{[31]}
\begin{equation}
  M_{gn}
  =\frac{\lvert\mathcal{F}_n\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{S}_n\rvert}
  g\left(
    \frac{\lvert\mathcal{F}_n\rvert}
         {\lvert\lambda_n\rvert\lvert\mathcal{S}_n\rvert^2}
  \right),
  \tag{28.6.7}\label{eq:28.6.7}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    g(x)
      &\equiv\frac{1}{2x^2}
      \left[(1+x)\ln(1+x)+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right]\\
      &=1+\frac{x^2}{6}+\frac{x^4}{15}+\frac{x^6}{28}+\cdots\,.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.8}\label{eq:28.6.8}
\end{equation}
The masses of the squarks and sleptons are given by the diagrams of
Figure~\ref{fig:28.6}, which now involve just two loops. Another detailed
calculation gives the mass parameters \(M_{sn}^2\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.4} as~\hyperref[ch28-ref-31]{[31]}
\begin{equation}
  M_{sn}^2
  =\frac{\lvert\mathcal{F}_n\rvert^2}
         {\lvert\mathcal{S}_n^2\rvert}
  f\left(
    \frac{\lvert\mathcal{F}_n\rvert}
         {\lvert\lambda_n\rvert\lvert\mathcal{S}_n\rvert^2}
  \right),
  \tag{28.6.9}\label{eq:28.6.9}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    f(x)={}&\frac{1+x}{x^2}
    \left[
      \ln(1+x)
      -2\operatorname{Li}_2\left(\frac{x}{1+x}\right)
      +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Li}_2\left(\frac{2x}{1+x}\right)
    \right]
    +x\longrightarrow -x\\
    ={}&1+\frac{1}{36}x^2-\frac{11}{450}x^4
      -\frac{319}{11760}x^6+\cdots\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.10}\label{eq:28.6.10}
\end{equation}
with \(\operatorname{Li}_2\) the dilogarithm
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Li}_2(x)\equiv-\int_0^x\frac{\ln(1-t)}{t}\,dt\,.
  \tag{28.6.11}\label{eq:28.6.11}
\end{equation}
In particular, if (as is usually assumed) the various \(S_n\) are all the
same, and if
\(\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\ll
\lvert\lambda_n\rvert\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^2\) for all \(n\), then
\(f\) and \(g\) in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.9} and \eqref{eq:28.6.7} may be
set equal to 1, so that
\begin{equation}
  M_{gn}=M_{sn}
  =\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert/\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert\equiv M\,.
  \tag{28.6.12}\label{eq:28.6.12}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.3}, we can express the gaugino masses
\eqref{eq:28.6.2} as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    m_{\mathrm{wino}}&=(g^2/16\pi^2)TM\,,\\
    m_{\mathrm{bino}}&=(5/3)(g'^2/16\pi^2)TM\,,\\
    m_{\mathrm{gluino}}&=(g_s^2/16\pi^2)TM\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.13}\label{eq:28.6.13}
\end{equation}
while the squared squark and slepton masses \eqref{eq:28.6.4} become
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    M_Q^2={}&2TM^2
      \left[
        \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
        +\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
        +\frac{5}{3}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
      \right],\\
    M_{\bar U}^2={}&2TM^2
      \left[
        \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
        +\frac{5}{3}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
      \right],\\
    M_{\bar D}^2={}&2TM^2
      \left[
        \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
        +\frac{5}{3}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
      \right],\\
    M_L^2={}&2TM^2
      \left[
        \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
        +\frac{5}{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2
          \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
      \right],\\
    M_{\bar E}^2={}&2TM^2\frac{5}{3}
      \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2 .
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.14}\label{eq:28.6.14}
\end{equation}
There is no special reason to expect that
\(\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\ll
\lvert\lambda_n\mathcal{S}\rvert^2\), but this assumption is not actually
very restrictive, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.6} already requires that
\(\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq
\lvert\lambda_n\rvert\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^2\), and it turns out that
the functions \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) do not differ much from unity for
\(x<1\) unless \(x\) is very close to 1.

The extreme simplicity of the results \eqref{eq:28.6.13} and
\eqref{eq:28.6.14} has been explained by Giudice and
Rattazzi~\hyperref[ch28-ref-32]{[32]} by using Seiberg's arguments of
holomorphy~\hyperref[ch28-ref-33]{[33]}, described in Section 27.6.
Suppose we introduce a superpotential for the messenger superfields like
\eqref{eq:28.6.5}, but with a single external singlet superfield \(S\):
\begin{equation}
  f(S,\Phi)=S\sum_n\lambda_n\Phi_n\bar\Phi_n\,.
  \tag{28.6.15}\label{eq:28.6.15}
\end{equation}
Among other effects the kinematic term for the gauge superfield \(V_i\)
(with \(i=3,2,1\) for \(SU(3)\), \(SU(2)\), and \(U(1)\)) in the
Wilsonian effective Lagrangian at a renormalization scale \(\mu\) will now
take the form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge},\mu}
  =\operatorname{Re}
  \left[
    \sum_iN_i(S,\mu)
      W_i^\alpha W_{i\alpha}
  \right]_{\mathcal{F}},
  \tag{28.6.16}\label{eq:28.6.16}
\end{equation}
with some functions \(N_i(S,\mu)\) replacing the factors
\(1/2g_i^2(\mu)\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.3.22}. (The \(\theta\)-term is
dropped here because it has no effect in perturbation theory. A sum is
implied over indices on \(W_{i\alpha}\) that label different members of
the adjoint representations of \(SU(3)\) and \(SU(2)\), which are not
explicitly shown.) The gauge coupling constant is now given by setting the
superfield \(S\) equal to the expectation value \(\mathcal{S}\) of its
scalar component
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{2g_i^2(\mu)}=N_i(\mathcal{S},\mu)\,.
  \tag{28.6.17}\label{eq:28.6.17}
\end{equation}
Also, recalling that \(W_{i\alpha}=\lambda_{i\alpha}+O(\theta)\) and
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.2.11}, the terms in the Lagrangian density
\eqref{eq:28.6.16} of second order in the gaugino fields are, up to
derivative terms,
\[
  -2\sum_i\operatorname{Re}
  \left[
    -iN_i(\mathcal{S},\mu)
      \lambda_i\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\bar\lambda_i
    +[N_i(S,\mu)]_{\mathcal{F}}
      \lambda_i^\alpha\lambda_{i\alpha}
  \right].
\]
This gives the gaugino masses
\begin{equation}
  m_{gi}(\mu)
  =\left|
    \frac{[N_i(S,\mu)]_{\mathcal{F}}}{2N_i(\mathcal{S},\mu)}
  \right|
  =g_i^2(\mu)\left|[N_i(S,\mu)]_{\mathcal{F}}\right|.
  \tag{28.6.18}\label{eq:28.6.18}
\end{equation}
Now let us consider the behavior of \(N_i(\mathcal{S},\mu)\) as a function
of a real positive \(\mathcal{S}\), with the phases of the messenger
superfields adjusted so that all \(\lambda_n\) are real and positive.
Suppose we fix the values of the gauge couplings \(g_i(\mu)\) at some
scale \(\mu=K\) above all the messenger particle masses. Taking account
of the change in the constants \(b_i\) in the renormalization group
equations \(\mu\,dg_i(\mu)/d\mu=b_i g_i^3\) as \(\mu\) passes through the
various messenger masses, the solution of this equation when \(\mu\) is
\emph{below} all the messenger particle masses takes the form
\[
  \frac{1}{g_i^2(\mu)}
  =\frac{1}{g_i^2(K)}
   -2b_i^{(0)}\ln\left(\frac{M_1}{K}\right)
   -2b_i^{(1)}\ln\left(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\right)
   -\cdots
   -2b_i^{(N)}\ln\left(\frac{\mu}{M_N}\right),
\]
where we label the messenger particles so that their masses
\(M_n=\lambda_n\mathcal{S}\) satisfy
\[
  M_1>M_2>\cdots>M_N\,,
\]
and \(b_i^{(n)}\) is calculated taking account only of particles with
masses less than \(M_n\). Since all \(M_n\) are proportional to
\(\mathcal{S}\), we see that \(N_i(\mathcal{S},\mu)\) has the
\(\mathcal{S}\) dependence
\begin{equation}
  N_i(\mathcal{S},\mu)
  =-b_i^{\mathrm{messenger}}\ln\mathcal{S}
   +\mathcal{S}\text{-independent terms},
  \tag{28.6.19}\label{eq:28.6.19}
\end{equation}
where
\(b_i^{\mathrm{messenger}}=b_i^{(0)}-b_i^{(N)}\) is the contribution to
\(b_i\) of all the messenger superfields. According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.9.45} (with \(C_{i1}=0\) and
\(C_{i2}^{f}=C_{i2}^{s}=\sum_nT_{in}\)), this is
\begin{equation}
  b_i^{\mathrm{messenger}}
  =\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\sum_nT_{in}\,.
  \tag{28.6.20}\label{eq:28.6.20}
\end{equation}
Since \(N_i(S,\mu)\) is required by supersymmetry to be a holomorphic
function of \(S\), we see that
\begin{equation}
  N_i(S,\mu)
  =-\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\sum_nT_{in}\ln S
   +S\text{-independent terms}.
  \tag{28.6.21}\label{eq:28.6.21}
\end{equation}
Expanding around \(S=\mathcal{S}\), to first order in \(\mathcal{F}\) we
have \([\ln\mathcal{S}]_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{S}\) and so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.18} gives the gaugino masses
\begin{equation}
  m_{gi}(\mu)
  =\frac{g_i^2(\mu)}{16\pi^2}\sum_nT_{in}
   \left|\frac{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{S}}\right|.
  \tag{28.6.22}\label{eq:28.6.22}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.3}, we see that this is the same as our previous
result \eqref{eq:28.6.13}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.14} for the squark and
slepton masses was obtained by Giudice and Rattazzi in a similar way, by
studying the kinematic terms for the quark and lepton superfields instead
of the gauge superfields.

Incidentally, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.13} and \eqref{eq:28.6.14} could be
obtained (in general with different values of \(M\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.13} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.14}) without a specific
dynamical assumption like Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.5}, if we supposed that the
supersymmetry-breaking sector respected invariance under some grand
unified group \(G\) that had both the known quarks and leptons and the
messenger fields as complete representations. In this case the
coefficients \(M_{gn}\) and \(M_{sn}\) in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.2} and
\eqref{eq:28.6.4} would have values \(M_g(d)\) and \(M_s(d)\),
respectively, that depend only on the irreducible representation \(d\) of
\(G\) to which the \(n\)th messenger fields belong. The sums of \(T_{in}\)
over \(n\) belonging to any irreducible representation \(d\) of \(G\)
have the same ratios as the sums in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.3}, so
\(\sum_{n\in d}T_{in}=k_iT(d)\), where \(k_3=k_2=1\), \(k_1=5/3\), and
therefore
\[
  \sum_nT_{in}M_{gn}
  =\sum_dM_g(d)\sum_{n\in d}T_{in}
  =k_iM_g\,,
\]
where \(M_g=\sum_dM_g(d)T(d)\). Likewise
\[
  \sum_nT_{in}M_{sn}^2
  =\sum_dM_s^2(d)\sum_{n\in d}T_{in}
  =k_iM_s^2\,,
\]
where \(M_s^2=\sum_dM_s^2(d)T(d)\).
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.2} and \eqref{eq:28.6.4} would then yield
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.13} and \eqref{eq:28.6.14}, except with \(M_g\) in
place of \(TM\) and \(M_s^2\) in place of \(2TM^2\). But the assumption
that \(M_{gn}\) and \(M_{sn}\) respect invariance under \(G\) is implausible
if the messenger mass scale is far below the grand unified scale, since
whatever we assume about a grand unified gauge group, the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge interactions would
make coupling constants like \(\lambda_n\) run differently for \(\Phi_n\)
with different \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) quantum
numbers in the same representation of the grand unified group.

These results are subject to various radiative corrections, of which the
most important is that we must use values of \(g_s\), \(g\), and \(g'\)
renormalized at a scale comparable to the mass being calculated. Indeed,
the gaugino mass ratios given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.13} could also be
derived under quite different assumptions: that all gaugino masses are
equal at the grand unification scale where the coupling constants are
related by \(g_s^2=g^2=5g'^2/3\) and become different at lower energies
as described by the renormalization group equations.

For an illustrative example of numerical results, suppose that the
messenger superfields form an \(SU(2)\) singlet \(SU(3)\) triplet with
charge \(e/3\) and an \(SU(2)\) doublet \(SU(3)\) singlet with charges 0
and \(-e\), together with left-chiral superfields in the complex-conjugate
representations of \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\).
Then, as already mentioned, Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.3} is satisfied with
\(T=2\mathbin{\cdot}1/2=1\), so by using the correct values of the gauge
couplings, the masses of the squarks, gluino, \(L\) sleptons, wino,
\(E\) sleptons, and bino are calculated to be in the
ratios~\hyperref[ch28-ref-34]{[34]}
\(11.6::7.0::2.5::2::1.1::1.0\). In larger representations of \(G\) we
could have \(T\gg1\), in which case the gluino would be the heaviest of
these particles, and the sleptons the lightest ones.

There are also radiative corrections aside from the running of the gauge
couplings. According to one calculation~\hyperref[ch28-ref-34]{[34]}, in
the model with an \(SU(2)\) singlet \(SU(3)\) triplet with charge \(e/3\)
and an \(SU(2)\) doublet \(SU(3)\) singlet with charges 0 and \(-e\),
together with left-chiral superfields in the complex-conjugate
representations of \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\),
radiative corrections give the ratios of the masses of the squarks,
gluino, \(L\) sleptons, wino, \(E\) sleptons, and bino as
\(9.3::6.4::2.6::1.9::1.35::1.0\).

As we saw in the previous section, the wino and bino may mix with the
charged and neutral higgsinos, so the wino and bino masses that have been
calculated here must be regarded as inputs to a calculation of physical
masses of mixtures known as \emph{charginos} and \emph{neutralinos},
rather than physical masses themselves.

Now we shall consider the masses of the Higgs scalars in these models. If
we were to consider only the two-loop diagrams by which these scalars get
masses through gauge interactions with the supersymmetry-breaking sector,
then since (apart from signs) they have the same
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) quantum numbers as the
left-handed lepton doublets, their masses would be given by a formula like
the fourth of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.4}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    [m_1^2]_{\text{2 loop}}=[m_2^2]_{\text{2 loop}}
    &=M_L^2\\
    &=\sum_nM_{sn}^2
      \left[
        \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{2n}
        +\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2
         \left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2T_{1n}
      \right]^2 .
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.23}\label{eq:28.6.23}
\end{equation}
If this were the whole story, then it would be impossible to meet the
condition for \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) breaking found in the previous
section that (unless \(\tan\beta\) is very close to unity) one of
\(m_1^2+\lvert\mu\rvert^2\) and \(m_2^2+\lvert\mu\rvert^2\) must be
negative. Fortunately, the large masses of the top quark and squark
produce a negative contribution to \(m_2^2\) that leads naturally to a
spontaneous breakdown of electroweak symmetry. The couplings of the Higgs
doublets to the third-generation quark superfields are described by the
superpotential
\begin{equation}
  f_{\text{3rd gen}}
  =\lambda_b(H_1^{\mathsf T}eQ)\bar B
   +\lambda_t(H_2^{\mathsf T}eQ)\bar T\,,
  \tag{28.6.24}\label{eq:28.6.24}
\end{equation}
where \(Q\) is the \(SU(2)\) quark doublet left-chiral superfield
\((T,B)\), while \(\bar T\) and \(\bar B\) are the left-chiral
superfields of the left-handed top and bottom antiquarks, and
\(\lambda_t\) and \(\lambda_b\) are the Yukawa couplings, related to the
\(t\) and \(b\) quark masses by \(m_t=\lambda_tv_2\) and
\(m_b=\lambda_bv_1\). The last term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.7} then gives
the terms in the potential involving an interaction between the squark
fields and Higgs fields as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    V_{\mathrm{sq}\,H}={}&
      \left|
        \lambda_b\mathcal{H}_1^-\bar{\mathcal B}
        +\lambda_t\mathcal{H}_2^0\bar{\mathcal T}
      \right|^2
      +\left|
        \lambda_b\mathcal{H}_1^0\bar{\mathcal B}
        +\lambda_t\mathcal{H}_2^+\bar{\mathcal T}
      \right|^2\\
      &+\lvert\lambda_b\rvert^2
      \left|\mathcal{H}_1^0\mathcal B-\mathcal{H}_1^-\mathcal T\right|^2
      +\lvert\lambda_t\rvert^2
      \left|\mathcal{H}_2^+\mathcal B-\mathcal{H}_2^0\mathcal T\right|^2 .
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.25}\label{eq:28.6.25}
\end{equation}
where script letters denote the scalar field components of superfields.
The squark loop contributions to the potential of \(\mathcal{H}_1\) and
\(\mathcal{H}_2\) are then
\begin{equation}
  V_H^{\text{squark loop}}
  =3\langle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^*\rangle
  \left[
    2\lvert\lambda_b\rvert^2(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1)
    +2\lvert\lambda_t\rvert^2(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2)
  \right],
  \tag{28.6.26}\label{eq:28.6.26}
\end{equation}
where \(\langle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^*\rangle\) is the vacuum expectation
value of the product of any one of the squark fields and its complex
conjugate at the same spacetime point. (In taking this to be the same for
all squark types, we are here using Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.4}, which tells us
that the squark masses \(M_Q\) do not vary much among
\(\mathcal{T}\), \(\mathcal{B}\), \(\bar{\mathcal{T}}\), and
\(\bar{\mathcal{B}}\) squarks. The factor 3 in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.26}
takes account of the three colors of each squark type.) The vacuum
expectation value \(\langle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^*\rangle\) is given in
lowest order by
\begin{equation}
  \langle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^*\rangle
  \equiv
  \langle\mathcal{S}(x)\mathcal{S}^*(x)\rangle_{\mathrm{VAC}}
  =\frac{-i}{(2\pi)^4}\int
    \frac{d^4p}{p^2+M_Q^2-i\epsilon}\,.
  \tag{28.6.27}\label{eq:28.6.27}
\end{equation}
This of course is divergent, but the contribution to supersymmetry-breaking
coefficients would be cancelled by quark loops if the squarks had the zero
bare mass of the quarks, so the effect of quark loops is to subtract from
\eqref{eq:28.6.27} the same expression with \(M_Q\) replaced with zero,
which with a Wick rotation becomes
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \langle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^*\rangle
    &\longrightarrow
    \frac{M_Q^2i}{(2\pi)^4}\int
      \frac{d^4p}
      {(p^2+M_Q^2-i\epsilon)(p^2-i\epsilon)}\\
    &=-\frac{M_Q^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int_0^{M^2}
      \frac{\pi^2\,dp^2}{p^2+M_Q^2}
    \simeq-\frac{M_Q^2}{16\pi^2}
      \ln\left(\frac{M^2}{M_Q^2}\right).
  \end{aligned}
\]
We have inserted an ultraviolet cut-off at the messenger mass \(M\),
because at momenta above \(M\) the squark mass must be replaced with a
momentum-dependent mass, which goes to the supersymmetric value zero at
very high momenta. Making this substitution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.26}
gives a net contribution to the potential due to squark and quark loops:
\begin{equation}
  (V_m)^{\text{3 loop}}
  =\frac{3M_Q^2}{16\pi^2}\ln\left(\frac{M^2}{M_Q^2}\right)
  \left[
    2\lvert\lambda_b\rvert^2(\mathcal{H}_1^\dagger\mathcal{H}_1)
    +2\lvert\lambda_t\rvert^2(\mathcal{H}_2^\dagger\mathcal{H}_2)
  \right].
  \tag{28.6.28}\label{eq:28.6.28}
\end{equation}
(This is a three-loop contribution, because the squared squark masses are
given by two-loop diagrams. There are also terms in the potential that are
quadratic in both the Higgs and squark superfields, arising from products
of the Higgs and squark terms in the squares of the
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge field \(D\)-components. These do not
make a three-loop contribution to the Higgs masses because the sum of each
\(SU(2)\times U(1)\) quantum number of the squarks vanishes, so
that their contributions to the \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) gauge field
\(D\)-components have zero vacuum expectation value.) Comparing
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.28} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.9} and adding the two-loop
contribution \eqref{eq:28.6.23} to the masses, we see then that
\begin{equation}
  m_1^2\simeq M_L^2
  -\frac{3M_Q^2\lvert\lambda_b\rvert^2}{8\pi^2}
   \ln\left(\frac{M^2}{M_Q^2}\right),
  \tag{28.6.29}\label{eq:28.6.29}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  m_2^2\simeq M_L^2
  -\frac{3M_Q^2\lvert\lambda_t\rvert^2}{8\pi^2}
   \ln\left(\frac{M^2}{M_Q^2}\right).
  \tag{28.6.30}\label{eq:28.6.30}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.14} and
\(\lvert\lambda_t\rvert=m_t/v_2
=m_t(2\sqrt{2}G_F)^{1/2}/\sin\beta\), we may write
\eqref{eq:28.6.30} as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    m_2^2\simeq2TM^2\Bigg[
      &\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
      +\frac{5}{12}\left(\frac{g'^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2\\
      &-\frac{\sqrt{2}G_Fm_t^2}{\pi^2\sin^2\beta}
       \left(\frac{g_s^2}{16\pi^2}\right)^2
       \ln\left(\frac{3}{8T(g_s^2/16\pi^2)}\right)
    \Bigg].
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.31}\label{eq:28.6.31}
\end{equation}
For \(T=1\), \(g_s^2/4\pi=0.118\), \(g^2/4\pi=0.0340\),
\(g'^2/4\pi=0.0101\), and \(m_t=180\ \mathrm{GeV}\), this is
\begin{equation}
  m_2^2\simeq M_L^2
  \left[1-\frac{3.06}{\sin^2\beta}\right],
  \tag{28.6.32}\label{eq:28.6.32}
\end{equation}
which is negative for all values of \(\beta\), thus providing a natural
mechanism for the spontaneous breaking of the electroweak gauge symmetry.
Also, \(M_L^2=(0.91\mathbin{\cdot}10^{-4})M^2/8\pi^2\). Unless
\(\tan\beta\) is huge, we have
\(\lvert\lambda_b\rvert\ll\lvert\lambda_t\rvert\), so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.6.27} gives
\begin{equation}
  m_1^2\simeq M_L^2\,.
  \tag{28.6.33}\label{eq:28.6.33}
\end{equation}

The aspect of electroweak phenomenology for which the predictions of
gauge-mediated supersymmetry models are most uncertain and unsatisfactory
has to do with the parameter \(\mu\) in the supersymmetry-preserving term
\(\mu[(H_1^{\mathsf T}eH_2)]_{\mathcal{F}}\) and the related
supersymmetry-breaking term \(B\mu\) in the Lagrangian density. These are
related because the interactions of the Higgs superfields with the gauge,
lepton, and quark superfields are invariant under a symmetry
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    H_1&\longrightarrow e^{i\varphi}H_1\,,
      &H_2&\longrightarrow e^{i\varphi}H_2\,,\\
    Q&\longrightarrow e^{-i\varphi}Q\,,
      &V_i&\longrightarrow V_i\,,\\
    \bar D&\longrightarrow\bar D\,,
      &\bar U&\longrightarrow\bar U\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{28.6.34}\label{eq:28.6.34}
\end{equation}
which in the absence of a superpotential term
\(\mu(H_1^{\mathsf T}eH_2)\) would forbid radiative corrections from
producing a term
\(B\mu\operatorname{Re}(\mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2)\) in
the scalar field potential.

It is not possible for \(B\mu\) to vanish because then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22} and the fact that (as we have seen) \(m_A\neq0\)
would imply that \(\sin2\beta=0\), or in other words either \(v_1=0\) or
\(v_2=0\), which would imply that either all the charge \(-e/3\) quarks
and charged leptons are massless or all the charge \(+2e/3\) quarks are
massless. (If \(B\mu=0\) and \(\mu=0\) then this problem persists to all
orders, because the appearance of non-zero vacuum expectation values for
both \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) would imply that symmetry under any combination
of the transformation \eqref{eq:28.6.34} and electroweak \(U(1)\) gauge
transformations is spontaneously broken, so the \(C\)-odd neutral scalar
would be a Goldstone boson with \(m_A=0\).) It is natural to try to account
for a non-zero value of \(B\mu\) as a radiative correction in a theory in
which symmetry under the transformation \eqref{eq:28.6.34} is explicitly
broken by a supersymmetric term
\(\mu[(H_1^{\mathsf T}eH_2)]_{\mathcal{F}}\) in the Lagrangian density.
This gives a very small value of \(B\mu\) at the messenger
scale~\hyperref[ch28-ref-34]{[34]}, although renormalization group effects
greatly increase \(B\mu\) at lower energies. According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22}, a relatively small \(B\mu\) would fit well with
the idea that the large mass of the top quark arises from a large value of
\(\tan\beta\). In any case, as already mentioned, the experimental lower
bound on the chargino mass tells us that \(\lvert\mu\rvert\) is at least
about \(60\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

The trouble is that the appearance of a non-zero value of \(\mu\)
resurrects the hierarchy problem that supersymmetry was supposed to solve:
instead of asking why the Higgs mass terms in the Lagrangian density are
so much smaller than the Planck mass or the mass at which gauge couplings
are unified, we now have to ask why \(\mu\) is this small?

The hierarchy problem would be put to rest if the Higgs superfields
interact with the supersymmetry-breaking sector in such a way that a term
\(\mu[(H_1^{\mathsf T}eH_2)]_{\mathcal{F}}\) is forbidden by some
symmetry, but appears when that symmetry is spontaneously broken. A
massless Goldstone boson may be avoided if the symmetry is discrete rather
than continuous. The simplest possibility is simply to extend the
symmetry transformation \eqref{eq:28.6.34} to include a transformation
\[
  S\longrightarrow e^{-2i\varphi}S\,,
\]
which would allow a term in the superpotential of the form
\[
  \lambda'S(\mathcal{H}_1^{\mathsf T}e\mathcal{H}_2).
\]
We can avoid a continuous symmetry by also including a term \(S^3\) in
the superpotential, so that the Lagrangian is invariant only under
transformations with \(\varphi\) a multiple of \(2\pi/3\), which is still
enough to forbid a non-zero bare value of \(\mu\). In this case the
appearance of non-zero vacuum values \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{F}\)
for the scalar and auxiliary components of \(S\) yields
\[
  B\mu=\lvert\lambda'\mathcal{F}\rvert\,,
  \qquad
  \mu=\lvert\lambda'\mathcal{S}\rvert\,.
\]
This would result in \(B\) having a very large
\(\lvert\lambda'\rvert\)-independent value \(M\), given by
\eqref{eq:28.6.12}, which is greater by a factor of order
\((g_s^2/16\pi)^{-1}\simeq100\) than the squark or gluino masses. But
then, since Eqs.~\eqref{eq:28.6.32} and \eqref{eq:28.6.33} give
\(m_1^2+m_2^2<0\), the stability condition \eqref{eq:28.5.11} would
require that \(\lvert\mu\rvert\geq M/2\), and hence
Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.5.22} would require that \(m_A\) is also much greater
than the squark and slepton masses. This is ruled out by the relation
\eqref{eq:28.5.23} and the estimates \eqref{eq:28.6.33} and
\eqref{eq:28.6.32} of \(m_1^2\) and \(m_2^2\) unless \(\tan\beta\) is
very close to unity.

We will see in Section 31.6 that theories of gravity-mediated
supersymmetry breaking naturally yield acceptable values of \(B\mu\) and
\(\mu\). Such theories are characterized by a very high energy scale of
supersymmetry breaking, in various versions of order either
\(10^{11}\ \mathrm{GeV}\) or \(10^{13}\ \mathrm{GeV}\). There have been
several suggestions~\hyperref[ch28-ref-35]{[35]} on ways to obtain
acceptable values of \(B\mu\) and \(\mu\) in theories in which
supersymmetry is broken at relatively low energy, such as theories of
gauge-mediated supersymmetry breaking, but none of them is particularly
compelling. Also, since we do not know where \(\mu\) comes from, we do not
have any reason to suppose that it is real, so theories of gauge-mediated
supersymmetry breaking are at risk of yielding too much \(CP\) violation,
just as in the more general framework of Section 28.4.

Like the scalar squared masses \(m_1^2\) and \(m_2^2\), the parameters
\(A_{ij}\) and \(C_{ij}\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:28.4.1} are given by two-loop
diagrams. However, they have the dimensions of mass rather than mass
squared, and they are much less than the scalar and gaugino masses, so
they make a relatively unimportant contribution to supersymmetry breaking.

As in any model with supersymmetry broken at energies much less than
\(10^{10}\ \mathrm{GeV}\), the lightest \(R\)-odd particle in all models
based on gauge-mediated supersymmetry breaking is the gravitino. As we
will see in Section 31.3, the gravitino mass is of the order of
\(\sqrt{G}\) times the squared energy \(F\) characteristic of
supersymmetry breaking, defined so that the vacuum energy is \(F^2/2\).
Where supersymmetry is broken by the \(\mathcal{F}\)-terms
\(\mathcal{F}_{n0}\) of the
\(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)-neutral chiral
superfields \(S_n\), we have
\(F^2=\sum_n\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n0}\rvert^2\). If there are no large
dimensionless quantities in the Lagrangian for the \(S_n\), then squark
masses in this model are of the order of
\(g_s^2\sqrt{F}/16\pi^2\simeq10^{-2}\sqrt{F}\), so in order for this to
be less than the naturalness bound of \(10^4\ \mathrm{GeV}\), we must have
\(\sqrt{F}<10^6\ \mathrm{GeV}\), which gives a gravitino mass less than
\(1\ \mathrm{keV}\). Gravitational couplings are so weak at accessible
energies that it is only the helicity \(\pm1/2\) states of the gravitino
that can actually be produced, and these behave just like goldstino
states. As shown by Eq.~\eqref{eq:27.5.12}, in the models discussed here
the goldstino field appears with coefficient \(i\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_n\)
in the fermionic component \(\psi_n\) of \(S_n\). Goldstinos are emitted
in the decay of the \(R\)-odd sparticles into the corresponding \(R\)-even
particles of the standard model through radiative corrections, with
goldstinos emerging from vertices connecting internal \(\psi_n\) and
\(\mathcal{S}_n\) lines. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:29.2.10}, the
goldstino emission amplitudes are inversely proportional to \(F\), which
makes these decays comparatively slow, although perhaps fast enough to be
observed.

Because the decay of \(R\)-odd particles into goldstinos is slow, it is
phenomenologically important in these models to identify the
next-to-lightest \(R\)-odd particle, into which all the heavier \(R\)-odd
particles will decay before it in turn decays into a goldstino. As we have
seen, the next-to-lightest \(R\)-odd particle is usually a slepton, wino,
or bino. (It is possible in models with a messenger superfield having the
same \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\) quantum numbers as
the Higgs doublets that the mixing between these superfields would lower
the mass of the doublet messengers so much that the lightest \(R\)-odd
particle would be the gluino~\hyperref[ch28-ref-35a]{[35a]}.) Detailed
calculations including the effects of \(SU(2)\times U(1)\)
breaking indicate that here is a large region of parameter space in which
the next-to-lightest \(R\)-odd particle is one of the two tau
sleptons~\hyperref[ch28-ref-35b]{[35b]}.
