\subsection{Superspace Integrals, Field Equations, and the Current Superfield}

The `\(\mathcal{F}\)-terms' and `\(D\)-terms' from which we construct the
Lagrangian density may be expressed as integrals over the superspace
coordinates \(\theta^\alpha,\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}\). The rules for integrals over fermionic
parameters originally given by Berezin~\hyperref[ch26-ref-4]{[4]} are derived
in Section 9.5. Briefly, because the square of any fermionic parameter
vanishes, any function of a set of \(N\) fermionic parameters \(\xi_n\) may
be expressed as
\begin{equation}
  f(\xi)
  =\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}\xi_n\right)c
   +\text{terms with fewer \(\xi\) factors}\,,
  \tag{26.6.1}\label{eq:26.6.1}
\end{equation}
and its integral over the \(\xi\)s is defined simply by
\begin{equation}
  \int d^N\xi\,f(\xi)\equiv c\,.
  \tag{26.6.2}\label{eq:26.6.2}
\end{equation}
The coefficient \(c\) may itself depend on other unintegrated c-number
variables that anticommute with the \(\xi\)s over which we integrate, in which
case it is important to standardize the definition of \(c\) by moving all
\(\xi\)s to the left of \(c\) before integrating over them, as we have done
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.1}. With this definition, integration over fermionic
variables is a linear operation. It resembles the integral over a real
variable in the sense that, since a shift \(\xi_n\longrightarrow\xi_n+a_n\)
of the variable \(\xi_n\) by a constant \(a_n\) changes the product
\(\prod_n\xi_n\) only by terms involving fewer \(\xi\) factors, it does not
affect the value of the integral
\begin{equation}
  \int d^N\xi\,f(\xi+a)=\int d^N\xi\,f(\xi)\,.
  \tag{26.6.3}\label{eq:26.6.3}
\end{equation}
Also, as a special case of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.2}, the integral over \(N\)
fermionic parameters of a polynomial of order \(<N\) vanishes. Integrals over
fermionic and bosonic parameters are strikingly different in the way they
respond to change of variables: for bosonic parameters \(x_n\) we have
\(d^Nx'=\operatorname{Det}(\partial x'/\partial x)\,d^Nx\), while for
fermionic parameters
\begin{equation}
  d^N\xi'
  =\left[\operatorname{Det}
    \left(\frac{\partial\xi'}{\partial\xi}\right)\right]^{-1}d^N\xi\,.
  \tag{26.6.4}\label{eq:26.6.4}
\end{equation}
In particular, the dimensionality of \(d\xi\) is \emph{opposite} to the
dimensionality of \(\xi\).

According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.10}, the \(D\)-term of a general superfield
\(S(x,\theta)\) (which may be elementary or composite) is equal, up to a
derivative term, to the coefficient of
\(\theta\theta\bar\theta\bar\theta/2\).  With the integration order
\(d^4\theta=d^2\theta\,d^2\bar\theta\), the component product is
\[
  \frac12\theta\theta\bar\theta\bar\theta
  =-2\theta_1\theta_2\bar\theta_{\dot1}\bar\theta_{\dot2}\,,
\]
so the coefficient of this function is \(-1/2\) the integral
over \(d^4\theta\). Since this is the \(D\)-term up to a derivative, we have
then
\begin{equation}
  \int d^4x\,[S]_D
  =-\frac12\int d^4x\int d^2\theta\,d^2\bar\theta\,
    S(x,\theta,\bar\theta)\,.
  \tag{26.6.5}\label{eq:26.6.5}
\end{equation}
In the same way, using Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.3.11}, we find that the spacetime
integral of the \(\mathcal{F}\)-term of a general left-chiral superfield
\(\Phi\) (again, either elementary or composite) may be expressed as
\begin{equation}
  \int d^4x\,[\Phi]_{\mathcal{F}}
  =\frac12\int d^4x\int d^2\theta\,
    \Phi(x,\theta,\bar\theta)\,.
  \tag{26.6.6}\label{eq:26.6.6}
\end{equation}

Since we are now integrating over \(\theta\)s, it is convenient to introduce
a delta function, defined as usual by the condition that for an arbitrary
function \(f(\theta)\)
\begin{equation}
  \int d^2\theta'\,d^2\bar\theta'\,
  \delta^4(\theta'-\theta)f(\theta',\bar\theta')
  =f(\theta,\bar\theta)\,.
  \tag{26.6.7}\label{eq:26.6.7}
\end{equation}
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:9.5.40}, this condition is satisfied by
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta^4(\theta'-\theta)
  &=\delta^2(\theta'-\theta)\,
    \delta^2(\bar\theta'-\bar\theta)\\
  &=\frac14
    (\theta-\theta')(\theta-\theta')
    (\bar\theta-\bar\theta')(\bar\theta-\bar\theta').
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.6.8}\label{eq:26.6.8}
\end{equation}

The representation of the action as an integral over superspace allows an
easy derivation of the field equations in superfield form. Consider, for
instance, the action for a set of left-chiral scalar superfields \(\Phi_n\)
(which includes as a special case the general renormalizable theory of
left-chiral superfields \(\Phi_n\)):
\begin{equation}
  I
  =\frac12\int d^4x\,[K(\Phi,\Phi^*)]_D
   +2\operatorname{Re}\int d^4x\,[f(\Phi)]_{\mathcal{F}}\,,
  \tag{26.6.9}\label{eq:26.6.9}
\end{equation}
with \(K\) an arbitrary function of \(\Phi_n\) and \(\Phi_n^*\) without
derivatives, and \(f\) an arbitrary function of \(\Phi_n\), also without
derivatives. (The motivation for this form of the action and the expression of
the action in terms of component fields is described in Section 26.8.) We
cannot derive correct field equations by simply demanding that this is
stationary with respect to arbitrary variations in \(\Phi\), because the
\(\Phi_n\) are constrained by the requirement
\(\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Phi_n=0\) for left-chiral superfields. To make sure
that this condition is preserved by any variation, we can use a trick that
will also turn out to be useful in deriving superspace Feynman rules in
Chapter 30. We write the \(\Phi_n\) in terms of \emph{potential superfields}
\(S_n(x,\theta)\) as
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_n=\bar D^2S_n,\qquad
  \bar D^2\equiv\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar D^{\dot\alpha}\,,
  \tag{26.6.10}\label{eq:26.6.10}
\end{equation}
from which it follows (using Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.21}) that
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_n^*=D^2S_n^*,\qquad
  D^2\equiv D^\alpha D_\alpha\,,
  \tag{26.6.11}\label{eq:26.6.11}
\end{equation}
An \(S_n\) satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.10} always exists, though it need
not be local. Indeed, for any left-chiral superfield \(\Phi_n\),
\begin{equation}
  \bar D^2D^2\Phi_n=-16\Box\Phi_n\,,
  \tag{26.6.12}\label{eq:26.6.12}
\end{equation}
so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.10} is satisfied by the solution of
\begin{equation}
  -16\Box S_n=D^2\Phi_n\,.
  \tag{26.6.13}\label{eq:26.6.13}
\end{equation}

The expression \(\bar D^2S\) is left-chiral for any \(S\), so the action
must be stationary with respect to arbitrary variations in the \(S_n\). Using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.5}, the action may be expressed in terms of the \(S_n\) and
\(S_n^*\) as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  I={}&-\frac14\int d^4x\int d^2\theta\,d^2\bar\theta\,
  K(D^2S^*,\bar D^2S)
  \\
  &+2\operatorname{Re}\int d^4x\,
  [f(\bar D^2S)]_{\mathcal{F}}\,.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.6.14}\label{eq:26.6.14}
\end{equation}
The variation of the first term under infinitesimal changes \(\delta S_n\) in
the \(S_n\) (but not the \(S_n^*\)) is easily calculated by an integration by
parts in superspace:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  &-\delta\frac14\int d^4x\int d^2\theta\,d^2\bar\theta\,
    K(D^2S^*,\bar D^2S)
  \\
  &\qquad=-\frac14\sum_n\int d^4x\int d^2\theta\,d^2\bar\theta\,
    \delta S_n\bar D^2
    \frac{\delta K(D^2S^*,\bar D^2S)}
         {\delta\bar D^2S_n}\,.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.3.31} and \eqref{eq:26.6.5} allow us to express the
variation of the integral in the superpotential term under infinitesimal
changes \(\delta S_n\) in the \(S_n\) as
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \delta\int d^4x\,[f(\bar D^2S)]_{\mathcal{F}}
  &=
  \sum_n\int d^4x
  \left[
    \left.
    \frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}
    \right|_{\Phi=\bar D^2S}
    \bar D^2\delta S_n
  \right]_{\mathcal{F}}
  \\
  &=
  \sum_n\int d^4x
  \left[
    \bar D^2\left(
      \left.
      \frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}
      \right|_{\Phi=\bar D^2S}
      \delta S_n
    \right)
  \right]_{\mathcal{F}}
  \\
  &=-2\sum_n\int d^4x
  \left[
    \left.
    \frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}
      \right|_{\Phi=\bar D^2S}
    \delta S_n
  \right]_D
  \\
  &=+\sum_n\int d^4x\int d^4\theta\,
    \left.
    \frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}
    \right|_{\Phi=\bar D^2S}
    \delta S_n\,.
  \end{aligned}
\]
The condition for Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.14} to be stationary with respect to
arbitrary variations in \(S_n\) is then
\[
  \bar D^2
  \frac{\delta K(D^2S^*,\bar D^2S)}
       {\delta\bar D^2S_n}
  =4
  \left.
  \frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}
  \right|_{\Phi=\bar D^2S}\,,
\]
or in terms of the chiral superfields
\begin{equation}
  \bar D^2
  \frac{\delta K(\Phi,\Phi^*)}{\delta\Phi_n}
  =4\frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}\,.
  \tag{26.6.15}\label{eq:26.6.15}
\end{equation}
The complex conjugate yields
\begin{equation}
  D^2
  \frac{\delta K(\Phi,\Phi^*)}{\delta\Phi_n^*}
  =4\left(\frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}\right)^* .
  \tag{26.6.16}\label{eq:26.6.16}
\end{equation}
It can readily be checked that the components of these equations yield the
field equations for the components of \(\Phi_n^*\) and \(\Phi_n\). For
instance, recalling that
\(\bar D^2(\bar\theta\bar\theta)=-4\), the
  \(\theta,\bar\theta\)-independent part of \(\bar D^2\Phi_n^*\) is
  \(-4\mathcal{F}_n^*\), while the \(\theta\)-independent part of
\(\partial f(\Phi)/\partial\Phi_n\) is
\(\partial f(\phi)/\partial\phi_n\), so the \(\theta\)-independent part of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.6.15} for \(K=\sum_n\Phi_n^*\Phi_n\) yields the relation
\(\mathcal{F}_n^*=-\partial f(\phi)/\partial\phi_n\), in agreement with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.6}.

As an example of the use of this formalism, let us consider the superfields to
which conserved currents belong. Suppose that the superpotential and Kahler
potential in the action \eqref{eq:26.6.9} are invariant under an infinitesimal
global transformation
\begin{equation}
  \delta\Phi_n=i\epsilon\sum_m\mathcal{T}_{nm}\Phi_m\,,
  \qquad
  \delta\Phi_n^*=-i\epsilon\sum_m\mathcal{T}_{mn}\Phi_m^*\,,
  \tag{26.6.17}\label{eq:26.6.17}
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon\) a real infinitesimal constant and \(\mathcal{T}_{nm}\) a
Hermitian matrix, perhaps part of a Lie algebra of similar transformation
matrices. Since the superpotential depends only on the \(\Phi_n\), it is
automatically also invariant under the extended transformations
\begin{equation}
  \delta\Phi_n=i\epsilon\Lambda\sum_m\mathcal{T}_{nm}\Phi_m\,,
  \qquad
  \delta\Phi_n^*=-i\epsilon\Lambda^*
    \sum_m\mathcal{T}_{mn}\Phi_m^*\,,
  \tag{26.6.18}\label{eq:26.6.18}
\end{equation}
where \(\Lambda(x,\theta)\) is a superfield that must be taken left-chiral in
order that the \(\delta\Phi_n\) be left-chiral, but is otherwise unrestricted.
On the other hand, other terms such as the Kahler potential are not in
general invariant under these transformations, because
\(\Lambda\neq\Lambda^*\). For general fields the change in the action must
therefore be of the form
\begin{equation}
  \delta I
  =i\epsilon\int d^4x\int d^2\theta\,d^2\bar\theta\,
    [\Lambda-\Lambda^*]\mathcal{J}\,,
  \tag{26.6.19}\label{eq:26.6.19}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{J}(x,\theta)\) is some real superfield, known as the
\emph{current superfield}. But if the field equations are satisfied then the
action is stationary under \emph{any} variation in the superfields, so the
integral \eqref{eq:26.6.19} must vanish for any left-chiral superfield
\(\Lambda(x,\theta)\). Any such \(\Lambda\) may be put in the form
\(\Lambda=\bar D^2S\), so this means that the current superfield must
satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \bar D^2\mathcal{J}=D^2\mathcal{J}=0\,.
  \tag{26.6.20}\label{eq:26.6.20}
\end{equation}
That is, \(\mathcal{J}\) is a \emph{linear} superfield. As we saw in Section
26.3, this means that its components satisfy
\begin{equation}
  N^{\mathcal{J}}=M^{\mathcal{J}}
  =\partial^\mu V_\mu^{\mathcal{J}}=0\,,
  \qquad
  \lambda_\alpha^{\mathcal{J}}
  =i\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}\partial_\mu
    \bar\omega^{\mathcal{J}\dot\alpha}\,,
  \qquad
  D^{\mathcal{J}}=-\Box C^{\mathcal{J}}\,.
  \tag{26.6.21}\label{eq:26.6.21}
\end{equation}
This allows us to identify the \(V\)-component \(V_\mu^{\mathcal{J}}\) as the
conserved current associated with this symmetry.

For the particular action \eqref{eq:26.6.9}, the current superfield takes the
form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{J}
  =\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial K(\Phi,\Phi^*)}{\partial\Phi_n}
    \mathcal{T}_{nm}\Phi_m
  =\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial K(\Phi,\Phi^*)}{\partial\Phi_n^*}
    \mathcal{T}_{mn}\Phi_m^*\,,
  \tag{26.6.22}\label{eq:26.6.22}
\end{equation}
with the equality of these two expressions a consequence of symmetry under
the transformation \eqref{eq:26.6.17}. Then, using the field equation
\eqref{eq:26.6.15},
\begin{equation}
  \bar D^2\mathcal{J}
  =\sum_{nm}
    \left[
      \bar D^2
      \frac{\partial K(\Phi,\Phi^*)}{\partial\Phi_n}
    \right]
    \mathcal{T}_{nm}\Phi_m
  =+4\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial f(\Phi)}{\partial\Phi_n}
    \mathcal{T}_{nm}\Phi_m\,,
  \tag{26.6.23}\label{eq:26.6.23}
\end{equation}
which vanishes because of the assumed invariance of the superpotential under
the transformation \eqref{eq:26.6.17}. In the same way, using the second of
the two expressions for \(\mathcal{J}\) and the field equation
\eqref{eq:26.6.16}, we find that \(D^2\mathcal{J}=0\), thus verifying
the conservation condition \eqref{eq:26.6.20}.
