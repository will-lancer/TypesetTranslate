\subsection[General Kahler Potentials]{General Kahler Potentials\footnotemark}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of
development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}

There are several circumstances in which it is necessary to consider
non-renormalizable Lagrangian densities of the general form
\eqref{eq:26.3.30}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  =2\operatorname{Re}[f(\Phi)]_{\mathcal{F}}
  +\frac12[K(\Phi,\Phi^*)]_D\,,
  \tag{26.8.1}\label{eq:26.8.1}
\end{equation}
where the superpotential \(f\) is an arbitrary function of left-chiral scalar
superfields \(\Phi_n\) but not their derivatives, and the Kahler potential
\(K\) is an arbitrary function of the \(\Phi_n\) and \(\Phi_n^*\) but not
their derivatives.

This situation arises in effective field theories whose symmetries rule out
any renormalizable interactions, or in which the renormalizable interactions
all happen to be small. It is often then possible to calculate scattering
amplitudes at low energy from tree graphs, using a Lagrangian with the smallest
value for some combination of the numbers of derivatives, fermion fields, and
any small renormalizable couplings. In Section 19.5 we examined such an
effective field theory with no renormalizable couplings, involving nucleons and
soft pions. The dynamically broken gauge theories discussed in Section 21.4
provide examples of effective field theories of this sort with small
renormalizable couplings. This circumstance also arises in supersymmetric
theories whose symmetries do not allow a superpotential, or where the
superpotential is for some reason small. We will encounter an example of this
sort when we consider the extended \(N=2\) supersymmetric theory of Abelian
gauge superfields and gauge-neutral chiral scalar superfields in Section 29.5.
We will show there that low-energy scattering amplitudes in this theory are
generated by tree graphs using a Lagrangian density of the form
\eqref{eq:26.8.1}, with \(f=0\) and \(K\) a function only of the \(\Phi_n\)
and \(\Phi_n^*\) but no derivatives, plus \(\mathcal{F}\)-terms quadratic in
gauge superfields. The inclusion of Kahler potentials having arbitrary
dependence on \(\Phi_n\) and \(\Phi_n^*\) but not on their derivatives is
particularly important in effective field theories in which some scalar
fields are of the same order of magnitude as the fundamental energy scale of
the underlying theory, although all other field values and all energies are
much smaller. This will be of interest, for instance, in connection with
theories of gravity-mediated supersymmetry breaking, discussed in Section
31.6.

Let us consider how to express the Lagrangian density \eqref{eq:26.8.1} in
terms of component fields. We did not use the assumption that \(f(\Phi)\) is
a \textit{cubic} polynomial in deriving Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.4}, so this
continues to give the \(\mathcal{F}\)-terms in the Lagrangian contributed by
an arbitrary superpotential. To derive the \(D\)-terms, we note that the term
in the Kahler potential of fourth order in \(\theta\) is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \Delta\Phi_n\equiv\Phi_n-\phi_n
  ={}&-\sqrt2\,\theta\psi_n+\theta^2\mathcal F_n
     -i\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta\,\partial_\mu\phi_n\\
    &+\frac{i}{\sqrt2}\theta^2\bar\theta
       \bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi_n
     +\frac14\theta^2\bar\theta^2\Box\phi_n ,
  \\
  K(\Phi,\Phi^*)\big|_{\theta^2\bar\theta^2}
  ={}&
  \left.
  \sum_{\substack{p,q\geq0\\1\leq p+q\leq4}}
  \frac{1}{p!\,q!}
  \sum_{\substack{n_1,\ldots,n_p\\m_1,\ldots,m_q}}
  \frac{\partial^{p+q}K}
       {\partial\phi_{n_1}\cdots\partial\phi_{n_p}
        \partial\phi_{m_1}^*\cdots\partial\phi_{m_q}^*}
  \prod_{r=1}^{p}\Delta\Phi_{n_r}
  \prod_{s=1}^{q}\Delta\Phi_{m_s}^*
  \right|_{\theta^2\bar\theta^2}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.8.2}\label{eq:26.8.2}
\end{equation}
Here a sum or product over an empty index list is understood to be one.
We can again use Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.A.18} and \eqref{eq:26.A.19} as well as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.9} to put the \(\theta\) dependence of this expression in
the form of an over-all factor \(\theta^2\bar\theta^2\), and find
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  K(\Phi,\Phi^*)\big|_{\theta^2\bar\theta^2}
  =-\frac12\theta^2\bar\theta^2
  \biggl\{&
  -\frac12\sum_n
    \frac{\partial K(\phi,\phi^*)}{\partial\phi_n}\Box\phi_n
  -\frac12\sum_n
    \frac{\partial K(\phi,\phi^*)}{\partial\phi_n^*}\Box\phi_n^*
  \\
  &+\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial^2K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m^*}
    \left[
      -i\bar\psi_m\bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi_n
      -i\psi_n\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\bar\psi_m
      -2\mathcal{F}_n\mathcal{F}_m^*
    \right]
  \\
  &+2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm\ell}
    \frac{\partial^3K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell^*}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)\mathcal{F}_\ell^*
  \\
  &-2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm\ell}
    \frac{\partial^3K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell^*}
    \bigl[-i\psi_m\sigma^\mu\bar\psi_\ell\bigr]
    \partial_\mu\phi_n
  \\
  &-\frac12\sum_{nm\ell k}
    \frac{\partial^4K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m
          \partial\phi_\ell^*\partial\phi_k^*}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)(\bar\psi_k\bar\psi_\ell)
  \\
  &+\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial^2K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m^*}
    \partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_m^*
  \\
  &-\frac12\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial^2K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m}
    \partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_m
  \\
  &-\frac12\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial^2K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n^*\partial\phi_m^*}
    \partial_\mu\phi_n^*\partial^\mu\phi_m^*
  \biggr\}.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.8.3}\label{eq:26.8.3}
\end{equation}
To make the reality properties of the fermion kinematic terms transparent, we
can use Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.21} to write
\[
  -i\psi_n\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\bar\psi_m
  =\left(-i\bar\psi_n\bar\sigma^\mu
    \partial_\mu\psi_m\right)^* .
\]
The \(D\)-term of \(K(\Phi,\Phi^*)\) is the coefficient of
\(\theta^2\bar\theta^2/2\) minus half the d'Alembertian of the
\(\theta\)-independent term in \(K(\Phi,\Phi^*)\), which is just
\(K(\phi,\phi^*)\), so
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \frac12[K(\Phi,\Phi^*)]_D
  ={}&
  \operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm}\mathcal{G}_{nm}
  \left[
    i\bar\psi_m\bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi_n
    +\mathcal{F}_n\mathcal{F}_m^*
    -\partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_m^*
  \right]
  \\
  &-\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm\ell}
    \frac{\partial^3K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell^*}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)\mathcal{F}_\ell^*
  \\
  &+\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm\ell}
    \frac{\partial^3K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell^*}
    \bigl[-i\psi_m\sigma^\mu\bar\psi_\ell\bigr]
    \partial_\mu\phi_n
  \\
  &+\frac14\sum_{nm\ell k}
    \frac{\partial^4K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m
          \partial\phi_\ell^*\partial\phi_k^*}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)(\bar\psi_k\bar\psi_\ell)\,,
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.8.4}\label{eq:26.8.4}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{G}(\phi,\phi^*)\) is the \textit{Kahler metric}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_{nm}(\phi,\phi^*)
  \equiv
  \frac{\partial^2K(\phi,\phi^*)}
       {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m^*}\,.
  \tag{26.8.5}\label{eq:26.8.5}
\end{equation}
Note that the constant matrix \(g_{nm}\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.2} is replaced
here with the Kahler metric \(\mathcal{G}_{nm}(\phi,\phi^*)\). Because the
Kahler metric is field-dependent, we cannot in general make it equal to the
unit matrix by a field redefinition, so the total Lagrangian must be left in
the form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L}={}&
  \operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm}\mathcal{G}_{nm}
  \left[
    i\bar\psi_m\bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi_n
    +\mathcal{F}_n\mathcal{F}_m^*
    -\partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_m^*
  \right]
  \\
  &-\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm\ell}
    \frac{\partial^3K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell^*}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)\mathcal{F}_\ell^*
  \\
  &+\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm\ell}
    \frac{\partial^3K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell^*}
    \bigl[-i\psi_m\sigma^\mu\bar\psi_\ell\bigr]
    \partial_\mu\phi_n
  \\
  &+\frac14\sum_{nm\ell k}
    \frac{\partial^4K(\phi,\phi^*)}
         {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m
          \partial\phi_\ell^*\partial\phi_k^*}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)(\bar\psi_k\bar\psi_\ell)
  \\
  &-\operatorname{Re}\sum_{nm}
    \frac{\partial^2f(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m}
    (\psi_n\psi_m)
  +2\operatorname{Re}\sum_n
    \mathcal{F}_n\frac{\partial f(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n}\,.
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.8.6}\label{eq:26.8.6}
\end{equation}
The antisymmetric part of the Hermitian Weyl kinetic form differs by a total
derivative and could be discarded if \(\mathcal{G}_{nm}\) were constant,
but must be kept for general Kahler potentials. This result will be extended
to include gauge superfields at the end of Section 27.4.

As discussed in Section 19.6, the spontaneous breakdown of a global symmetry
group \(G\) to a subgroup \(H\) entails the existence of a set of real
massless Goldstone bosons with scalar fields \(\pi_k\), for which the term in
the Lagrangian with the minimum number of derivatives takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{G/H}
  =-\sum_{k\ell}G_{k\ell}(\pi)
    \partial_\mu\pi_k\partial^\mu\pi_\ell\,,
  \tag{26.8.7}\label{eq:26.8.7}
\end{equation}
where \(G_{k\ell}(\pi)\) is the metric of the coset space \(G/H\). (Theories
with Lagrangian densities of this general form are known as
\textit{non-linear \(\sigma\)-models}.) By writing the complex fields
\(\phi_n\) in terms of their real and imaginary parts, the term
\(-\sum_{nm}\mathcal{G}_{nm}(\phi,\phi^*)
\partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_m^*\) in the Lagrangian density
\eqref{eq:26.8.6} can be put in the form \eqref{eq:26.8.7}, but the converse
is not generally true: the condition that a set of real coordinates like the
Goldstone boson fields \(\pi_k\) can be interpreted as the real and imaginary
parts of a set of complex coordinates like the fields \(\phi_n\) with the
metric in these coordinates locally given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.8.5} defines
what is called a \textit{Kahler manifold}.\footnote{The significance of
Kahler manifolds in this context was pointed out in an early paper by
Zumino~\hyperref[ch26-ref-8]{[8]}. Note that it is not necessary for the
metric to be expressible in the form \eqref{eq:26.8.5} over the whole
manifold with a single Kahler potential \(K(\phi,\phi^*)\); it is only
necessary that the manifold can be covered in finite overlapping patches in
which this holds, with different Kahler potentials in each patch. The
simplest example of a Kahler manifold is the flat complex plane, with Kahler
potential \(\lvert z\rvert^2\). As an example of a coset space \(G/H\) that
is a Kahler manifold, Zumino gave the case where
\(G=GL(p,\mathbb{C})\cdot GL(p+q,\mathbb{C})\) and
\(H=GL(p,\mathbb{C})\), with \(p\) and \(q\) arbitrary positive-definite
integers, and \(GL(N,\mathbb{C})\) the group of complex non-singular
\(N\cdot N\) matrices. The coset space \(G/H\) here has complex coordinates
\(\phi_n\) that may be taken as the components of a complex
\(p\cdot(p+q)\) matrix \(A\), which under \(G\) and \(H\) undergoes the
transformations \(A\to BAC\) and \(A\to BA\), respectively, where \(B\) and
\(C\) are square non-singular complex matrices of dimensionality \(p\) and
\(p+q\), respectively. The Kahler potential in this case is simply
\(K\propto\ln\det AA^\dagger\).} But it should not be thought that in the
common cases where \(G/H\) is \textit{not} a Kahler manifold, it is impossible
for \(G\) to be spontaneously broken to \(H\) while keeping supersymmetry
unbroken. What happens in these cases is the appearance of extra massless
bosons, which, together with the Goldstone bosons, do form a Kahler manifold.

This is because the superpotential \(f(\phi)\) depends on \(\phi\) but not
\(\phi^*\), so if the whole Lagrangian is invariant under a global symmetry
group \(G\) then the superpotential is automatically invariant under a group
\(G_{\mathbb{C}}\), the complexification of \(G\): if \(G\) consists of
transformations \(\exp(i\sum_A\theta_A t_A)\) with generators \(t_A\) and
arbitrary real parameters \(\theta_A\), then \(G_{\mathbb{C}}\) consists of
transformations \(\exp(i\sum_A z_A t_A)\) with the same generators and
arbitrary complex parameters \(z_A\). (For instance, if \(G\) is \(U(n)\)
then \(G_{\mathbb{C}}\) is \(GL(n,\mathbb{C})\), the group of all complex
non-singular matrices, while if \(G\) is \(SU(n)\) then \(G_{\mathbb{C}}\) is
\(SL(n,\mathbb{C})\), the group of all complex non-singular matrices with
unit determinant.) Likewise, if some stationary point \(\phi^{(0)}\) of
\(f(\phi)\) is left invariant by some subgroup \(H\) of \(G\), then it will
also be left invariant by a subgroup \(H_{\mathbb{C}}\) of
\(G_{\mathbb{C}}\), the complexification of \(H\). Whether or not \(G/H\) is
a Kahler manifold, the complexified coset space
\(G_{\mathbb{C}}/H_{\mathbb{C}}\) is always a Kahler manifold. This follows
because \(G_{\mathbb{C}}/H_{\mathbb{C}}\) is a complex submanifold of the flat
complex space of the \(\phi_n\), which is a Kahler manifold, and it is a
theorem that any complex submanifold of a Kahler manifold is a Kahler
manifold~\hyperref[ch26-ref-9]{[9]}. If parameterized by the values of
\(\phi_n(z)=[\exp(i\sum_A z_A t_A)\phi^{(0)}]_n\), the Kahler manifold
\(G_{\mathbb{C}}/H_{\mathbb{C}}\) has the metric obtained by embedding it in
the flat complex space of the \(\phi_n\), usually taken with line element
\(\sum_n d\phi_n\,d\phi_n^*\).

It is true that \(G_{\mathbb{C}}\) is not a symmetry of the whole Lagrangian,
but the Goldstone bosons associated with the breakdown of \(G_{\mathbb{C}}\)
to \(H_{\mathbb{C}}\) are nevertheless exactly massless. This is guaranteed
by the non-renormalization theorem of Section 27.6, or, more simply, by the
result of Section 25.4, that massless spin zero particles must come in
\textit{pairs} that are related by supersymmetry transformations and that
therefore have the same transformation under any global symmetry group \(G\)
that commutes with supersymmetry.
