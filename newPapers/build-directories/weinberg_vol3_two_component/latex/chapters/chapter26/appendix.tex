\chapterappendix{}{Two-Component Spinor Identities}

This appendix collects the algebraic identities for anticommuting
two-component spinors that are needed in dealing with superfields.  An
undotted spinor and its complex conjugate will be written
\[
  \zeta_\alpha=
  \begin{pmatrix}\zeta_1\\ \zeta_2\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bar\zeta_{\dot\alpha}=
  \begin{pmatrix}\zeta_1^*\\ \zeta_2^*\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus our conjugation convention is
\begin{equation}
  (\zeta_\alpha)^*=\bar\zeta_{\dot\alpha},
  \qquad
  (\bar\zeta_{\dot\alpha})^*=\zeta_\alpha .
  \tag{26.A.1}\label{eq:26.A.1}
\end{equation}
Complex conjugation also exchanges raised undotted and dotted indices:
\begin{equation}
  (\zeta^\alpha)^*=\bar\zeta^{\dot\alpha},
  \qquad
  (\bar\zeta^{\dot\alpha})^*=\zeta^\alpha .
  \tag{26.A.2}\label{eq:26.A.2}
\end{equation}

Indices are raised and lowered with
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
  =\epsilon_{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \epsilon^{\alpha\beta}
  =\epsilon^{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
  \tag{26.A.3}\label{eq:26.A.3}
\end{equation}
so \(\epsilon_{12}=+1\), \(\epsilon^{12}=-1\), and
\(\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon^{\beta\gamma}
=\delta_\alpha{}^\gamma\).  The invariant contractions are
\begin{equation}
  \zeta\chi\equiv\zeta^\alpha\chi_\alpha
  =-\zeta_\alpha\chi^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \bar\zeta\bar\chi
  \equiv\bar\zeta_{\dot\alpha}\bar\chi^{\dot\alpha}
  =-\bar\zeta^{\dot\alpha}\bar\chi_{\dot\alpha}.
  \tag{26.A.4}\label{eq:26.A.4}
\end{equation}
For example,
\[
  \zeta\chi
  =\zeta_1\chi_2-\zeta_2\chi_1,
  \qquad
  \bar\zeta\bar\chi
  =-\bar\zeta_{\dot1}\bar\chi_{\dot2}
   +\bar\zeta_{\dot2}\bar\chi_{\dot1}.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:26.A.2} and the ordering in
\eqref{eq:26.A.4} fix all conjugation signs below.

With \(\sigma^\mu=(\mathbf1,\boldsymbol{\sigma})\) and
\(\bar\sigma^\mu=(\mathbf1,-\boldsymbol{\sigma})\), index raising gives
\begin{equation}
  \bar\sigma^{\mu\,\dot\alpha\alpha}
  =\epsilon^{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}
    \sigma^\mu_{\beta\dot\beta},
  \qquad
  \sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}
  =\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}
    \bar\sigma^{\mu\,\dot\beta\beta}.
  \tag{26.A.5}\label{eq:26.A.5}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\bar\sigma^{\mu\,\dot\alpha\alpha}\) is obtained by raising both
spinor indices, consistently with Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.2}.  It should not be
confused with the ordinary matrix
Hermitian conjugate: the numerical matrices \(\sigma^\mu\) themselves are
Hermitian.
In particular,
\[
  \sigma^0=\bar\sigma^0=\mathbf1,
  \qquad
  \sigma^i=-\bar\sigma^i
  \quad(i=1,2,3).
\]
The Lorentz generators in the two inequivalent spinor representations are
\begin{equation}
  (\sigma^{\mu\nu})_\alpha{}^\beta
  \equiv\frac14
   (\sigma^\mu\bar\sigma^\nu-\sigma^\nu\bar\sigma^\mu)_\alpha{}^\beta,
  \qquad
  (\bar\sigma^{\mu\nu})^{\dot\alpha}{}_{\dot\beta}
  \equiv\frac14
   (\bar\sigma^\mu\sigma^\nu-\bar\sigma^\nu\sigma^\mu)
      ^{\dot\alpha}{}_{\dot\beta}.
  \tag{26.A.6}\label{eq:26.A.6}
\end{equation}

For Grassmann-odd spinors, applying the contraction convention
\eqref{eq:26.A.4} before exchanging the fields gives
\begin{equation}
  \zeta\chi=\chi\zeta,\qquad
  \bar\zeta\bar\chi=\bar\chi\bar\zeta,\qquad
  \zeta\sigma^\mu\bar\chi
  =-\bar\chi\bar\sigma^\mu\zeta,\qquad
  \zeta\sigma^{\mu\nu}\chi
  =-\chi\sigma^{\mu\nu}\zeta .
  \tag{26.A.7}\label{eq:26.A.7}
\end{equation}
The first equality, for example, follows component by component:
\[
  \chi_1\zeta_2-\chi_2\zeta_1
  =\zeta_1\chi_2-\zeta_2\chi_1 .
\]
For a single anticommuting spinor, the last identity therefore implies
\begin{equation}
  \zeta\sigma^\mu\bar\zeta
   +\bar\zeta\bar\sigma^\mu\zeta=0,
  \qquad
  \zeta\sigma^{\mu\nu}\zeta=0,
  \qquad
  \bar\zeta\bar\sigma^{\mu\nu}\bar\zeta=0 .
  \tag{26.A.8}\label{eq:26.A.8}
\end{equation}
This is the direct two-component counterpart of the vanishing covariants
used in the original discussion; Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.8} is often the
quickest way to eliminate an apparently allowed tensor term.

The fundamental same-spinor dyads are
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\alpha\theta_\beta
  =\frac12\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\theta^2,
  \qquad
  \bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}\bar\theta_{\dot\beta}
  =-\frac12\epsilon_{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}\bar\theta^2,
  \tag{26.A.9}\label{eq:26.A.9}
\end{equation}
where
\[
  \theta^2=2\theta_1\theta_2,
  \qquad
  \bar\theta^2=-2\bar\theta_{\dot1}\bar\theta_{\dot2}.
\]
The mixed dyad is equivalently a Lorentz vector:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\alpha\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}
  =-\frac12\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}
    (\theta\sigma_\mu\bar\theta).
  \tag{26.A.10}\label{eq:26.A.10}
\end{equation}
All rearrangements of two-component indices ultimately reduce to the
Schouten identity
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\gamma\delta}
  +\epsilon_{\alpha\gamma}\epsilon_{\delta\beta}
  +\epsilon_{\alpha\delta}\epsilon_{\beta\gamma}=0,
  \tag{26.A.11}\label{eq:26.A.11}
\end{equation}
and its dotted counterpart. In components this simply states that
\[
  \epsilon_{12}\epsilon_{12}
  +\epsilon_{11}\epsilon_{22}
  +\epsilon_{12}\epsilon_{21}=0 .
\]

There is no chirality projector to carry along in two-component notation:
the index type itself specifies the Lorentz representation,
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_\alpha:\ (\tfrac12,0),
  \qquad
  \bar\zeta_{\dot\alpha}:\ (0,\tfrac12).
  \tag{26.A.12}\label{eq:26.A.12}
\end{equation}
Thus the independent Grassmann monomials terminate as
\[
  1,\quad\theta_\alpha,\quad\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha},\quad
  \theta^2,\quad\bar\theta^2,\quad
  \theta_\alpha\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha},\quad
  \theta^2\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha},\quad
  \bar\theta^2\theta_\alpha,\quad
  \theta^2\bar\theta^2 .
\]
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.11}, the non-vanishing cubic products reduce to
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\alpha\theta_\beta\bar\theta_{\dot\gamma}
  =\frac12\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\theta^2
    \bar\theta_{\dot\gamma},
  \qquad
  \bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}\bar\theta_{\dot\beta}\theta_\gamma
  =-\frac12\epsilon_{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}\bar\theta^2\theta_\gamma .
  \tag{26.A.13}\label{eq:26.A.13}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
  \theta_\alpha\theta_\beta\theta_\gamma=0,
  \qquad
  \bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}\bar\theta_{\dot\beta}
    \bar\theta_{\dot\gamma}=0,
\]
while products with two indices of one type and one of the other are
proportional to the corresponding quadratic invariant.
Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.13} by a fourth coordinate, and using
\eqref{eq:26.A.14} below, gives the complete quartic reduction.
The intermediate dotted dyad is
\begin{equation}
  \theta^2\bar\theta_{\dot\gamma}\bar\theta_{\dot\delta}
  =-\frac12\epsilon_{\dot\gamma\dot\delta}
    \theta^2\bar\theta^2 .
  \tag{26.A.14}\label{eq:26.A.14}
\end{equation}
A second use of Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.11}, or direct use of the cubic
reduction, then gives
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\alpha\theta_\beta
  \bar\theta_{\dot\gamma}\bar\theta_{\dot\delta}
  =-\frac14\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
    \epsilon_{\dot\gamma\dot\delta}\theta^2\bar\theta^2 .
  \tag{26.A.15}\label{eq:26.A.15}
\end{equation}

Contracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.13} with an arbitrary undotted spinor gives
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\alpha(\theta\chi)
  =-\frac12\theta^2\chi_\alpha,
  \qquad
  \bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}(\bar\theta\bar\chi)
  =-\frac12\bar\theta^2\bar\chi_{\dot\alpha}.
  \tag{26.A.16}\label{eq:26.A.16}
\end{equation}
For example, the first identity is just
\[
  \theta_\alpha\theta^\beta\chi_\beta
  =-\frac12\delta_\alpha{}^\beta\theta^2\chi_\beta .
\]
The corresponding vector identities are
\begin{equation}
  (\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)\theta_\alpha
  =-\frac12\theta^2(\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)_\alpha,
  \qquad
  (\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}
  =-\frac12(\theta\sigma^\mu)_{\dot\alpha}\bar\theta^2 .
  \tag{26.A.17}\label{eq:26.A.17}
\end{equation}
From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.A.16} and \eqref{eq:26.A.17} follow the Fierz
identities most often used in superfield multiplication:
\begin{equation}
  (\theta\chi)(\theta\eta)
  =-\frac12\theta^2(\chi\eta),
  \qquad
  (\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)
  (\theta\sigma^\nu\bar\theta)
  =-\frac12\eta^{\mu\nu}\theta^2\bar\theta^2 .
  \tag{26.A.18}\label{eq:26.A.18}
\end{equation}
The useful matrix form of the quartic identity is
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\alpha\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}
  (\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)
  =\frac14\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}
    \theta^2\bar\theta^2 .
  \tag{26.A.19}\label{eq:26.A.19}
\end{equation}

It remains to record reality properties.  With the convention
\eqref{eq:26.A.1}, the ordering convention \eqref{eq:26.A.4}, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.A.2}, complex conjugation gives
\[
  (\zeta^\alpha\chi_\alpha)^*
  =\bar\zeta_{\dot\alpha}\bar\chi^{\dot\alpha},
  \qquad
  (\zeta\sigma^\mu\bar\chi)^*
  =\chi\sigma^\mu\bar\zeta .
\]
The complete set needed here may be summarized as
\begin{equation}
  (\zeta\chi)^*=\bar\zeta\bar\chi,\qquad
  (\zeta\sigma^\mu\bar\chi)^*
    =\chi\sigma^\mu\bar\zeta,\qquad
  (\zeta\sigma^{\mu\nu}\chi)^*
    =\bar\chi\bar\sigma^{\mu\nu}\bar\zeta .
  \tag{26.A.20}\label{eq:26.A.20}
\end{equation}
The sigma-matrix conjugation used here follows from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.4.40} after reduction to the generators
\eqref{eq:26.A.6}.  For later reference, the ordered conjugation rules,
including the squared-superderivative rule for a Grassmann-even superfield
\(S\), are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  (AB)^*&=B^*A^*,
  &(\zeta\chi)^*&=\bar\zeta\bar\chi,
  &(\zeta\sigma^{\mu\nu}\chi)^*
    &=\bar\chi\bar\sigma^{\mu\nu}\bar\zeta,
  \\
  \left(-i\zeta\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\bar\chi\right)^*
    &=-i\bar\zeta\bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\chi,
  &(\zeta\sigma^\mu\bar\zeta)^*
    &=\zeta\sigma^\mu\bar\zeta,
  \\
  \left(\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar D^{\dot\alpha}S\right)^*
    &=D^\alpha D_\alpha S^* .
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{26.A.21}\label{eq:26.A.21}
\end{equation}
Indeed,
\(\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar D^{\dot\alpha}
=-2\bar D_{\dot1}\bar D_{\dot2}\) whereas
\(D^\alpha D_\alpha=+2D_1D_2\).  Complex conjugation reverses the two
odd derivatives, so these two component signs give the last equality in
the ordered conjugation table.

The sigma matrices provide a complete basis for mixed index pairs, and their
ordered products separate into scalar and Lorentz-generator parts:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
  \sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}
  \bar\sigma_{\mu}^{\dot\beta\beta}
  =-2\delta_\alpha{}^\beta
    \delta_{\dot\alpha}{}^{\dot\beta},
  \qquad
  \sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\alpha}
  \sigma_{\mu\,\beta\dot\beta}
  =-2\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\dot\alpha\dot\beta},
  \\
  \sigma^\mu\bar\sigma^\nu
  =-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1+2\sigma^{\mu\nu},
  \qquad
  \bar\sigma^\mu\sigma^\nu
  =-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1+2\bar\sigma^{\mu\nu},
  \\
  \sigma^\mu\bar\sigma^\nu+\sigma^\nu\bar\sigma^\mu
  =-2\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1,
  \qquad
  \bar\sigma^\mu\sigma^\nu+\bar\sigma^\nu\sigma^\mu
  =-2\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1 .
  \end{gathered}
\]

Finally, the statement that a general Dirac fermion is a sum of two
self-conjugate fermions has a direct two-component form.  Let
\(u_\alpha\) and \(v_\alpha\) be its two independent Weyl fields.  They may
be written in terms of two Weyl fields \(\chi_{+\alpha}\) and
\(\chi_{-\alpha}\), each paired with its own dotted conjugate, as
\begin{equation}
  u_\alpha=\chi_{+\alpha}+i\chi_{-\alpha},
  \qquad
  v_\alpha=\chi_{+\alpha}-i\chi_{-\alpha}.
  \tag{26.A.22}\label{eq:26.A.22}
\end{equation}
The two self-conjugate pairs are obtained explicitly from
\begin{equation}
  \chi_{+\alpha}\equiv\frac12(u_\alpha+v_\alpha),
  \qquad
  \chi_{-\alpha}\equiv\frac1{2i}(u_\alpha-v_\alpha),
  \qquad
  (\chi_{\pm\alpha})^*=\bar\chi_{\pm\dot\alpha}.
  \tag{26.A.23}\label{eq:26.A.23}
\end{equation}
