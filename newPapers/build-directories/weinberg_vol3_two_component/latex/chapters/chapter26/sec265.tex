\subsection{Spontaneous Supersymmetry Breaking in the Tree Approximation}

We saw in the previous section that supersymmetry is unbroken (at least in the
tree approximation) in renormalizable theories of chiral superfields if
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.8} has a solution, that is, if there is a value $\phi_0$ of
the fields at which the superpotential $f(\phi)$ is stationary:
\begin{equation}
  \left.
  \frac{\partial f(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n}
  \right|_{\phi=\phi_0}=0\,.
  \tag{26.5.1}\label{eq:26.5.1}
\end{equation}
There are as many independent variables here as there are equations to
satisfy, so we generally expect there to be solutions of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.1}. In order for supersymmetry to be spontaneously broken
in these theories, it is necessary to impose restrictions on the form of the
superpotential.

To see how a choice of superpotential may allow supersymmetry to be
spontaneously broken, we will consider a generalization of a class of models
due to O'Raifeartaigh~\hyperref[ch26-ref-3]{[3]}. Suppose that the
superpotential is a linear combination of a set $Y_i$ of left-chiral
superfields, with coefficients given by functions $f_i(X)$ of a second set of
left-chiral superfields $X_n$:
\begin{equation}
  f(X,Y)=\sum_i Y_i f_i(X)\,.
  \tag{26.5.2}\label{eq:26.5.2}
\end{equation}
The conditions for supersymmetry to be unbroken by the values $x_n$ and $y_i$
of the scalar components of these superfields are that
\begin{equation}
  0=\frac{\partial f(x,y)}{\partial y_i}=f_i(x)\,,
  \tag{26.5.3}\label{eq:26.5.3}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  0=\frac{\partial f(x,y)}{\partial x_n}
   =\sum_i y_i\frac{\partial f_i(x)}{\partial x_n}\,.
  \tag{26.5.4}\label{eq:26.5.4}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.4} can always be solved by taking $y_i=0$, with no effect
on the problem of solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.3}. On the other hand, if the
number of $X_n$ superfields is smaller than the number of $Y_i$ superfields,
then Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.3} imposes more conditions on the $x_n$ than there are
variables, so without fine-tuning a solution is impossible, and
supersymmetry is broken.

It may appear that the initial assumption \eqref{eq:26.5.2} itself represents
a radical form of fine-tuning, but in fact this form can be imposed on the
superpotential by assuming a suitable $R$-symmetry. As discussed in Section
26.3, in theories with $N=1$ supersymmetry an $R$-symmetry is a $U(1)$
symmetry for which the $\theta$ superspace coordinate has a non-trivial
transformation property. If we assume an $R$-symmetry for which \(\theta\)
carries quantum number $+1$, then the $\mathcal{F}$-term of any
superpotential has a
quantum number equal to that of the superpotential itself minus 2, so $R$
invariance requires the superpotential itself to have $R=2$. We can
therefore impose the structure \eqref{eq:26.5.2} by requiring $R$ invariance,
with the $Y_i$ and $X_n$ superfields given $R$ quantum numbers $+2$ and 0,
respectively.

The scalar fields in this sort of model have a potential
\begin{equation}
  V(x,y)
  =\sum_i\lvert f_i(x)\rvert^2
   +\sum_n\left\lvert
     \sum_i y_i\frac{\partial f_i(x)}{\partial x_n}
   \right\rvert^2 .
  \tag{26.5.5}\label{eq:26.5.5}
\end{equation}
The potential is always minimized by choosing the $x_n$ to minimize the first
term; whatever values this gives the $x_n$, the second term can always be
minimized by taking $y_i=0$. Whether or not supersymmetry can be
spontaneously broken, these models have the peculiar feature that there are
always directions in the space of the fields in which the minimum of the
potential is flat. Whatever values $x_{n0}$ of the $x_n$ minimize the first
term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.5}, the second term vanishes not only for $y_i=0$,
but for any vector $y_i$ in a direction orthogonal to all the vectors
$(v^n)_i=(\partial f_i(x)/\partial x_n)_{x=x_0}$. If there are $N_X$
superfields $X_n$ and $N_Y$ superfields $Y_i$ with $N_Y>N_X$, then the $v^n$
cannot span the space of the $y$s, and there will be at least $N_Y-N_X$ of
these flat directions. For any non-vanishing values $y_i=y_{0\ii}$ along any
one of these flat directions, the $R$-symmetry of the Lagrangian density is
spontaneously broken, and the Goldstone boson field $\phi$ associated with
this global symmetry breakdown corresponds to a term $\propto y_{0\ii}$ in the
$y_i$.

The simplest example of this class of models is provided by the case where
there is just one $X$ superfield and two $Y$ superfields. Renormalizability
requires the coefficient functions $f_i(X)$ to be quadratic functions of
$X$, and by taking suitable linear combinations of the $Y_i$ and shifting and
rescaling $X$ we can choose these functions so that
\begin{equation}
  f_1(X)=X-a\,,
  \qquad
  f_2(X)=X^2\,,
  \tag{26.5.6}\label{eq:26.5.6}
\end{equation}
with an arbitrary constant $a$. There clearly is no simultaneous solution of
the two equations \eqref{eq:26.5.1} unless the superpotential is fine-tuned so
that $a=0$. The potential \eqref{eq:26.5.5} here is
\begin{equation}
  V(x,y)=\lvert x\rvert^4+\lvert x-a\rvert^2
          +\lvert y_1+2xy_2\rvert^2 .
  \tag{26.5.7}\label{eq:26.5.7}
\end{equation}
The sum of the first two terms has a unique global minimum $x_0$. The flat
direction here is the one for which $y_1+2x_0y_2=0$. For $a=0$ we have
$x_0=0$, and the minima of the potential are along the line with $y_1=0$ and
$y_2$ arbitrary.

Whatever the reason for a spontaneous breakdown of supersymmetry, this
phenomenon always entails the existence of a massless spin $1/2$ particle,
the \textit{goldstino}, analogous to the Goldstone bosons associated with the
spontaneous breakdown of ordinary global symmetries. (The one exception,
discussed in Section 31.3, is that in supergravity theories, where
supersymmetry is a local symmetry, the goldstino appears as the helicity
$\pm1/2$ states of a massive particle of spin $3/2$, the gravitino.) In
renormalizable theories of chiral superfields, the tree-approximation vacuum
expectation values $\phi_{n0}$ of the scalar fields must be at a minimum of
the potential $\sum_n\lvert\partial f(\phi)/\partial\phi_n\rvert^2$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.7}, so
\begin{equation}
  \sum_m \mathcal{M}_{nm}
  \left(
    \left.
    \frac{\partial f(\phi)}{\partial\phi_m}
    \right|_{\phi=\phi_0}
  \right)^*=0\,,
  \tag{26.5.8}\label{eq:26.5.8}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}_{nm}\equiv
  \left.
  \frac{\partial^2f(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m}
  \right|_{\phi=\phi_0}.
  \tag{26.5.9}\label{eq:26.5.9}
\end{equation}
If Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.1} is not satisfied, then Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.5.8} tells
us that the matrix $\mathcal{M}_{nm}$ has at least one eigenvector with
eigenvalue zero, so according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.4.10} there must be at
least one linear combination of the spin $1/2$ particles described by
$\psi_n$ with zero mass. For instance, for the model defined by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.5.2} and \eqref{eq:26.5.6}, the matrix $\mathcal{M}$ has the
non-vanishing components
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}_{xy_1}=\mathcal{M}_{y_1x}=1\,,
  \qquad
  \mathcal{M}_{xy_2}=\mathcal{M}_{y_2x}=2x_0\,,
  \tag{26.5.10}\label{eq:26.5.10}
\end{equation}
so this matrix has eigenvalues $\pm2x_0$ and 0, with the last eigenvalue
corresponding to the goldstino mode. In Chapter 29 we will show without the
use of perturbation theory that the spontaneous breakdown of supersymmetry
requires the existence of goldstinos, and explore their general properties.
