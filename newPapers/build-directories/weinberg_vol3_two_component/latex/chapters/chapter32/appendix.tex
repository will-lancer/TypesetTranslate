\chapterappendix{}{Spinors in Higher Dimensions}

This appendix describes the fundamental spinor representations of the Lie
algebra of the Lorentz group \(O(d-1,1)\) in any number \(d\) of spacetime
dimensions. These are obtained from the corresponding Clifford algebra,
consisting of an irreducible set of finite matrices \(\gamma_\mu\) that
satisfy the anticommutation relations
\begin{equation}
  \{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\}=2\eta_{\mu\nu}\,,
  \tag{32.A.1}\label{eq:32.A.1}
\end{equation}
where \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\) is diagonal, with elements \(+1\) on the diagonal
except for \(\eta_{00}=-1\), where \(x^0\) is the time-component. From these
we can construct matrices
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}
  \equiv\frac{1}{4i}[\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]
  =-\mathcal J_{\nu\mu}\,,
  \tag{32.A.2}\label{eq:32.A.2}
\end{equation}
that satisfy the commutation relations \eqref{eq:2.4.12} of the Lorentz
group generators
\begin{equation}
  i[\mathcal J_{\mu\nu},\mathcal J_{\rho\sigma}]
  =\eta_{\nu\rho}\mathcal J_{\mu\sigma}
   -\eta_{\mu\rho}\mathcal J_{\nu\sigma}
   -\eta_{\sigma\mu}\mathcal J_{\rho\nu}
   +\eta_{\sigma\nu}\mathcal J_{\rho\mu}\,.
  \tag{32.A.3}\label{eq:32.A.3}
\end{equation}
As we will see, although Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.2} always gives a representation
of the Lorentz algebra, it is not always an irreducible representation.

We must now distinguish between the cases of even and odd dimensionality.

\subsubsection*{Even Dimensions: \(d=2n\)}

To construct a convenient specific representation for the gamma matrices in
\(d=2n\) dimensions, we introduce \(n\) matrices
\begin{equation}
  a_u\equiv\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\gamma_{2u-1}+i\gamma_{2u}\bigr),
  \qquad u=1,2,\ldots,n\,,
  \tag{32.A.4}\label{eq:32.A.4}
\end{equation}
and take \(\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_{2n}\) as Hermitian, it being understood
that as usual
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2n}\equiv-i\gamma_0\,.
  \tag{32.A.5}\label{eq:32.A.5}
\end{equation}
These have the anticommutation relations
\begin{equation}
  \{a_u,a_v^\dagger\}=\delta_{uv}\,,
  \qquad
  \{a_u,a_v\}=\{a_u^\dagger,a_v^\dagger\}=0\,.
  \tag{32.A.6}\label{eq:32.A.6}
\end{equation}
We introduce a vector \(\ket{0}\) in the representation space of the
\(\gamma\)s, defined by the condition
\begin{equation}
  a_u^\dagger\ket{0}=0\,,
  \tag{32.A.7}\label{eq:32.A.7}
\end{equation}
and define the basis vectors
\begin{equation}
  \ket{s_1s_2\cdots s_n}
  =a_1^{s_1}a_2^{s_2}\cdots a_n^{s_n}\ket{0}\,.
  \tag{32.A.8}\label{eq:32.A.8}
\end{equation}
Because \(a_u^2=0\), the operator \(a_u\) raises the value of \(s_u\) to
\(+1\) if \(s_u=0\) and annihilates the vector if \(s_u=+1\) (as well as
yielding a sign \((-1)^S\) where \(S\equiv\sum_{v<u}s_v\)), so all \(s_u\)
take only the values 0 and \(+1\), and the vectors therefore span a space
of dimensionality \(2^n\). In this basis, the matrices \(a_u\) take the
form
\begin{equation}
  a_u=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\
    0&1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes\cdots\otimes
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\
    0&1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&1\\
    0&0
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes\mathbf{1}\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}\,,
  \tag{32.A.9}\label{eq:32.A.9}
\end{equation}
with the last \(2\times2\) matrix in the \(u\)th place. Taking the Hermitian
and anti-Hermitian parts then gives the gamma matrices
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2u-1}=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\
    0&1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes\cdots\otimes
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\
    0&1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&1\\
    1&0
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes\mathbf{1}\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}\,,
  \tag{32.A.10}\label{eq:32.A.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2u}=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\
    0&1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes\cdots\otimes
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\
    0&1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&-i\\
    i&0
  \end{pmatrix}
  \otimes\mathbf{1}\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}\,.
  \tag{32.A.11}\label{eq:32.A.11}
\end{equation}
(Note that this does not give the same representation of the gamma matrices
in four spacetime dimensions as was introduced in Section 5.4, and has been
used throughout this book.)

The representation \eqref{eq:32.A.10}--\eqref{eq:32.A.11} gives the
Euclidean \(\gamma\)s the simple reality and symmetry properties
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_i^*=\gamma_i^{\mathrm T}
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \gamma_i\,,&\text{for \(i\) odd},\\
    -\gamma_i\,,&\text{for \(i\) even},
  \end{cases}
  \tag{32.A.12}\label{eq:32.A.12}
\end{equation}
where \(i=1,2,\ldots,2n\). This can be expressed as a similarity relation
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C^{-1}\gamma_i\mathcal C
  =(-1)^n\gamma_i^{\mathrm T}
  =(-1)^n\gamma_i^*\,,
  \tag{32.A.13}\label{eq:32.A.13}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal C\) is the matrix
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\equiv\gamma_2\gamma_4\cdots\gamma_{2n}\,.
  \tag{32.A.14}\label{eq:32.A.14}
\end{equation}

Taking account of the factor \(-i\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.5}, we can write
this in terms of Minkow\-skian components as
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_\mu^*
  =-\beta\gamma_\mu^{\mathrm T}\beta
  =-(-1)^n(\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}\gamma_\mu(\mathcal C\beta)\,,
  \tag{32.A.15}\label{eq:32.A.15}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \beta\equiv\gamma_{2n}=-i\gamma_0\,.
  \tag{32.A.16}\label{eq:32.A.16}
\end{equation}

In any even dimension we may define a matrix \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) that plays
a role similar to that of \(\gamma_5\) in four dimensions. We take
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2n+1}\equiv i^n\gamma_1\gamma_2\cdots\gamma_{2n}\,.
  \tag{32.A.17}\label{eq:32.A.17}
\end{equation}
The phase here is chosen so that
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2n+1}^2=\mathbf{1}\,.
  \tag{32.A.18}\label{eq:32.A.18}
\end{equation}
From the anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:32.A.1}, it follows immediately
that \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) anticommutes with the other gamma matrices
\begin{equation}
  \{\gamma_{2n+1},\gamma_\mu\}=0
  \qquad\text{for \(\mu=1,2,\ldots,2n-1,0\)}\,.
  \tag{32.A.19}\label{eq:32.A.19}
\end{equation}
It is straightforward to check that \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) is real and symmetric
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2n+1}^\dagger
  =\gamma_{2n+1}^*
  =\gamma_{2n+1}^{\mathrm T}
  =\gamma_{2n+1}\,.
  \tag{32.A.20}\label{eq:32.A.20}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.19} we see that \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) commutes with the
generators \eqref{eq:32.A.2} of the \(O(2n-1,1)\) algebra:
\begin{equation}
  [\gamma_{2n+1},\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}]=0\,,
  \tag{32.A.21}\label{eq:32.A.21}
\end{equation}
so that the \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\) cannot furnish an \textit{irreducible}
representation of the algebra of \(O(2n-1,1)\). Instead, we may define a
pair of `Weyl' irreducible representations by projecting out the subspaces
with \(\gamma_{2n+1}=\pm1\):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}
  \equiv\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}
  \left(\frac{1\pm\gamma_{2n+1}}{2}\right).
  \tag{32.A.22}\label{eq:32.A.22}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.15} and the relation
\((\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}\gamma_{2n+1}\mathcal C\beta
=-(-1)^n\gamma_{2n+1}\) we find that the complex conjugate and transpose
of the Weyl Lorentz generators are
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\bigr)^*
  =-(\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}
    \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\mp(-1)^n}
    (\mathcal C\beta)\,,
  \tag{32.A.23}\label{eq:32.A.23}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\bigr)^{\mathrm T}
  =-\mathcal C^{-1}\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm(-1)^n}\mathcal C\,.
  \tag{32.A.24}\label{eq:32.A.24}
\end{equation}
Hence, for \(n\) even, the Weyl irreducible representations are equivalent
to complex conjugates of each other, while for \(n\) odd each is equivalent
to its own complex conjugate.\footnote{We say that one representation of
the Lorentz algebra by matrices \(\mathcal L_{\mu\nu}\) (such as
\(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\) or \(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^+\) or
\(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^-\)) is the complex conjugate of another
representation by matrices \(\mathcal L'_{\mu\nu}\) if
\(\mathcal L'_{\mu\nu}=-\mathcal L_{\mu\nu}^*\). The minus sign is included
because the matrices that represent elements of the Lorentz group in the
neighborhood of the identity are of the form
\(\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{2}i\omega^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L_{\mu\nu}\), with
\(\omega^{\mu\nu}\) real infinitesimals.} For \(n\) odd, we still have to
decide whether the Weyl representations are \textit{real}, which would mean
that there is a matrix \(\mathcal S\) such that
\begin{equation}
  -\bigl(\mathcal S\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\mathcal S^{-1}\bigr)^*
  =\mathcal S\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\mathcal S^{-1}\,,
  \tag{32.A.25}\label{eq:32.A.25}
\end{equation}
or \textit{pseudoreal}, in which case there is no such \(\mathcal S\).
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.23}, the condition \eqref{eq:32.A.25} may be
written as the requirement that
\(\mathcal S^{-1}\mathcal S^*(\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}\) commutes with all
\(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\). Since the matrices
\(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\pm}\) form an irreducible set, this would require
that \(\mathcal S^{-1}\mathcal S^*(\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}\) be proportional
to the unit matrix
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta=\alpha\mathcal S^{-1}\mathcal S^*\,,
  \tag{32.A.26}\label{eq:32.A.26}
\end{equation}
with \(\alpha\) some constant. For this to be possible, we must have
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta(\mathcal C\beta)^*
  =|\alpha|^2\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{1}\,.
  \tag{32.A.27}\label{eq:32.A.27}
\end{equation}
But \(\mathcal C\beta=\gamma_2\gamma_4\cdots\gamma_{2n-2}\), and since all
\(\gamma_i\) with \(i\) even are imaginary, we have
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C\beta(\mathcal C\beta)^*
  =(-1)^{n-1}\bigl(\gamma_2\gamma_4\cdots\gamma_{2n-2}\bigr)^2
  =(-1)^a\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{1}\,,
  \tag{32.A.28}\label{eq:32.A.28}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  a=(n-1)+(n-2)+\cdots+1=n(n-1)/2\,.
  \tag{32.A.29}\label{eq:32.A.29}
\end{equation}
Hence the Weyl representations can only be real for \(n=1\pmod 4\) and
must be pseudoreal for \(n=3\pmod 4\).

We also note for use in Section 32.1 that
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C^*=(-1)^n\mathcal C\,,
  \qquad
  \mathcal C^{\mathrm T}=(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\mathcal C\,,
  \qquad
  \mathcal C^{-1}=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.A.30}\label{eq:32.A.30}
\end{equation}
and therefore Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.13} gives
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal C\gamma_\mu)^{\mathrm T}
  =(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\mathcal C\gamma_\mu\,.
  \tag{32.A.31}\label{eq:32.A.31}
\end{equation}
The \(\gamma_\mu\) form a vector, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
  [\mathcal J_{\mu\nu},\gamma_\rho]
  =-i\gamma_\mu\eta_{\nu\rho}
   +i\gamma_\nu\eta_{\mu\rho}\,,
  \tag{32.A.32}\label{eq:32.A.32}
\end{equation}
and they have normal parity, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
  \beta\gamma_0\beta=+\gamma_0\,,
  \qquad
  \beta\gamma_i\beta=-\gamma_i
  \quad\text{for \(i=1,\ldots,2n-1\)}\,.
  \tag{32.A.33}\label{eq:32.A.33}
\end{equation}
The anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:32.A.1} prevents us from constructing
new tensors by taking symmetric products of \(\gamma\)s, but it allows us
to construct antisymmetric tensors of rank up to \(2n\)
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{[\mu_1}\gamma_{\mu_2}\cdots\gamma_{\mu_p]}\,,
  \tag{32.A.34}\label{eq:32.A.34}
\end{equation}
where the square brackets indicate antisymmetrization, and \(p\le 2n\).
Each has a number of independent spacetime components given by the binomial
coefficient \(\binom{2n}{p}\), so the total number of matrices of this type
is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{p=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{p}=2^{2n}\,.
  \tag{32.A.35}\label{eq:32.A.35}
\end{equation}
None of these vanishes (as can be seen by calculating their squares) and
they all have different Lorentz and/or parity transformation rules and are
therefore linearly independent, so any \(2^n\times2^n\) matrix may be written
as a linear combination of the \(2^{2n}\) antisymmetric tensors
\eqref{eq:32.A.34}.

\subsubsection*{Odd Dimensions: \(d=2n+1\)}

Now let us consider an odd spacetime dimensionality \(d=2n+1\). We can
easily find a set of \(2n+1\) Dirac \(n\times n\) matrices satisfying the
anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:32.A.1}: we simply use the same
\(\gamma_\mu\) with \(\mu=1,2,\ldots,2n-1,0\) as for \(d=2n\), and add
the matrix \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.17}. According
to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.18} and \eqref{eq:32.A.19} these gamma matrices
satisfy the anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:32.A.1}, with \(\mu\) and
\(\nu\) running over the values \(1,2,\ldots,2n-1,0,2n+1\), and again
\(\gamma_0=i\gamma_{2n}\).

Unlike the case of even dimensionality, we cannot here find any non-trivial
matrix that commutes with all the Lorentz generators, because
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.17} and \eqref{eq:32.A.18} show that the product of the
\(2n+1\) gamma matrices is trivial:
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_1\gamma_2\cdots\gamma_{2n}\gamma_{2n+1}
  =i^{-n}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf{1}\,.
  \tag{32.A.36}\label{eq:32.A.36}
\end{equation}
The Lorentz generators \eqref{eq:32.A.2} with \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) running
over the values \(1,2,\ldots,2n-1,0,2n+1\) therefore furnish an irreducible
representation of the Lorentz group by themselves. To test their reality
properties, we note that \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) is real and symmetric and
satisfies
\((\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}\gamma_{2n+1}\mathcal C\beta
=-(-1)^n\gamma_{2n+1}\), so Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.15} applies for
\(\mu=2n+1\) as well as for \(\mu=1,2,\ldots,2n-1,0\). The Lorentz
generators therefore satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^*
  =-(\mathcal C\beta)^{-1}\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\mathcal C\beta\,,
  \tag{32.A.37}\label{eq:32.A.37}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm T}
  =-\mathcal C^{-1}\mathcal J_{\mu\nu}\mathcal C\,,
  \tag{32.A.38}\label{eq:32.A.38}
\end{equation}
so in each odd dimension the fundamental spinor representation is either
real or pseudoreal. Exactly the same argument as in the case where \(d=2n\)
tells us that the spinor representations for \(d=2n+1\) are again real or
pseudoreal according to whether the sign \((-1)^a\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.28} is positive or negative, and therefore according to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.29} they are real for \(n=0\pmod 4\) and
\(n=1\pmod 4\) and pseudoreal for \(n=2\pmod 4\) and \(n=3\pmod 4\).

We can again construct antisymmetric tensors \eqref{eq:32.A.34}, now of rank
\(p\) up to \(2n+1\), but only half of these are independent, because they
obey relations
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_{2n+1}}
  \gamma_{[\mu_1}\gamma_{\mu_2}\cdots\gamma_{\mu_p]}
  \propto
  \gamma^{[\mu_{p+1}}\gamma^{\mu_2}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}]}\,,
  \tag{32.A.39}\label{eq:32.A.39}
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon^{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_{2n+1}}\) as usual totally
antisymmetric. (For \(d=2n\) no such relations are possible, because the
left- and right-hand sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.39} have opposite parity,
but this argument does not apply for \(d=2n+1\), where
\(\epsilon^{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_{2n+1}}\) has even spatial parity.) The
total number of independent matrices of the form \eqref{eq:32.A.34} is now
given by
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{p=0}^{n}\binom{2n+1}{p}=2^{2n}\,,
  \tag{32.A.40}\label{eq:32.A.40}
\end{equation}
so any \(2n\times2n\) matrix may be written as a linear combination of the
\(n+1\) independent antisymmetric tensors \eqref{eq:32.A.34} with
\(0\le p\le n+1\).

Finally, we note that for either \(d=2n\) or \(d=2n+1\), the \(O(d-1,1)\)
Dirac and Lorentz algebras are related to the corresponding \(O(d)\)
algebras by setting
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2n}\equiv-i\gamma_0\,,
  \qquad
  \mathcal J_{i\,2n}\equiv-i\mathcal J_{i0}\,,
  \tag{32.A.41}\label{eq:32.A.41}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
  \{\gamma_i,\gamma_j\}=2\delta_{ij}\,,
  \tag{32.A.42}\label{eq:32.A.42}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{ij}
  \equiv\frac{1}{4i}[\gamma_i,\gamma_j]
  =-\mathcal J_{ji}\,,
  \tag{32.A.43}\label{eq:32.A.43}
\end{equation}
with \(i\) and \(j\) running from 1 to \(d\). It follows from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.42} that for \(i\ne j\),
\(\mathcal J_{ij}^2=1/4\), so the eigenvalues of each \(\mathcal J_{ij}\)
are limited to \(\pm1/2\). To be more specific, in the fundamental spinor
representation the generators of the Cartan subalgebra are represented by
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{2u-1\,2u}
  =\frac{1}{2}[a_u,a_u^\dagger]
  =a_ua_u^\dagger-\frac{1}{2}\,,
  \tag{32.A.44}\label{eq:32.A.44}
\end{equation}
for which the basis vectors \eqref{eq:32.A.8} are eigenvectors, with
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_{2u-1\,2u}\ket{s_1s_2\cdots s_n}
  =\left(s_u-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ket{s_1s_2\cdots s_n}\,.
  \tag{32.A.45}\label{eq:32.A.45}
\end{equation}

The difference between dimensionalities \(d=2n\) and \(d=2n+1\) is that
for \(d=2n\) we have two fundamental spinor representations in which the
eigenvalue
\((-2\sigma_1)(-2\sigma_2)\cdots(-2\sigma_n)\) of \(\gamma_{2n+1}\) is
constrained to be \(+1\) or \(-1\), while for \(d=2n+1\) there is one
fundamental spinor representation with no such limitation on the
\(\sigma_u\).

It was the limitation of the eigenvalues of each \(\mathcal J_{ij}\) to
\(\pm1/2\) that in Section 32.1 identified the fundamental spinor
representations as the only possible representations of the Lorentz
algebra that could be furnished by fermionic symmetry generators. Indeed,
from this condition we could have inferred that the \(O(d)\) generators
may be represented in a basis of the form \eqref{eq:32.A.8}, with
\(s_u=\sigma_u+1/2\), and carried out the derivations of this appendix in
reverse order, using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:32.A.4}--\eqref{eq:32.A.7} (along
with Eq.~\eqref{eq:32.A.17} for \(d\) odd) to express the Lorentz
generators in terms of a set of \(\gamma_\mu\) satisfying the
anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:32.A.1}.
