\subsection{Space Inversion Properties of Supersymmetry Generators}

In theories that respect the conservation of parity, the result
\(P^{-1}\WeylQ_{ar}P\) of acting on the fermionic symmetry generator
\(\WeylQ_{ar}\) with the parity operator \(P\) must also be a fermionic
symmetry generator. Since \(J_i\) and \(K_i\) are respectively even and odd
under space inversion, Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.1} shows that the effect of acting
on \(A_i\) with the parity operator is
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}A_iP=B_i.
\tag{25.3.1}\label{eq:25.3.1}
\end{equation}
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.3}, the definition of \(\WeylQ_{ar}\) as a
\((0,1/2)\) operator means that
\begin{equation}
[B_i,\WeylQ_{ar}]
=-\frac12\sum_b(\sigma_i)_{ab}\WeylQ_{br},
\qquad
[A_i,\WeylQ_{ar}]=0.
\tag{25.3.2}\label{eq:25.3.2}
\end{equation}
Applying the parity operator yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
[A_i,P^{-1}\WeylQ_{ar}P]
&=-\frac12\sum_b(\sigma_i)_{ab}P^{-1}\WeylQ_{br}P,
&
[B_i,P^{-1}\WeylQ_{ar}P]&=0,
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.3.3}\label{eq:25.3.3}
\end{equation}
so \(P^{-1}\WeylQ_{ar}P\) is a \((1/2,0)\) symmetry generator, and therefore
must be a linear combination of the \(\WeylQ_{ar}^{\dagger}\). According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.6}, Lorentz invariance dictates the form of this relation
as
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\WeylQ_{ar}P
=\sum_{bs}\mathcal P_{rs}e_{ab}\WeylQ_{bs}^{\dagger},
\tag{25.3.4}\label{eq:25.3.4}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal P\) is a numerical matrix, and the matrix \(e\) is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.9}.

We can learn something about the properties of the matrix \(\mathcal P\) by
requiring that\linebreak
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.4} be consistent with the fundamental
anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:25.2.7}.\linebreak
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.4} and its adjoint yield
\[
P^{-1}\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}^{\dagger}\}P
=\sum_{cdtu}\mathcal P_{rt}e_{ac}\mathcal P_{su}^{*}e_{bd}
\{\WeylQ_{ct}^{\dagger},\WeylQ_{du}\}.
\]
Inserting Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.7}, this becomes
\[
\delta_{rs}\sigma^\mu_{ab}P^{-1}P_\mu P
=\sum_{cdtu}\mathcal P_{rt}e_{ac}\mathcal P_{su}^{*}e_{bd}
\delta_{tu}\sigma^\mu_{dc}P_\mu.
\]
But \(e\sigma_i^Te^{-1}=-\sigma_i\) and
\(e\sigma_0^Te^{-1}=+\sigma_0\), while \(P^{-1}P_iP=-P_i\) and
\(P^{-1}P_0P=P_0\), so this reduces to the statement that \(\mathcal P\) is
unitary
\begin{equation}
\mathcal P\mathcal P^\dagger=\mathbf{1}.
\tag{25.3.5}\label{eq:25.3.5}
\end{equation}

The matrix \(\mathcal P\) is to some extent arbitrary, because for any set of
fermionic generators \(\WeylQ_{ar}\) that satisfies Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.3.2}
and \eqref{eq:25.2.7}, we construct another set \(\WeylQ'_{ar}\) that also
satisfies Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.3.2} and \eqref{eq:25.2.7} by a unitary
transformation
\begin{equation}
\WeylQ'_{ar}=\sum_s\mathcal U_{rs}\WeylQ_{as},
\qquad
\mathcal U^\dagger=\mathcal U^{-1},
\tag{25.3.6}\label{eq:25.3.6}
\end{equation}
so that the parity transformation rule \eqref{eq:25.3.4} becomes
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\WeylQ'_{ar}P
=\sum_{bs}\mathcal P'_{rs}e_{ab}\WeylQ_{bs}^{\prime\dagger},
\tag{25.3.7}\label{eq:25.3.7}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathcal P'=\mathcal U\mathcal P\mathcal U^{-1*}
=\mathcal U\mathcal P\mathcal U^T.
\tag{25.3.8}\label{eq:25.3.8}
\end{equation}

For simple supersymmetry \(\mathcal P\) is just a \(1\cdot1\) phase factor,
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.4} reads
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\WeylQ_aP
=\mathcal P\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_b^\dagger.
\tag{25.3.9}\label{eq:25.3.9}
\end{equation}
Combining this with its adjoint gives
\begin{equation}
P^{-2}\WeylQ_aP^2=-\WeylQ_a,
\tag{25.3.10}\label{eq:25.3.10}
\end{equation}
independently of the value chosen for the phase factor \(\mathcal P\). This
has the striking consequence that if a boson in a supermultiplet of
particles has a \emph{real} intrinsic parity, then the fermions obtained by
acting on this boson state with \(\WeylQ_a\) have \emph{imaginary} intrinsic
parities.

Because for simple supersymmetry \(\mathcal U\) and \(\mathcal P\) are just
phase factors, it is obvious from Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.8} that by a suitable
choice of \(\mathcal U\), the phase factor \(\mathcal P'\) can be made
anything we like. It will be convenient to choose \(\mathcal P'=+i\), so
that Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.7} takes the simple form (now dropping primes)
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\WeylQ_aP
=i\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_b^\dagger.
\tag{25.3.11}\label{eq:25.3.11}
\end{equation}
In the dotted and undotted notation introduced in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.34}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.11} and its adjoint read
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}P=iQ_\alpha,
\qquad
P^{-1}Q_\alpha P=i\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}.
\tag{25.3.12}\label{eq:25.3.12}
\end{equation}
The index placement is the one in the comparison column
\((Q_\alpha,\bar Q^{\dot\alpha})^{\mathrm T}\), for which the same
statement is
\[
i\beta
=i\begin{pmatrix}
0&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]
with the displayed block matrix used only to verify the two-component
transformation law.

For extended supersymmetry it is not always possible to choose \(\mathcal U\)
so that \(\mathcal P'\) is diagonal. However, a theorem in matrix algebra
(proved in Appendix C of Chapter 2) shows that it is possible to choose
\(\mathcal U\) so that \(\mathcal P'\) is block diagonal, in general with
some of the matrices on the diagonal equal to \(1\cdot1\) submatrices that
can be chosen equal to \(i\) (or any other phase factors we like), and other
submatrices on the diagonal given by \(2\cdot2\) matrices that can be chosen
to have the form
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\exp(i\phi)\\
\exp(-i\phi)&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]
with various phases \(\phi\). Correspondingly, with this choice of
\(\mathcal U\) (and now dropping primes) the two-component \(\WeylQ\)s are of
two types. The \(\WeylQ\)s of the first type also satisfy
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.3.11}:
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\WeylQ_{ar}P
=i\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_{br}^\dagger.
\tag{25.3.13}\label{eq:25.3.13}
\end{equation}
The two-component \(\WeylQ\)s of the second type come in pairs, which we will
call \(\WeylQ_{as1}\) and \(\WeylQ_{as2}\), with the \(s\)th pair having the
parity transformation rule
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P^{-1}\WeylQ_{as1}P
&=e^{i\phi_s}\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_{bs2}^{\dagger},
&
P^{-1}\WeylQ_{as2}P
&=e^{-i\phi_s}\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_{bs1}^{\dagger}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.3.14}\label{eq:25.3.14}
\end{equation}
In particular, we now have
\begin{equation}
P^{-2}\WeylQ_{as1}P^2=-e^{2i\phi_s}\WeylQ_{as1},
\qquad
P^{-2}\WeylQ_{as2}P^2=-e^{-2i\phi_s}\WeylQ_{as2}.
\tag{25.3.15}\label{eq:25.3.15}
\end{equation}
This shows that, unless \(\phi_s=0\pmod{\pi}\), it is not possible to form
supersymmetry generators of the first type out of linear combinations of
extended supersymmetry generators of the second type.

In the dotted and undotted notation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.34}, the effect
of the parity operator on extended supersymmetry generators of the first
type is
\begin{equation}
P^{-1}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}_rP=iQ_{\alpha r},
\qquad
P^{-1}Q_{\alpha r}P=i\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}_r,
\tag{25.3.16}\label{eq:25.3.16}
\end{equation}
while for generators of the second type
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P^{-1}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}_{s1}P
&=e^{i\phi_s}Q_{\alpha,s2},
&
P^{-1}Q_{\alpha,s1}P
&=-e^{-i\phi_s}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}_{s2},
\\
P^{-1}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}_{s2}P
&=e^{-i\phi_s}Q_{\alpha,s1},
&
P^{-1}Q_{\alpha,s2}P
&=-e^{i\phi_s}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}_{s1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.3.17}\label{eq:25.3.17}
\end{equation}
