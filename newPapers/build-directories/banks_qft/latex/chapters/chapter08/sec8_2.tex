\subsection{BRST symmetry}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=107 print=97 kind=prose
It would be straightforward to derive the Feynman rules for the general
gauge-invariant Lagrangian, apart from one disturbing fact. The quadratic terms
in the gauge fields look like $\dim G$ copies of Maxwell's Lagrangian, and
cannot be inverted to give a propagator. We have already encountered this
problem in our discussion of QED, and finessed it by introducing a small photon
mass. We promised a more extensive discussion of the massless limit. The time
has come to fulfill that promise. The nature of this book prevents us from
exploring the full extent of this subject. We will content ourselves with
introducing what I will call the Becchi--Rouet--Stora--Tyutin (BRST) trick
\cite{banks-ref-46,banks-ref-47}. The reader interested in the geometrical foundations of this trick can
consult the second volume of Weinberg's monograph \cite{banks-ref-42}. More detailed
references may be found in \cite{banks-ref-48,banks-ref-49,banks-ref-50}. It would be helpful at this point to do
Problems 8.1--8.3. These will show you how BRST symmetry works for free-field
theories.

\BanksImplicitHook{I-CH08-003}

The basic idea of the BRST trick is simple: introduce the gauge parameters in
an enlarged field theory, where the gauge symmetry is an ordinary global
symmetry. The tricky part is that, to make it all work, the scalar gauge
parameters must be quantized as fermions. Explicitly, we introduce two scalar
fermion fields $\bar c$ and $c$, and a boson field $N$, all transforming in the
adjoint representation of the gauge symmetry. $c$ is called the \emph{ghost}
field, $\bar c$ the \emph{anti-ghost}, and $N$ the Nakanishi--Lautrup Lagrange
multiplier. The BRST symmetry acts as an infinitesimal gauge transformation,
with gauge parameter $\epsilon c$ on all of the ordinary fields in the theory
($\epsilon$ is a constant Grassmann parameter). In addition,
\begin{align*}
  \delta_{\mathrm{BRST}}c^a&=\epsilon f^a{}_{bc}c^bc^c,\\
  \delta_{\mathrm{BRST}}\bar c^a&=\epsilon N^a,\\
  \delta_{\mathrm{BRST}}N^a&=0.
\end{align*}
Note that $\delta_{\mathrm{BRST}}^2$ applied to any of these fields vanishes,
as a consequence of the Jacobi identity for the structure constants and the
Fermi statistics of the $c$ fields. It is easy to verify that the same is true
for the action of $\delta_{\mathrm{BRST}}$ on ordinary fields. The operator
$Q_{\mathrm{BRST}}$ which implements this symmetry on the quantum Hilbert space
should be nilpotent, $Q_{\mathrm{BRST}}^2=0$.

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=108 print=98 kind=prose
We also implement a $U(1)$ symmetry of these equations under which $N^a$ and
all ordinary fields have charge zero, while $c^a$ and $\bar c^a$ have opposite
charge (normalized to $\pm1$). The BRST charge $Q_{\mathrm{BRST}}$ has charge
1. This $U(1)$ quantum number is called the \emph{ghost number}.

The nilpotency of the BRST operator makes it very easy to add invariant ghost
field terms to the Lagrangian. Simply choose a term that has the form
$\delta\mathcal L=[Q_{\mathrm{BRST}},\Psi]_+$, where $\Psi$ is a fermionic
operator of ghost number $-1$, called the \emph{gauge fermion}. For most
purposes, the most convenient choice is
$\Psi=\bar c^a(F^a+\frac12\kappa N^a)$, where $F^a$ is a function of ordinary
fields. The resulting \emph{gauge-fixing Lagrangian} has the form
\begin{equation*}
  \delta\mathcal L=\int\dd^D y\,\bar c^a(x)
  \frac{\delta F^a(x)}{\delta\omega^b(y)}c^b(y)
  +\frac12\kappa(N^a)^2+N^aF^a.
\end{equation*}
We can integrate out the Lagrange multiplier, and convert this to
\begin{equation*}
  \delta\mathcal L=\int\dd^D y\,\bar c^a(x)
  \frac{\delta F^a(x)}{\delta\omega^b(y)}c^b(y)
  -\frac{1}{2\kappa}(F^a)^2.
\end{equation*}
$F^a$ should be chosen so that the ghost kinetic term is non-degenerate, if we
wish to carry out a perturbation expansion. The choice
$F^a=\partial_\mu A^{\mu a}$ is the most common one, because it is simple and
covariant. In models in which the Higgs phenomenon occurs, a slightly more
general form, involving scalar fields, is preferable.

Like any other global symmetry, the BRST symmetry will give rise to Ward
identities in the quantum theory. They have the form
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_j\left\langle O_1(x_1)\cdots[Q_{\mathrm{BRST}},O_j(x_j)]_{\pm}
  (-1)^{\sigma_j}\cdots O_n(x_n)\right\rangle=0.
\end{equation*}
The $O_i$ are any operators in the theory. The peculiar features of this Ward
identity come from the fact that the symmetry generator is fermionic. This
accounts for the commutator/anti-commutator ambiguity, depending on the fermion
parity of $O_j$, as well as the factor $(-1)^{\sigma_j}$ which reflects the
Leibniz rule for Grassmann numbers. The most important consequence of this
identity comes from the case in which $O_1$ is itself a BRST commutator (or
anti-commutator) and the rest of the $O_k$ are BRST-invariant. The Ward identity
then implies that
\begin{equation*}
  \langle O_1(x_1)\cdots O_n(x_n)\rangle=0.
\end{equation*}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=109 print=99 kind=prose
In particular, since the difference between any two choices of gauge-fixing
Lagrangian is a BRST anti-commutator, this identity shows that the expectation
values of all BRST-invariant operators are independent of the choice of
gauge-fixing Lagrangian.

There are two obvious classes of BRST-invariant operators. The first consists
of gauge-invariant functionals of the ordinary variables. The second, called
BRST trivial, are operators of the form $[Q_{\mathrm{BRST}},A]_\pm$. It is easy
to see that the two classes are disjoint. It is somewhat harder to prove
\cite{banks-ref-48,banks-ref-49,banks-ref-50} that there are no other ways of being BRST-invariant. Thus, the
non-trivial BRST-invariant operators are precisely the gauge-invariant
observables of the classical theory. The Ward identity shows us that their
expectation values will be identical for all choices of gauge-fixing Lagrangian.

\BanksImplicitHook{I-CH08-004}

This is the key result that enables us to resolve the irritating problem that,
in the Higgs phase of non-abelian gauge theories, there is no choice of gauge
that is simultaneously covariant, unitary, and renormalizable. BRST invariance
guarantees the unitarity of gauge-invariant Green functions computed in a
covariant gauge, because it implies that they are equal to Green functions of
the same operators computed in a non-covariant, but unitary, gauge (like the
axial gauge $A_3{}^a=0$).

The covariant gauge Feynman rules for non-abelian gauge theory may be found in
Appendix D. The problem sets will give the reader ample opportunity to practice
the use of these rules. In the text we will do some perturbative calculations
when we get to the discussion of the renormalization group for non-abelian
gauge theory. Before entering into the intricacies of perturbative calculations,
however, I want to give the reader a feeling for the physics context in which
non-abelian gauge theory is important and the qualitative nature of the physical
phenomena which arise from this theory.

A historical note: the ghost fields first appeared in the work of Feynman,
DeWitt, and Mandelstam \cite{banks-ref-51,banks-ref-52,banks-ref-53} and were explained in terms of a change of
variables in the functional integral by Faddeev and Popov \cite{banks-ref-54}. They are often
called Faddeev--Popov ghosts. The BRST symmetry is the most elegant way to
formulate the introduction of ghosts, and can easily be generalized to include
gauge equivalences that do not form a group.
