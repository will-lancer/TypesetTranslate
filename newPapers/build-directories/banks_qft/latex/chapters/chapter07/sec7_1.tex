\subsection{Space-time symmetries}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=88 print=78 kind=prose
The treatment of space-time symmetries is roughly similar to that of internal
symmetries, but the differences are illuminating. Let's start from space-time
translations. The first obvious difference is that the Lagrangian density is no
longer invariant. Under translations,
\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal L(x)\longrightarrow\mathcal L(x+a),
\end{equation*}
or, infinitesimally,
\begin{equation*}
  \delta\mathcal L=a^\nu\partial_\nu\mathcal L.
\end{equation*}

As a consequence, when we (using the field vector notation) make an
infinitesimal, space-time-dependent translation $x^\mu\to x^\mu+f^\mu(x)$,
we use the passive field variation
$\delta\phi_i=-f^\nu\partial_\nu\phi_i$ and find
\begin{equation*}
  \delta S=\int\dd^D x\left[
  -\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_i}f^\nu\partial_\nu\phi_i(x)
  -\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_i)}
  \partial_\mu\bigl(f^\nu\partial_\nu\phi_i(x)\bigr)\right].
\end{equation*}
Thus,
\begin{align*}
  \delta S
  &=\int\dd^D x\,\partial_\mu f_\nu
  \left[-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_i)}
  \partial^\nu\phi_i(x)+\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L\right]\\
  &=\int\dd^D x\,\partial_\mu f_\nu T^{\mu\nu}(x).
\end{align*}

Thus, in classical mechanics, when the equations of motion are satisfied, the
stress-energy tensor or energy-momentum tensor $T^{\mu\nu}(x)$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0,
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation*}
  P^\nu=\int\dd^{D-1}\mathbf x\,T^{0\nu}
\end{equation*}
is time-independent. $P^\nu$ is the energy momentum, also identified in the
quantum theory as the operator that generates infinitesimal translations in
space-time on the Hilbert space of states. The quantum analog of stress-energy
conservation is the Ward identity
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\partial^x_\mu\langle0|T(T^{\mu\nu}(x)\phi_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots
\phi_{i_n}(x_n))|0\rangle\\
={}&+\ii\sum_k\delta^D(x-x_k)\langle0|T(\phi_{i_1}(x_1)\cdots\\
&\qquad\partial^\nu\phi_{i_k}(x_k)\cdots\phi_{i_n}(x_n))|0\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

As before, this is derived by implementing a change of variables in the
path integral, replacing the fields by their values after a space-time-dependent
translation, and then noting that the change of variables does not change the
value of the functional integral.

The fact that an infinitesimal space-time-dependent translation is actually a
general infinitesimal coordinate transformation suggests that the conserved
currents associated with Lorentz, or other, space-time symmetries will also be
associated with the stress tensor. In fact, this is true, but only after we take
into account the fact that Noether's definition of the stress tensor is somewhat
ambiguous. In fact, it is easy to verify (see the problems) that the change
\begin{equation*}
  T^{\mu\nu}\longrightarrow T^{\mu\nu}+\partial_\lambda M^{\mu\nu\lambda},
\end{equation*}
where $M^{\mu\nu\lambda}=-M^{\lambda\nu\mu}$, changes neither the fact that
the stress tensor is conserved nor the value of the translation generator,
$P^\nu$.

The easiest way to resolve this ambiguity is to consider the quantum field
theory we are studying on a general space-time geometry, rather than sticking
to flat Minkowski space. This is of course a good thing to do, because
Einstein's theory of gravitation tells us that gravitation is nothing but curved
space-time geometry, with the geometry determined by the matter in it. (To
quote J.~A.~Wheeler: ``Space-time tells matter how to move, and matter tells
space-time how to curve.'') The basic idea of curved space-time geometry is
that the Minkowski metric $\eta_{\mu\nu}$, which determines infinitesimal
space-time intervals between points, is replaced by a general symmetric tensor
$g_{\mu\nu}(x)$, which is an invertible matrix with signature
$(-,+,\ldots,+)$. It is easy to generalize the action for scalar and Maxwell fields
simply by replacing $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ by $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ and the volume element
by
\begin{equation*}
  \dd^D x\longrightarrow\dd^D x\sqrt{-g},
\end{equation*}
where $g$ is the determinant of the metric. Spinor fields require a more
involved discussion, which we will take up after we have understood non-abelian
gauge theory and vector bundles.

The scalar Lagrangian is
\begin{equation*}
  -\frac12\sqrt{-g}\left(g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\phi\partial_\nu\phi+2V(\phi)\right),
\end{equation*}
while the Maxwell Lagrangian is
\begin{equation*}
  -\frac14\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\lambda}g^{\nu\kappa}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\lambda\kappa}.
\end{equation*}
It is easy to see that, if we make a general coordinate transformation on the
fields, as well as the transformation
\begin{equation*}
  g_{\mu\nu}(x)\longrightarrow g_{\lambda\kappa}(y(x))
  \frac{\partial x^\lambda}{\partial y^\mu}
  \frac{\partial x^\kappa}{\partial y^\nu},
\end{equation*}
then the action is invariant.

As a consequence
\begin{equation*}
  \int\dd^D x\sqrt{-g}\,\delta_f g_{\mu\nu}(x)T^{\mu\nu}(x)=0,
\end{equation*}
whenever the fields $\phi_i(x)$ satisfy the classical equations of motion.\footnote[3]{In
the quantum theory, using by now familiar manipulations, this translates into
a Ward--Takahashi (W--T) identity in which the insertion of the indicated
variation into a Green function gives us the variation of the Green function
under the transformation.} Here
\begin{equation*}
  T^{\mu\nu}(x)\equiv\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}(x)},
\end{equation*}
and $\delta_f g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ is the variation of the metric under the
infinitesimal coordinate transformation $x^\mu\to x^\mu+f^\mu(x)$. In the
approximation where we neglect gravitational back reaction of the quantum fields
on the metric, we are of course interested only in fixed background metrics.
The W--T identities are only interesting when the coordinate transformations
correspond to isometries of the metric, i.e. transformations that leave the form
of the metric invariant. For Minkowski space this means Poincare transformations.
The conformal-group statements in the following paragraph are specialized to
$D=4$.
Another interesting possibility is conformal isometries, where
\begin{equation*}
  \delta_f g_{\mu\nu}(x)=s(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x).
\end{equation*}
These are of interest if the quantum field theory is invariant under Weyl
transformations of the metric, $g_{\mu\nu}(x)\to\Omega^2(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x)$.
The conformal isometry group of Minkowski space is isomorphic to $SO(2,4)$.
In addition to Poincare transformations, it contains scale transformations
$x^\mu\to\lambda x^\mu$, for positive $\lambda$, and special conformal
transformations, whose infinitesimal form is
$\delta x^\mu=b^\mu x^2-2b^\nu x^\mu x_\nu$. Conformal invariance implies
that $T^\mu{}_{\mu}$, the trace of the stress tensor, vanishes. It is also
obvious that conformal transformations cannot be symmetries of a theory with a
discrete mass spectrum, unless all the particle masses are zero.

The stress tensor derived by variation with respect to the metric is symmetric.
It is also conserved, as a consequence of translation invariance. This symmetric
tensor (the Belinfante tensor) is equal to the Noether current only for
translations for scalar fields. In general it differs from the Noether current
by a divergence of an anti-symmetric tensor, the Noether ambiguity. Symmetry of
the Belinfante tensor also implies that $\partial_\mu J^{\mu\nu\lambda}=0$,
where
\begin{equation*}
  J^{\mu\nu\lambda}\equiv x^\nu T^{\mu\lambda}-x^\lambda T^{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation*}
The corresponding conserved charges
\begin{equation*}
  J^{\nu\lambda}=\int\dd^{D-1}\mathbf x\,J^{0\nu\lambda}
\end{equation*}
are the boost and angular-momentum generators. Note that the boost generators
contain explicit time dependence, so that the conservation law does not imply
that they commute with the Hamiltonian. Indeed, it should not because the
commutation rules of the Poincare algebra say that the commutator of a boost
generator with the Hamiltonian is a spatial momentum component.
