\subsection{Perturbation theory}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=34 print=24 kind=prose
The Euclidean formulation of field theory\footnote[7]{The idea of Euclidean field theory is due to Schwinger, and the rotation of contours in momentum space is called the Wick rotation.} leads to mathematical expressions whose properties are simpler to understand.
Euclidean methods also turn out to be crucial for understanding field theory at finite
temperature and quantum tunneling amplitudes. They are also central to numerical
techniques (lattice field theory) for obtaining non-perturbative solutions to field theory.
So we will generally take the attitude that a field theory is defined by analytic continuation
of a Euclidean functional integral. We simply write the formal expression for the Euclidean
action and integrate $\ee^{-S_E}\phi(x_1)\ldots\phi(x_n)$ over fields in Euclidean space, and then
analytically continue the result to get time-ordered products of Lorentzian fields.

In this way, we obtain the definition of the generating functional of Euclidean Green
functions, also known as Schwinger functions:

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=34 print=24 kind=equation id=3.24
\begin{equation}
Z_E[J]=\frac{\int[d\phi]\ee^{-S_E[\phi]+\ii\int\dd^D x\,\phi(x)J(x)}}{\int[d\phi]\ee^{-S_E[\phi]}},
\label{eq:3.24}
\end{equation}

which has the form of the characteristic function of a probability distribution. Note
that in writing this formula we have also analytically continued the source function $J$
to $\ii J$. If we refrain from doing this we obtain the Boltzmann formula for the partition
function of a classical statistical system in an external field $J$. The ``potential'' of the
statistical system is the Euclidean action,

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=34 print=24 kind=equation id=3.25
\begin{equation}
S_E\equiv\int\dd^D x_E\left[\frac12(\partial_E\phi)^2+V(\phi)\right],
\label{eq:3.25}
\end{equation}

where we now integrate over $D$-dimensional Euclidean space, rather than Minkowski
space-time. In the standard four-dimensional application, $D=4$. This profound analogy between QFT and classical statistical mechanics has
led to an enormously successful cross fertilization between the two fields.

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=34 print=24 kind=prose
In problems amenable to perturbation theory, the non-quadratic part of the potential
is multiplied by a small dimensionless parameter $g$. The classical action has the form
$S=S_0-g\int\dd^D x\,V(\phi(x))$, where $S_0$ is the action of a free massive field. Therefore, we can
write a power-series expansion for $Z[J]\equiv I[J]/I[0]$:

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=34 print=24 kind=equation id=3.26
\begin{equation}
I[J]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\ii g)^n}{n!}\int[d\phi]\ee^{\ii S_0+\ii\int\dd^D x\,\phi J}\prod_{r=1}^n\left[\int\dd^D x_r\,V(x_r)\right].
\label{eq:3.26}
\end{equation}

If $V$ has a power-series expansion in $\phi$, then every integral that is required in order
to evaluate this formula has the form

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=34 print=24 kind=display
\[
\int[d\phi]\ee^{\ii S_0}\phi(y_1)\ldots\phi(y_m).
\]

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=35 print=25 kind=prose
The $\phi$s in this formula, which come from the same $V(x_i)$, will all have the same value
of $y=x_i$, and we will integrate over the $x_i$ variables. When evaluating $Z[J]$, we expand
both numerator and denominator. Then it is easy to see that every such integral in
the expansion will be accompanied by the denominator $I_0$, the value of the functional
integral at $J=g=0$. The generating functional for these normalized functional
integrals is just $Z_0[J]=\langle0|T\ee^{\ii\int\phi J}|0\rangle$, the free-field vacuum persistence amplitude.
\BanksImplicitHook{I-CH03-008}

In free-field theory, we can either solve the (linear) SD equation directly or do the
Gaussian functional integral to obtain

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=35 print=25 kind=equation id=3.27
\begin{equation}
Z_0[J]=\ee^{-\frac12\int\dd^D x\,\dd^D y\,J(x)J(y)D_F(x-y)},
\label{eq:3.27}
\end{equation}

where $D_F(x)$ is the Feynman Green function or vacuum expectation value of the time-
ordered product of two free fields. It follows, by expanding out both the definition and
the solution for $Z_0[J]$, that

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=35 print=25 kind=equation id=3.28
\begin{equation}
\frac{\int[d\phi]\ee^{\ii S_0}\phi(y_1)\ldots\phi(y_m)}{\int[d\phi]\ee^{\ii S_0}}
=\sum_{\text{pairs}}\prod D_F(y_i-y_j).
\label{eq:3.28}
\end{equation}

This formula is called Wick's theorem. It leads to the following set of rules for
perturbation theory, called Feynman rules.

\begin{itemize}
\item Write the interaction potential as a sum of monomials $V=\sum(v_k/k!)\phi^k$; its Lorentzian action contribution is $-g\int\dd^D x\,V$.
\item A term in $n$th-order perturbation theory in $g$ will have contributions proportional
to $v_{k_1}\ldots v_{k_n}$. We associate this with a diagram with $n$ vertices. The $i$th vertex has $k_i$
lines coming out of it. These are called Feynman diagrams or Feynman graphs.
\item Each line must be connected either to a line from another vertex or to an external
source point. For a contribution to an $E$-point Green function, there will be $E$ such
external lines. Lines connecting two vertices are called internal lines. The number of
internal lines $I$ is given by $2I=\sum k_i-E$.
\item Write a factor $D_F(y_i-y_j)$ for an internal line connecting two space-time points.
\item Write a factor $D_F(y_i-x_a)J(x_a)$ for an external line.
\item Integrate over all internal and external points. If one wants to directly compute
individual Green functions, rather than the generating functional, omit the factors
of $J(x_a)$ and the integral over the external points.
\item We can easily generalize to interaction vertices like $\phi^2(\partial_\mu\phi)^2$, just letting the deriva-
tives act on the appropriate argument of $D_F$. Similarly, it is easy to generalize these
rules to theories of multiple scalar fields.
\item A given Feynman diagram in $n$th order comes with a factor $(-\ii g)^n v_{k_1}\ldots v_{k_n}S_G$, where
the combinatorial factor $S_G$ is a combination of the inverse factorials from the
definition of $v_k$ and the \emph{number of times a given graph can be obtained in the sum over
all possible pairings (also called all possible contractions)}. $S_G$ is called the symmetry
number of the graph, because it can be shown that it is equal to the inverse of the
order of the group of geometrical symmetries of the graph. I have always found that
the art of figuring out the geometrical symmetries is harder than reproducing $S_G$ by
counting the number of contractions which give the graph. It's a matter of taste. An
algorithm for doing the counting can be found in \cite{banks-ref-13}.
\end{itemize}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=36 print=26 kind=prose
\begin{itemize}
\item By Fourier transforming everything in sight, one can derive an equivalent set of rules
in momentum space. These are somewhat easier to write down, because the Fourier
transform $-\ii/(p^2+m^2-\ii\varepsilon)$ of $D_F$ is so simple. The rules involve integrating over
the momenta of closed loops in the diagram, with measure $\dd^D p/(2\pi)^D$ (equivalently,
integrating over all internal line momenta, with $(2\pi)^D\delta^D(\sum q_i)$ enforcing momentum
conservation at each vertex; one momentum delta function of the sum over external
momenta is left). Derivatives in the interaction turn into factors of momentum, but
one must take care to make sure that one has the right sum of momenta in these
factors.
\item In Euclidean space, the rules are even easier. We replace the factor of $-\ii$ in $(-\ii g)^n$ by a
minus sign, and drop the $\ii$ from each propagator. The Euclidean propagator is just
$1/(p_E^2+m^2)$.
\end{itemize}

It's extremely important for the reader to work out the derivation of these rules,
following the outline we have supplied above.
The Feynman rules for a large class of field theories, along with standard dia-
grammatic conventions, are collected in Appendix D. There one can also find several
examples of working out the combinatoric factors for individual graphs. This is proba-
bly a good point in the exposition for students to stop and do a few exercises to convince
themselves that they know how to set up the computation of Feynman graphs. If you
go too far in this exercise you'll encounter the disturbing fact that most of the loop
graphs are infinite. We won't learn how to tackle this problem, and what it means, until
Chapter 9.
