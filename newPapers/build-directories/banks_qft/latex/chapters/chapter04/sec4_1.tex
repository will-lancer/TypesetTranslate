\BanksChapter{4}{Particles of spin 1, and gauge invariance}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=48 print=38 kind=section id=4.1
\subsection{Massive spinning particles}

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=48 print=38 kind=prose
We now want to extend our discussion of particles and fields to particles with spin. The
reader has been asked to do this on her/his own in Problem 2.8. This section can be
viewed as an extended answer to parts of that exercise. The first step is to understand
the Lorentz transformation properties of single-particle states. We start with massive
particles in their rest frame. In the four-dimensional specialization used for the spin
labels below, the stability subgroup of the rest-frame momentum is $\mathrm{SU}(2)$,
so single-particle states are classified as irreducible representations of this group,
which is the same as the usual non-relativistic definition of spin. A particle of spin $j$
has $(2j+1)$ states, $\ket{0,m}$ identified with eigenvalues $m$ between $[-j,j]$, of the operator
$J_3$ representing infinitesimal rotations around the 3-axis. In general $D$,
the massive little group is $\mathrm{SO}(D-1)$.

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=48 print=38 kind=display id=4.1a
To generalize our discussion of spinless particles, we define the states of a massive
spinning particle, with momentum $p$ and spin component $m$, as
\begin{equation*}
  \ket{p,m}\equiv \sqrt{\frac{m}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}\,U(L(p))\ket{0,m}.
\end{equation*}
In this four-dimensional construction, $L(p)$ is defined as a boost along the 3-direction, with velocity
$|\mathbf p|/\omega_{\mathbf p}$, followed by
a clockwise rotation of the 3-direction into the direction of $\mathbf p$. It transforms the rest
frame to the frame where the particle has momentum $p$. Then, using the fact that $U(\Lambda)$
is supposed to be a representation of the Lorentz group, we have
\begin{equation*}
  U(\Lambda)\ket{p,m}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}\,
  U(L(\Lambda p))U\bigl(L^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda L(p)\bigr)\ket{0,m}.
\end{equation*}
The transformation
$R_W(\Lambda,p)\equiv L^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda L(p)$, called the Wigner rotation, takes the
rest frame into itself, and hence belongs to $\mathrm{SU}(2)$. We therefore know how it acts on
the rest-frame states. Thus

% BANKS-SOURCE: pdf=48 print=38 kind=equation id=4.1
\begin{equation}
  U(\Lambda)\ket{p,m}
  =\sqrt{\frac{m}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}\,U(L(\Lambda p))D^j_{mk}(R_W)\ket{0,k}
  =\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}}}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}\,
  D^j_{mk}(R_W)\ket{\Lambda p,k}.
  \label{eq:4.1}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the usual $(2j+1)$-dimensional representation of the Wigner rotation. It’s
easy to verify that this defines a unitary representation of the Poincaré group on the
space of states of a massive spinning particle. The creation and annihilation operators
for these particles will thus carry a label $a_m(\mathbf p)$, transforming in the spin-$j$ representation
of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$.
