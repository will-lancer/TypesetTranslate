\BanksSolution{4.1}
Let $p^\mu=(\omega_{\mathbf p},\mathbf p)$, with
$\omega_{\mathbf p}=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+\mu^2}$ and $p^2=-\mu^2$, and use the standard
boost from $(\mu,\mathbf0)$ to $p$.  Applying this boost to the rest-frame basis
$\epsilon_{(a)}{}^\mu(0)=(0,\delta_a^j)$ gives
\begin{equation*}
 \epsilon_{(a)}{}^0(p)=\frac{p_a}{\mu},
 \qquad
 \epsilon_{(a)}{}^j(p)=\delta_a^j+
 \frac{p^j p_a}{\mu(\omega_{\mathbf p}+\mu)},
 \qquad a=1,\ldots,D-1.
\end{equation*}
For the problem's $D=4$ specialization, $a=1,2,3$.  Lowering the Lorentz index
leaves the spatial components unchanged in the mostly-plus metric.  The
vectors obey
\begin{equation*}
 p_\mu\epsilon_{(a)}{}^\mu(p)=0,
 \qquad
 \epsilon_{(a)}{}^\mu(p)\epsilon_{(b)\mu}(p)=\delta_{ab},
 \qquad
 \sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\epsilon_{(a)}{}^\mu(p)\epsilon_{(a)}{}^\nu(p)
 =\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{\mu^2}.
\end{equation*}
The first identity follows by using
$\mathbf p^2=(\omega_{\mathbf p}-\mu)(\omega_{\mathbf p}+\mu)$;
the other two follow because the vectors are obtained from an orthonormal
rest-frame basis by a Lorentz transformation.

Write the field with lower Lorentz index as
\begin{equation*}
 B_\mu(x)=\int\frac{\dd^{D-1}\mathbf p}
 {\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^{D-1}}}
 \sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\left[\epsilon_\mu{}^{(a)}(p)a_a(\mathbf p)\ee^{+\ii p\cdot x}
 +\epsilon_\mu{}^{(a)}(p)^*a_a^\dagger(\mathbf p)\ee^{-\ii p\cdot x}\right].
\end{equation*}
The creation-operator law and its adjoint are
\begin{align*}
 U(\Lambda)a_a^\dagger(\mathbf p)U^{-1}(\Lambda)
 &=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}}}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}
 R_a{}^b(W(\Lambda,p))a_b^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}),\\
 U(\Lambda)a_a(\mathbf p)U^{-1}(\Lambda)
 &=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}}}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}
 R_a{}^b(W(\Lambda,p))^*a_b(\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}).
\end{align*}
The defining relation $\Lambda L(p)=L(\Lambda p)W(\Lambda,p)$, applied to the
rest-frame polarization vectors, gives the intertwining identity
\begin{equation*}
 \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\epsilon_\nu{}^{(a)}(p)
 =\epsilon_\mu{}^{(b)}(\Lambda p)R_b{}^a(W(\Lambda,p)).
\end{equation*}
Substitute these two identities into the mode expansion and change variables
$q=\Lambda p$.  The square-root factors cancel the Jacobian of the invariant
mass-shell measure, yielding
\begin{equation*}
 U(\Lambda)B_\mu(x)U^{-1}(\Lambda)
 =(\Lambda^{-1})_\mu{}^\nu B_\nu(\Lambda x).
\end{equation*}
Thus the $D-1$ physical modes can be assembled into a Lorentz $D$-vector,
with the relation $\partial_\mu B^\mu=0$ supplying the covariant constraint.

\BanksSolution{4.2}
Use the metric $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(-1,+1,\ldots,+1)$ and
$B_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\nu B_\mu-\partial_\mu B_\nu$.  The Proca Lagrangian is
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal L_P=-\frac14B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}-\frac12\mu^2B_\mu B^\mu.
\end{equation*}
Its Euler--Lagrange equation is
\begin{equation*}
 \partial_\nu B^{\mu\nu}-\mu^2B^\mu=0.
\end{equation*}
The canonical momenta are
\begin{equation*}
 \Pi^0=\frac{\partial\mathcal L_P}{\partial\dot B_0}=0,
 \qquad
 \Pi^i=\frac{\partial\mathcal L_P}{\partial\dot B_i}=B^{0i}.
\end{equation*}
The first equation is a primary constraint.  The $\mu=0$ field equation is a
secondary constraint and gives, at every fixed time,
\begin{equation*}
 \partial_i\Pi^i+\mu^2B_0=0,
 \qquad
 B_0=-\frac{1}{\mu^2}\partial_i\Pi^i.
\end{equation*}
Equivalently, using $\Pi^i=\dot B_i-\partial_iB_0$,
\begin{equation*}
 (\mu^2-\nabla^2)B_0=-\partial_i\dot B_i.
\end{equation*}
For nonzero $\mu$ this removes $B_0$ and leaves $D-1$ canonical spatial
degrees of freedom.  On the reduced phase space impose
\begin{equation*}
 [B_i(t,\mathbf x),\Pi^j(t,\mathbf y)]
 =\ii\delta_i{}^j\delta^{D-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y),
 \qquad [B_i,B_j]=[\Pi^i,\Pi^j]=0,
\end{equation*}
with all commutators involving $B_0$ obtained from the constraint.

The mode expansion that solves the constraints is
\begin{equation*}
 B_\mu(x)=\int\frac{\dd^{D-1}\mathbf p}
 {\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^{D-1}}}
 \sum_{r=1}^{D-1}\left[\epsilon_\mu{}^{(r)}(p)a_r(\mathbf p)\ee^{+\ii p\cdot x}
 +\epsilon_\mu{}^{(r)}(p)^*a_r^\dagger(\mathbf p)\ee^{-\ii p\cdot x}\right],
\end{equation*}
where $p^0=\omega_{\mathbf p}$ and
\begin{equation*}
 [a_r(\mathbf p),a_s^\dagger(\mathbf q)]
 =\delta_{rs}\delta^{D-1}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q).
\end{equation*}
The polarization completeness relation from Problem 4.1 is
\begin{equation*}
 \sum_r\epsilon_\mu{}^{(r)}(p)\epsilon_\nu{}^{(r)}(p)^*
 =\eta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{\mu^2}.
\end{equation*}
Using the two possible time orderings and the oscillator commutator gives
\begin{align*}
 D_{\mu\nu}(x)&\equiv\langle0|TB_\mu(x)B_\nu(0)|0\rangle\\
 &=\int\frac{\dd^D p}{(2\pi)^D}\,
 \frac{-\ii\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}+p_\mu p_\nu/\mu^2\right)}
 {p^2+\mu^2-\ii\varepsilon}\,\ee^{+\ii p\cdot x}.
\end{align*}
The first line is a sum of positive-frequency modes for $x^0>0$ and
negative-frequency modes for $x^0<0$.  The second line follows by the usual
$p^0$ contour integral.  It displays covariance even though the canonical
construction eliminated $B_0$ asymmetrically.

Let
\begin{equation*}
 K_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}(\partial^2-\mu^2)-\partial_\mu\partial_\nu.
\end{equation*}
Away from $x=0$, the factor multiplying $\ii$ in the momentum-space propagator,
$-(\eta_{\mu\nu}+p_\mu p_\nu/\mu^2)/(p^2+\mu^2)$, is the inverse of this
Proca operator on the transverse massive subspace.  At $x=0$, the
derivatives of the time-ordering step functions produce the canonical contact
term.  With the Feynman prescription the complete equation is
\begin{equation*}
 -\ii K_{\mu\rho}D^{\rho}{}_{\nu}(x)
 =\eta_{\mu\nu}\delta^D(x).
\end{equation*}
The minus sign follows from the lower-index Proca operator and the standard
choice of the vector propagator numerator.  Contracting with $\partial^\mu$ gives the corresponding
constraint and confirms that the Green function is the Feynman Green function
of the Proca equation.

\BanksSolution{4.3}
This solution uses the four-dimensional two-component spinor notation.  A field in
$(2j+1,0)$ is a totally
symmetric undotted spinor with $2j$ indices.  Choose intertwiners
$u_{\alpha(2j)}(p)$ and $v_{\alpha(2j)}(p)$ obeying the finite-dimensional
Lorentz transformation law.  In this four-dimensional ($D=4$) specialization,
a local chiral field has the form
\begin{equation*}
 \Psi_{\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_{2j}}(x)
 =\int\frac{\dd^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3 2|\mathbf p|}
 \left[u_{\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_{2j}}(p)a_{-j}(\mathbf p)\ee^{+\ii p\cdot x}
 +v_{\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_{2j}}(p)b_{+j}^\dagger(\mathbf p)\ee^{-\ii p\cdot x}\right].
\end{equation*}
Here the labels indicate the helicities of the annihilated particle and the
created antiparticle; reversing both labels is a harmless convention.  The
intertwining equations are chosen so that
\begin{equation*}
 U^\dagger(\Lambda)\Psi_{\alpha(2j)}(x)U(\Lambda)
 =S(\Lambda)_{\alpha(2j)}{}^{\beta(2j)}
 \Psi_{\beta(2j)}(\Lambda^{-1}x).
\end{equation*}
The complex-conjugate $(0,2j+1)$ field has dotted indices and is given, in the
same four-dimensional ($D=4$) specialization, by
\begin{equation*}
 \overline\Psi_{\dot\alpha_1\ldots\dot\alpha_{2j}}(x)
 =\int\frac{\dd^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3 2|\mathbf p|}
 \left[\bar v_{\dot\alpha(2j)}(p)b_{+j}(\mathbf p)\ee^{+\ii p\cdot x}
 +\bar u_{\dot\alpha(2j)}(p)a_{-j}^\dagger(\mathbf p)\ee^{-\ii p\cdot x}\right].
\end{equation*}
For a neutral field the particle and antiparticle operators can be identified
when the representation permits it, so the field contains both helicity signs.

The necessity of the two signs can be seen directly from the causal
commutator.  With both helicities and the statistics sign
$\varepsilon=(-1)^{2j}$, the positive- and negative-frequency terms combine
schematically as
\begin{equation*}
 [\Psi(x),\overline\Psi(y)]_{\varepsilon}
 \;\propto\;
 (\partial_{\alpha\dot\alpha})^{2j}
 \left[D^+(x-y)-D^+(y-x)\right],
\end{equation*}
where the bracket is replaced by an anticommutator for half-integer $j$.
The Pauli--Jordan function in square brackets vanishes at spacelike separation,
and derivatives preserve that support property.  If only one helicity is
present, one of the two frequency terms is absent.  The result is a derivative
of $D^+$ itself, which is nonzero at generic spacelike separation, so no local
field can be formed from that one-helicity Hilbert space.

Finally, a $2\pi$ rotation belongs to the little group.  The particle state
acquires $\ee^{2\pi\ii h}$, while a $(j,0)$ intertwiner acquires
$\ee^{2\pi\ii j}$.  A nonzero intertwiner therefore requires
\begin{equation*}
 \ee^{2\pi\ii h}=\ee^{2\pi\ii j},
 \qquad h=j\pmod{\mathbb Z}.
\end{equation*}
For the helicity pair $h=\pm j$ this implies
$\ee^{4\pi\ii j}=1$, hence $2j\in\mathbb Z$.  The allowed field statistics
are correspondingly $\varepsilon=+1$ for integer $j$ and $\varepsilon=-1$
for half-integer $j$.  This is the helicity quantization and the local
spin-statistics relation required by the construction.

\BanksSolution{4.4}
Use the delta-function normalization fixed in Chapter 2,
\begin{equation*}
 \langle p,m|q,n\rangle
 =\delta^{D-1}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)\delta_{mn}.
\end{equation*}
The mass-shell measure $\dd^{D-1}\mathbf p/(2\omega_{\mathbf p})$ is Lorentz invariant, so the
spatial delta function transforms as
\begin{equation*}
 \delta^{D-1}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda q})
 =\frac{\omega_{\mathbf p}}{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}}}
 \delta^{D-1}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q).
\end{equation*}
Using this identity and the unitarity of the spin-$j$ matrix $D^j(R_W)$,
the inner product of two transformed states is
\begin{align*}
 &\langle U(\Lambda)p,m|U(\Lambda)q,n\rangle\\
 &=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}}}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}
 \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda q}}}{\omega_{\mathbf q}}}
 \delta^{D-1}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda q})
 \left[D^j(R_W(\Lambda,p))^\dagger D^j(R_W(\Lambda,q))\right]_{mn}\\
 &=\delta^{D-1}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)\delta_{mn}.
\end{align*}
The two square-root factors give $\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda p}}/\omega_{\mathbf p}$,
which is cancelled by the Jacobian in the transformed delta function.  Thus
the Lorentz transformation is an isometry on the one-particle space and, by
second quantization, on the corresponding Fock space.

It remains to check multiplication.  From the definition
$R_W(\Lambda,p)=L^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda L(p)$,
\begin{align*}
 R_W(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1,p)
 &=L^{-1}(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1p)\Lambda_2L(\Lambda_1p)
   L^{-1}(\Lambda_1p)\Lambda_1L(p)\\
 &=R_W(\Lambda_2,\Lambda_1p)R_W(\Lambda_1,p).
\end{align*}
Applying (4.1) first with $\Lambda_1$ and then with $\Lambda_2$ gives the
product of square roots
\begin{equation*}
 \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda_1p}}}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}}
 \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda_2\Lambda_1p}}}{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda_1p}}}}
 =\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda_2\Lambda_1p}}}{\omega_{\mathbf p}}},
\end{equation*}
and the product of spin matrices is
\begin{equation*}
 D^j(R_W(\Lambda_2,\Lambda_1p))D^j(R_W(\Lambda_1,p))
 =D^j(R_W(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1,p)).
\end{equation*}
Therefore $U(\Lambda_2)U(\Lambda_1)=U(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1)$, while
$R_W(1,p)=1$ gives $U(1)=1$.  Translations complete the Poincare
representation through
\begin{equation*}
 U(a)|p,m\rangle=\ee^{-\ii p\cdot a}|p,m\rangle,
\end{equation*}
and the semidirect-product law follows from
$\Lambda p\cdot a=p\cdot\Lambda^{-1}a$.  Hence (4.1) is a unitary
representation of the Poincare group.
