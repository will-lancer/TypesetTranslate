\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-001}
The Lagrangian and its canonical momentum are
\[
  L=\frac1{g^2}\left(\frac12\dot q^i\dot q^i-V(q)\right),
  \qquad
  \Pi_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q^i}=\frac{\dot q_i}{g^2}.
\]
Consequently $\dot q_i=g^2\Pi_i$, and the Legendre transform gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  H&=\Pi_i\dot q^i-L\\
   &=g^2\Pi_i\Pi_i-
     \left(\frac{g^2}{2}\Pi_i\Pi_i-\frac{V}{g^2}\right)
    =\frac{g^2}{2}\Pi_i\Pi_i+\frac{V}{g^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For a flat configuration-space metric, coordinate quantization sends
$\Pi_i$ to $-\ii\partial_i$.  There is then no ordering ambiguity, and
$H\psi=E\psi$ becomes
\[
  \left[-\frac{g^2}{2}\partial_i\partial_i+\frac{V}{g^2}\right]\psi
  =E\psi,
\]
which is Eq.~(10.1).

The apparent momentum mismatch in the WKB discussion comes from the definition
of the phase.  Put $\psi=A\exp(\ii W/g^2)$ and suppress derivatives of $A$ for
the leading-order check.  Direct differentiation gives
\[
  -\frac{g^2}{2}\partial_i\partial_i\ee^{\ii W/g^2}
  =\left[\frac{(\partial_iW)^2}{2g^2}
    -\frac{\ii}{2}\partial_i\partial_iW\right]
    \ee^{\ii W/g^2}.
\]
After multiplication of the Schrödinger equation by $g^2$, its leading term is
\[
  \frac12(\partial_iW)^2+V=\mathcal E,
  \qquad \mathcal E\equiv g^2E.
\]
The physical Hamilton principal function is $W/g^2$.  Hence
\[
  \Pi_i=\frac{\partial}{\partial q^i}\left(\frac{W}{g^2}\right)
  =\frac{\partial_iW}{g^2},
  \qquad
  \dot q_i=g^2\Pi_i=\partial_iW.
\]
Thus Banks's momentum in the reduced Hamilton--Jacobi equation is the velocity
$\dot q_i$; the canonical momentum of the original action is
$\dot q_i/g^2$.  With the chapter's convention, $q$, $t$, $V$, $E$, and $g^2$
are dimensionless.  The kinetic and potential terms in $H$ carry the same
overall semiclassical scaling.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-002}
The first variation of the Euclidean action yields
\[
  \frac{\delta S_E}{\delta x(t)}=-\ddot x+V'(x).
\]
Every translated configuration $X_{t_1}(t)=X(t+t_1)$ therefore satisfies
\[
  -\ddot X(t+t_1)+V'(X(t+t_1))=0.
\]
Differentiate with respect to $t_1$ and then set $t_1=0$:
\[
  -\frac{\dd^2}{\dd t^2}\dot X(t)+V''(X(t))\dot X(t)=0.
\]
This is precisely $\mathcal M\dot X=0$.

There is also a geometric derivation.  The map
$t_1\mapsto X_{t_1}$ traces a curve in configuration space, and its tangent is
\[
  \frac{\partial X_{t_1}(t)}{\partial t_1}\bigg|_{t_1=0}=\dot X(t).
\]
The action is constant on that curve.  Its second derivative along the tangent
therefore vanishes at a stationary point, so the Hessian has the same tangent
in its kernel.

If $X$ approaches nondegenerate minima $x_\pm$, then linearization gives
\[
  X(t)-x_\pm=O(\ee^{-\omega_\pm|t|}),
  \qquad \omega_\pm^2=V''(x_\pm)>0.
\]
The derivative has the same exponential decay.  It follows that
$\int\dd t\,\dot X^2$ is finite, which makes the tangent a normalizable
fluctuation.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-003}
At a point $X(a)$ on the saddle curve, draw the unit tangent
$T=X'(a)$ and the mutually orthogonal normals $e_A$.  Nearby points lie on
short normal segments $\eta^Ae_A$; neighboring segments tilt as the curve
bends.  This tilt is the source of the first curvature correction to the
Jacobian.

Because $a$ is arc length, $T\cdot T=1$.  Write
\[
  \kappa_A=T'\cdot e_A,
  \qquad
  \omega_{AB}=e_A'\cdot e_B=-\omega_{BA}.
\]
Differentiating $T\cdot e_A=0$ gives $T\cdot e_A'=-\kappa_A$.  The coordinate
vectors are
\[
  \partial_a y=T+\eta^Ae_A',
  \qquad
  \partial_{\eta^A}y=e_A.
\]
Their metric therefore has the expansion
\[
\begin{aligned}
  g_{aa}&=1-2\kappa_A\eta^A+O(\eta^2),\\
  g_{aA}&=\eta^B\omega_{BA}+O(\eta^2),\\
  g_{AB}&=\delta_{AB}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus
\[
  \sqrt{\det g}=1-\kappa_A\eta^A+O(\eta^2),
  \qquad
  \dd^Ny=\dd a\prod_A\dd\eta^A
  \left[1-\kappa_A\eta^A+O(\eta^2)\right].
\]
Exactly on the curve, the measure is the Cartesian product of arc length and
normal Lebesgue measure.

Let $K_{AB}(a)$ be the Hessian restricted to the normal space.  Stationarity
along the saddle manifold and the tangent zero mode give
\[
  S\bigl(X(a)+\eta^Ae_A(a)\bigr)
  =S_0+\frac12\eta^AK_{AB}(a)\eta^B+O(\eta^3).
\]
At Gaussian order one evaluates the Jacobian at $\eta=0$.  Integration along
the exact symmetry orbit supplies $\int\dd a$ with uniform density, while the
normal integrations supply $(\det K_\perp)^{-1/2}$ with the usual choice of
$2\pi$ normalization.  Curvature enters through the displayed Jacobian and
through higher terms in the action.  Those terms begin beyond the Gaussian
approximation used in Banks's collective-coordinate argument.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-004}
Let $E_{\rm pert}$ be the perturbative ground energy computed in either well,
and write $Z_{\rm osc}=\ee^{-\beta E_{\rm pert}}$ for its leading propagation
factor.  In the notation of Banks's dilute-gas formula, define the positive
one-event fugacity
\[
  \kappa=D^{-1/2}\ee^{-S_0/g^2}.
\]
For $n$ events, integration over ordered centers gives
\[
  \int_{-\beta/2<t_1<\cdots<t_n<\beta/2}
  \dd t_1\cdots\dd t_n=\frac{\beta^n}{n!}.
\]
Once the initial well is fixed, the orientations of successive events are
fixed as well.  Starting in $L$, the first event is an instanton to $R$, the
next is an anti-instanton back to $L$, and the pattern repeats.

A diagonal history has $2N$ events.  It contains $N$ instantons and $N$
anti-instantons, as required by periodicity.  Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \matrixel{L}{\ee^{-\beta H}}{L}
  &=Z_{\rm osc}\sum_{N=0}^\infty
    \frac{(\beta\kappa)^{2N}}{(2N)!}
    =Z_{\rm osc}\cosh(\beta\kappa),\\
  \matrixel{R}{\ee^{-\beta H}}{R}
  &=Z_{\rm osc}\cosh(\beta\kappa).
\end{aligned}
\]
An off-diagonal history has an odd number of events, so
\[
  \matrixel{R}{\ee^{-\beta H}}{L}
  =\matrixel{L}{\ee^{-\beta H}}{R}
  =Z_{\rm osc}\sinh(\beta\kappa).
\]
In the basis $(\lvert L\rangle,\lvert R\rangle)$ this reads
\[
  \ee^{-\beta H}=Z_{\rm osc}
  \left[\cosh(\beta\kappa)\mathbf1
  +\sinh(\beta\kappa)\sigma_x\right]
  =Z_{\rm osc}\ee^{\beta\kappa\sigma_x}
\]
within the two-state semiclassical sector.

The reflection-even and reflection-odd states are
\[
  \lvert+\rangle=\frac{\lvert L\rangle+\lvert R\rangle}{\sqrt2},
  \qquad
  \lvert-\rangle=\frac{\lvert L\rangle-\lvert R\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
\]
Their energies are
\[
  E_+=E_{\rm pert}-\kappa,
  \qquad
  E_-=E_{\rm pert}+\kappa.
\]
The trace provides the same result:
\[
  Z=2Z_{\rm osc}\cosh(\beta\kappa)
   =\ee^{-\beta E_+}+\ee^{-\beta E_-},
  \qquad
  E_--E_+=2D^{-1/2}\ee^{-S_0/g^2}.
\]
The double-well alternation changes the combinatorics from the independent
instanton and anti-instanton sums used for Banks's periodic potential.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-005}
The instanton equation and its derivative are
\[
  -\ddot x_c+V'(x_c)=0,
  \qquad
  -\dddot x_c+V''(x_c)\dot x_c=0.
\]
After division by the constant $\sqrt{S_0}$, the second equation is
\[
  \left[-\frac{\dd^2}{\dd t^2}+V''(x_c)\right]\delta_0=0.
\]

Multiplication of the first equation by $\dot x_c$ yields
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\frac12\dot x_c^{\,2}-V(x_c)\right)=0.
\]
At either asymptotic vacuum, both terms vanish.  The constant is zero and
$\dot x_c^{\,2}=2V(x_c)$.  It follows that
\[
  S_0=\int\dd t\left(\frac12\dot x_c^{\,2}+V(x_c)\right)
  =\int\dd t\,\dot x_c^{\,2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
  \int\dd t\,\delta_0^2
  =\frac1{S_0}\int\dd t\,\dot x_c^{\,2}=1.
\]
Near a nondegenerate vacuum, $x_c-x_\pm$ and $\dot x_c$ decay exponentially.
This decay removes the boundary contribution in the first-integral argument
and makes the norm finite.  Translation of the instanton consequently replaces
the zero-mode Gaussian integral by an integral over its center, with the
normalization used in Banks's collective-coordinate measure.

This normalization is exact for a single instanton.  For a dilute
$n$-instanton configuration, the overall translation tangent has
$\int\dd t\,\dot x_c^2\simeq nS_0$ and is normalized by
$\sqrt{nS_0}$.  Each separately translated, well-separated constituent has
an approximate zero mode normalized by its own $\sqrt{S_0}$.  This distinction
resolves the reuse of the symbol $S_0$ in the surrounding multi-instanton
discussion.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-006}
The asymptotic assumptions imply
\[
  V''(X(t))\longrightarrow m_\pm^2>0
  \quad\hbox{as}\quad t\longrightarrow\pm\infty.
\]
They also make $\dot X$ square-integrable.  From the preceding zero-mode
calculation,
\[
  \mathcal M\dot X=0.
\]
Monotonicity gives a fixed sign for $\dot X$ throughout the interior of the
instanton.  The translation eigenfunction is therefore nodeless.

The Sturm oscillation theorem orders the discrete eigenfunctions of a
one-dimensional Schrödinger operator by their number of nodes: the lowest one
is nodeless, and every higher discrete eigenfunction has at least one node.
Since a nodeless normalizable eigenfunction already occurs at eigenvalue zero,
zero is the lowest eigenvalue.  A negative eigenvalue would require a lower
nodeless state and would force the zero mode into an excited nodal position,
which contradicts the theorem.  Thus the isolated monotone instanton has a
non-negative quadratic fluctuation operator.

The essential spectrum begins at
\[
  \lambda_{\rm cont}=\min(m_-^2,m_+^2)
\]
when the two asymptotic curvatures differ; it begins at $m^2$ when they agree.
The node theorem fixes the bottom of the spectrum.  It permits additional
discrete eigenvalues in $0<\lambda<\lambda_{\rm cont}$, often called shape
modes.  Their presence depends on the detailed potential.  Banks's conclusion
about the zero mode being lowest follows without needing a claim about those
positive bound states.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-007}
We use the canonically normalized radial functional
\[
  S_E=\Omega_{D-1}\int_0^\infty\dd r\,r^{D-1}
  \left[\frac12\phi_r^i\phi_r^i+V(\phi)\right].
\]
It produces the scalar variational equation printed on page 215.  The
schematic functional on the preceding page prints the kinetic term without
the factor $1/2$; reading that display literally would insert a factor
$1/2$ in front of $\partial^iV$ below.  The equation that Banks asks us to
generalize fixes the convention used here.

Variation of the radial action gives the vector equation
\[
  \phi_{rr}^i+\frac{D-1}{r}\phi_r^i=\partial^iV.
\]
Choose arc length along the image of the bounce and define
\[
  T^i=\frac{\dd\Phi^i}{\dd\ell},
  \qquad T^iT^i=1,
  \qquad K^i=\frac{\dd T^i}{\dd\ell},
  \qquad T^iK^i=0.
\]
The radial derivatives become
\[
  \phi_r^i=T^i\ell_r,
  \qquad
  \phi_{rr}^i=T^i\ell_{rr}+K^i\ell_r^2.
\]
Substitution into the vector equation yields
\[
  T^i\left(\ell_{rr}+\frac{D-1}{r}\ell_r\right)
  +K^i\ell_r^2=\partial^iV.
\]

Contracting with $T^i$ gives
\[
  \ell_{rr}+\frac{D-1}{r}\ell_r
  =T^i\partial_iV
  =\frac{\dd}{\dd\ell}V(\Phi(\ell)).
\]
For a chosen field-space curve, this is the single-coordinate bounce equation
with effective potential $V_{\rm eff}(\ell)=V(\Phi(\ell))$.  Projection with
$P_\perp^{ij}=\delta^{ij}-T^iT^j$ gives the complementary equation
\[
  \ell_r^2K^i=P_\perp^{ij}\partial_jV.
\]
It says that the normal force bends the field-space path.  These two equations
must be solved together, because the curvature depends on the radial speed
$\ell_r$.  Along any candidate path the action reduces to
\[
  S_E=\Omega_{D-1}\int_0^\infty\dd r\,r^{D-1}
  \left[\frac12\ell_r^2+V_{\rm eff}(\ell)\right].
\]

Regularity at the center requires $\phi_r^i(0)=0$, equivalently
$\ell_r(0)=0$ for a regular parametrization.  At infinity,
$\Phi^i(\ell(r))\to\nu_F^i$ and $\ell_r\to0$.  Positive eigenvalues of the
false-vacuum Hessian give exponential decay, with the familiar radial power
prefactor.

For a field-space metric $G_{ij}$, arc length obeys
$G_{ij}T^iT^j=1$.  Ordinary derivatives along the curve become target-space
covariant derivatives.  The formulas take the invariant form
\[
  D_r\phi_r^i+\frac{D-1}{r}\phi_r^i=\nabla^iV,
  \qquad
  \ell_r^2D_\ell T^i=P_\perp^{ij}\nabla_jV,
\]
with the same tangential equation after contraction by $T_i$.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-008}
Translations generate another family of stationary configurations,
\[
  I_c^a(x)=I^a(x-c).
\]
Its tangent at $c=0$ in the $i$th direction is $-\partial_iI^a$.  This already
identifies the collective-coordinate direction.  For a direct operator check,
differentiate the field equation with respect to $x^i$:
\[
  -\partial^2(\partial_iI^a)
  +\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial I^a\partial I^b}
   \partial_iI^b=0.
\]
The left side is $\mathcal M_{ab}\partial_iI^b$, proving the zero-mode
equation component by component.

For an $O(D)$-invariant one-field profile $I(r)$,
\[
  \partial_iI=\frac{x_i}{r}I_r.
\]
Regularity gives $I_r=O(r)$ near the origin, so this expression is smooth
there.  At large $r$, linearization around a nondegenerate vacuum gives
$I_r=O(r^{-(D-1)/2}\ee^{-mr})$.  Hence
\[
  \int\dd^D\mathbf x\,(\partial_iI)^2<\infty.
\]
If a constant vector $c_i$ obeyed $c_i\partial_iI=0$ everywhere, then
$(c\cdot x)I_r/r$ would vanish in every angular direction where the profile
changes.  This forces $c_i=0$.  The $D$ translation modes are consequently
linearly independent for a localized nonconstant instanton.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-009}
Put $\alpha=1/g^2$ and regulate the infrared at $|\mathbf R|=L$.  The relative
coordinate integral is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal I(L)
  &=2\pi\int_{r_+}^{L}R\,\dd R
    \left(\frac{R}{r_+}\right)^{-\alpha}\\
  &=2\pi r_+^2
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    \frac{(L/r_+)^{2-\alpha}-1}{2-\alpha},&\alpha\ne2,\\[6pt]
    \displaystyle \ln(L/r_+),&\alpha=2.
  \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]
The limit $L\to\infty$ is finite for $\alpha>2$, which means
\[
  g^2<\frac12.
\]
At
\[
  g_c^2=\frac12
\]
the divergence is logarithmic.  For $g^2>1/2$ it grows as a positive power of
$L$.

The interaction cost of separating a pair is
$g^{-2}\ln(R/r_+)$.  The number of available relative positions grows with
the two-dimensional measure $R\,\dd R$.  At small $g^2$ the interaction wins
and instantons occur predominantly in bound neutral pairs.  Beyond the
displayed threshold, large separations dominate and the gas behaves as an
unbound plasma.  This is the dilute-pair version of the
Kosterlitz--Thouless criterion.  A full many-body renormalization-group
treatment describes how the fugacity and effective interaction run near the
transition; the value above follows from Banks's stated bare normalization.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-010}
In an orthonormal polar frame the ansatz gives
\[
  D_r\phi=f'\ee^{\ii n\theta},
  \qquad
  D_\theta^{\rm orth}\phi=\frac{\ii n}{r}(1-a)f\ee^{\ii n\theta},
  \qquad
  B=F_{12}=\frac1r\partial_r(rA_\theta^{\rm orth})
  =\frac{n}{m}\frac{a'}r.
\]
Since $F_{ab}F_{ab}=2B^2$, angular integration reduces the action to
\[
  S_E=\frac{2\pi}{g^2}\int_0^\infty r\,\dd r
  \left[
    \frac{n^2}{2\mu^2m^2}\frac{a'^2}{r^2}
    +f'^2+\frac{n^2}{r^2}(1-a)^2f^2+\mu^2V(f)
  \right].
\]

Smoothness and finite action at the origin are ensured by
\[
  f(r)=O(r^{|n|}),
  \qquad a(r)=O(r^2).
\]
Indeed, the scalar radial and angular terms behave as $r^{2|n|-1}\dd r$,
the magnetic term behaves as $r\,\dd r$, and a bounded core potential
contributes $O(r\,\dd r)$.  At infinity the finite-action conditions are
\[
  f(r)\longrightarrow1,
  \qquad a(r)\longrightarrow1.
\]
They make $D_\theta\phi$ vanish and give the quantized flux
\[
  \int\dd^2\mathbf x\,B=\oint A=\frac{2\pi n}{m}.
\]
Linearizing in $f=1+\delta f$ and $a=1+\delta a$ produces massive radial
equations.  When the scalar and vector masses are positive, their decaying
solutions behave as modified Bessel functions,
$O(r^{-1/2}\ee^{-m_Hr})$ and $O(r^{-1/2}\ee^{-m_Ar})$, up to the powers carried
by the vector component.  Every tail contribution to the action is then
integrable.

It remains to justify the rotational restriction.  The boundary conditions
and mass assumptions above establish finite-action competitors, but they do
not by themselves imply uniqueness or rotational symmetry of the absolute
minimizer.  We therefore state the sufficient hypothesis used in this step:
in the unit-winding sector, assume that an absolute minimizer exists, has one
zero, and is unique modulo translations and gauge transformations.  This is a
separate variational theorem for the chosen potential and couplings.

Fix the center at the unique zero of this unit-winding minimizer.  A rotation
by $\alpha$, followed by
the constant phase transformation $\phi\mapsto\ee^{-\ii n\alpha}\phi$, maps
this centered topological sector to itself and preserves the action.  By the
assumed uniqueness modulo gauge, the transformed minimizer is gauge-equivalent
to the original one for every $\alpha$.  The gauge representatives can be
chosen continuously around the compact rotation group.  In radial gauge the
fixed-point conditions become
\[
  \partial_\theta\phi=\ii n\phi,
  \qquad \partial_\theta A_r=\partial_\theta A_\theta=0.
\]
Their general smooth solution is
\[
  \phi=f(r)\ee^{\ii n\theta},
  \qquad A_r=A_r(r),
  \qquad A_\theta^{\rm orth}=A_\theta^{\rm orth}(r).
\]
A remaining radial gauge transformation sets $A_r=0$, while flux and
regularity write the angular component as $n a(r)/(mr)$.  This proves that a
representative of the absolute minimizer lies in Banks's ansatz under the
stated existence and uniqueness hypothesis.

Restriction of the variational problem to invariant fields, together with the
principle of symmetric criticality, proves that a radial critical point solves
the full Euler--Lagrange equations.  Global minimality needs the additional
argument above.  Higher winding introduces possible separated vortex centers;
their preferred arrangement depends on the coupling.  A potential with extra
vacua can also spoil uniqueness.  These are the reasons the symmetry claim is
safest in the centered unit-winding sector used by Banks's displayed ansatz.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-011}
For a region of area $\mathcal A$, the separate Poisson sums over instantons
and anti-instantons are
\[
\begin{aligned}
  Z(\mathcal A,\theta)
  &=\sum_{N_+,N_-=0}^{\infty}
  \frac{(\zeta\mathcal A)^{N_++N_-}}{N_+!N_-!}
  \exp\left[\frac{\ii\theta}{m}(N_+-N_-)\right]\\
  &=\exp\left[2\zeta\mathcal A\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{m}\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
Here an instanton of flux $2\pi/m$ contributes $\ee^{\ii\theta/m}$, while an
anti-instanton contributes its complex conjugate.

Stokes' theorem gives
\[
  W_k(C)=\exp\left(\ii k\int_A F\right).
\]
An instanton inside $C$ therefore receives the extra factor
$\ee^{\ii2\pi k/m}$, and an anti-instanton receives
$\ee^{-\ii2\pi k/m}$.  Combining these phases with the theta weights replaces
\[
  \frac{\theta}{m}\longmapsto\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{m}
\]
for the gas inside the loop.  Events outside retain the original theta angle.

Write the total area as $\mathcal V$ and suppose the loop and the boundaries
are separated by distances large compared with the inverse masses.  Dilute-gas
factorization then gives
\[
  \langle W_k(C)\rangle
  =\frac{Z(A,\theta+2\pi k)\,
  Z(\mathcal V-A,\theta)}{Z(\mathcal V,\theta)}.
\]
Substitution of the Poisson sums cancels the outside volume and leaves
\[
  \langle W_k(C)\rangle=
  \exp\left\{2\zeta A\left[
  \cos\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{m}\right)
  -\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{m}\right)\right]\right\}.
\]
The area coefficient vanishes precisely when the two displayed cosines agree.
It vanishes for every value of $\theta$ when $k$ is a multiple of $m$, which
is the screening criterion emphasized by Banks.  Isolated theta values can
also make the leading dilute-gas coefficient vanish for a particular
non-multiple $k$; branch selection and higher effects then determine the
long-distance behavior.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-012}
The Bogomolny square on the source page uses the canonical $V=0$
normalization.  This three-dimensional Euclidean instanton is the static
sector of the $D=4$ Lorentzian monopole theory.
\[
% CONVENTION-SCOPE: fixed D=4 reason=three-dimensional Euclidean monopole instanton and D=4 Lorentzian soliton
  S=\frac1{g^2}\int\dd^3\mathbf x\left[
    \frac14(F^a{}_{\mu\nu})^2+
    \frac12(D_\mu\phi)^a(D_\mu\phi)^a\right].
\]
The preceding source display instead puts
$\tfrac12(D_\mu\phi)^2$ inside an overall factor $1/(4g^2)$; that coefficient
does not expand to the square or the bound printed on the next page.  The
square, the BPS equation, and $S\ge\nu|M|/g^2$ fix the convention used here.

With $B^a{}_\lambda=\tfrac12\epsilon_{\lambda\mu\nu}F^a{}_{\mu\nu}$, the volume
definition is
\[
% CONVENTION-SCOPE: fixed D=4 reason=three-dimensional Euclidean monopole instanton and D=4 Lorentzian soliton
  \nu M=\int\dd^3\mathbf x\,B^a{}_\lambda(D_\lambda\phi)^a.
\]
The invariant inner product on the adjoint obeys the covariant product rule
\[
  D_\lambda(\phi^aB^a{}_\lambda)
  =(D_\lambda\phi)^aB^a{}_\lambda
   +\phi^a D_\lambda B^a{}_\lambda.
\]
The contraction $\phi^aB^a{}_\lambda$ is a gauge singlet, so its covariant
derivative equals its ordinary derivative.  The Bianchi identity is
\[
  D_\lambda F_{\mu\nu}+D_\mu F_{\nu\lambda}
  +D_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}=0,
  \qquad\hbox{equivalently}\qquad
  D_\lambda B^a{}_\lambda=0.
\]
Consequently
\[
  B^a{}_\lambda(D_\lambda\phi)^a
  =\partial_\lambda(\phi^aB^a{}_\lambda).
\]
The divergence theorem now gives
\[
  \nu M=\int_{S^2_\infty}\dd S_\lambda\,\phi^aB^a{}_\lambda.
\]

To compare this with the form notation, orient the sphere as the boundary of
the three-dimensional volume.  Pullback of
$F^a=\tfrac12F^a{}_{\mu\nu}\dd x^\mu\wedge\dd x^\nu$ to the sphere satisfies
\[
  \int_{S^2}\phi^aF^a
  =\frac12\int_{S^2}\phi^aF^a{}_{\mu\nu}
    \epsilon_{\lambda\mu\nu}\dd S_\lambda
  =\int_{S^2}\dd S_\lambda\,\phi^aB^a{}_\lambda.
\]
Thus
\[
  M=\frac1{\nu}\int_{S^2_\infty}\phi^aF^a.
\]
Finite action supplies the needed limit: $D\phi\to0$ makes the direction
$\phi^a/\nu$ covariantly constant at infinity, so the integrand measures flux
in the surviving Abelian direction.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-013}
In $D=4$ Euclidean space with orientation $\epsilon^{0123}=+1$, for an Abelian
field,
\[
  \dd(A\wedge F)=\dd A\wedge F-A\wedge\dd F=F\wedge F.
\]
In components define
\[
  K^\mu=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}A_\nu F_{\rho\sigma}.
\]
The Bianchi identity removes the term in which the derivative hits $F$, while
antisymmetry replaces $\partial_\mu A_\nu$ by
$F_{\mu\nu}/2$.  Hence
\[
  \partial_\mu K^\mu
  =\frac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
    F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}
  =F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}.
\]

For the matrix-valued conventions in the question, the graded Leibniz rule
gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \dd C
  =\operatorname{tr}\bigg[
    (\dd A)^2-\frac23\bigl(
    \dd A\,A^2-A\,\dd A\,A+A^2\dd A\bigr)\bigg].
\end{aligned}
\]
Under the trace,
\[
  \operatorname{tr}(A\,\dd A\,A)
  =-\operatorname{tr}(\dd A\,A^2),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{tr}(A^2\dd A)
  =\operatorname{tr}(\dd A\,A^2).
\]
The first minus sign occurs because a one-form crosses total degree three.
It follows that
\[
  \dd C=\operatorname{tr}\left[(\dd A)^2-2\dd A\,A^2\right].
\]

\begin{aligned}

  &=\operatorname{tr}\left[

  &=\operatorname{tr}\left[(\dd A)^2-2\dd A\,A^2\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
The last equality uses
$\operatorname{tr}A^4=-\operatorname{tr}A^4=0$, obtained by moving one
one-form factor cyclically past the other three.  Therefore
\[

For a complex number with $\operatorname{Im}z>0$, spectral calculus gives
\[
  \int_0^\infty\dd T\,\ee^{\ii T(z-H)}
  =\frac{\ii}{z-H}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
  \frac1{z-H}=-\ii\int_0^\infty\dd T\,
  \ee^{\ii zT}\ee^{-\ii HT}.
\]
Taking the trace yields the first formula in the exercise.

Resolve the trace in coordinate states:
\[
  \Tr\ee^{-\ii HT}
  =\int\dd q_0\,\matrixel{q_0}{\ee^{-\ii HT}}{q_0}.
\]
Divide $T$ into $N$ intervals, insert $N-1$ coordinate resolutions and a
momentum resolution in each short-time kernel, and take $N\to\infty$.  The
initial and final coordinate are identified by the trace.  Thus
\[
  \Tr\ee^{-\ii HT}
  =\int_{q(T)=q(0)}\mathcal Dq\,
  \exp\left(\frac{\ii}{g^2}S_T[q]\right),
\]
where any determinant arising from momentum integration belongs to the
definition of $\mathcal Dq$.  Combining this expression with the proper-time
integral gives
\[
  \Tr\frac1{z-H}
  =-\ii\int_0^\infty\dd T
  \int_{q(T)=q(0)}\mathcal Dq\,
  \ee^{\ii zT}\ee^{\ii S_T[q]/g^2}.
\]
Banks's symbol $[\dd q(t)]$ includes the $-\ii\,\dd T$, the fixed-$T$
normalization, and the integration over periodic paths.  On this enlarged
space, the period is the functional $T[q]$, which produces his compact
notation.

Writing $z=E+\ii\epsilon$ with $\epsilon>0$ supplies
$\ee^{-\epsilon T}$ and makes the proper-time endpoint convergent.  The
boundary value $\epsilon\downarrow0$ defines the Lorentzian prescription.  A
nonconstant periodic classical path has a zero mode obtained by shifting its
time origin.  The trace naturally integrates over the possible base point on
the orbit, producing a factor of its primitive period.  A stationary-phase
calculation phrased in terms of unbased closed orbits must divide out this
translation orbit and include its collective-coordinate Jacobian, so that the
same factor appears once.

For field theory, coordinate states become field eigenstates

  \ee^{\ii S_T[\Phi]/g^2}.
\]
Gauge theories require gauge fixing, its determinant, and a trace over the
physical Hilbert space.  The periodic-time and proper-time structure remains
the one displayed above.

  \qquad

\[

  \frac12(\phi_c')^2+V(\phi_c)\right]
  =\frac1{g^2}\int\dd x\,(\phi_c')^2.
\]

\[
  \dot\phi_v=-\gamma v\phi_c'(y),
  \qquad
  \partial_x\phi_v=\gamma\phi_c'(y),
  \qquad
  \dd x=\frac{\dd y}{\gamma}.

  \qquad

\]

\[
  E=\int\dd x\,T^{00}=\gamma M,
  \qquad
  \mathbf P=\int\dd x\,T^{01}=\gamma vM.
\]
It follows at once that $E^2-\mathbf P^2=M^2$.  Thus

Conservation of $T^{\mu\nu}$ and spatial localization make this integral

eigenstates, and boosting those states extends the pole along

Assume that $G$ is a connected Lie group and that $H$ is a closed subgroup,
so $H\to G\to G/H$ is a fibration.  All homotopy groups are based at the
identity and its coset; when $H$ is disconnected, $\pi_1(H)$ means the
fundamental group of its identity component.

Let $f:S^2\to G/H$ represent the asymptotic Higgs field.  Choose local lifts
$g_N:D_N\to G$ and $g_S:D_S\to G$ on northern and southern disks.  On their
equatorial overlap they project to the same coset, so
\[
  g_N(\theta)=g_S(\theta)h(\theta),
  \qquad h(\theta)\in H.
\]
After fixing the base point, $h$ is a loop in $H$.  Its homotopy class is
$\partial[f]$.

The group operation in $\pi_2$ can be represented by pinching the sphere to a
wedge of two spheres and placing representatives $f_1$ and $f_2$ on the two
pieces.  Choose their lifts compatibly at the pinch point.  Along the equator,
the resulting transition loop traverses $h_1$ and then $h_2$.  Therefore
\[
  \partial([f_1]+[f_2])
  =[h_1*h_2]
  =[h_1]+[h_2],
\]
which proves that $\partial$ is a homomorphism.

The fibration $H\to G\to G/H$ has the exact segment
\[
  \pi_2(G)\longrightarrow\pi_2(G/H)
  \xrightarrow{\partial}\pi_1(H)
  \xrightarrow{i_*}\pi_1(G).
\]
Every Lie group has $\pi_2(G)=0$.  Exactness then makes $\partial$ injective,
and it also says
\[
  \operatorname{im}\partial=\ker i_*.
\]
Consequently
\[
  \pi_2(G/H)\simeq
  \ker\!\left[\pi_1(H)\to\pi_1(G)\right].
\]
When $G$ is simply connected, $\pi_1(G)=0$, so the kernel is all of
$\pi_1(H)$ and
\[
  \pi_2(G/H)\simeq\pi_1(H).
\]

The loop $h$ is the clutching function between two asymptotic gauge patches.
For an Abelian factor of $H$, its winding is the magnetic flux.  Membership in
the kernel means that this loop becomes contractible when viewed inside the
full gauge group $G$.  The contraction fills the asymptotic loop through the
monopole core, allowing a smooth finite-energy field configuration.

For a character $\lambda:T\to U(1)$ and a cocharacter
$m:U(1)\to T$, the composition
\[
  U(1)\xrightarrow{m}T\xrightarrow{\lambda}U(1)
\]
has an integer winding number.  Define it as
$\langle\lambda,m\rangle\in\mathbb Z$.  Transport of a state of weight
$\lambda$ around a Dirac string of charge $m$ gives
\[
  \exp\left(2\pi\ii\langle\lambda,m\rangle\right)=1.
\]
In a normalization with dimensional electric and magnetic charges, this is
$e_\lambda g_m=2\pi\langle\lambda,m\rangle$.  For two dyons, locality of their
relative wavefunction requires the antisymmetric pairing
\[
  \langle\lambda_i,m_j\rangle-\langle\lambda_j,m_i\rangle
  \in\mathbb Z,
\]
or
$e_i g_j-e_jg_i=2\pi n_{ij}$.

Let $P$ be the weight lattice of the simply connected cover $\bar G$.  A
representation of $\bar G$ descends to $G_\Gamma=\bar G/\Gamma$ exactly when
the center elements in $\Gamma$ act trivially.  Thus its allowed electric
lattice is
\[
  \Lambda_e(G_\Gamma)
  =\{\lambda\in P:\lambda(\gamma)=1
    \text{ for every }\gamma\in\Gamma\}.
\]
Quotienting can therefore remove electric weights.

Here $\Lambda_e(G_\Gamma)$ is the full character lattice allowed by the
chosen global gauge group, including admissible external probes.  If a model
specifies a smaller set of dynamical matter representations, their weights
generate a sublattice and impose a correspondingly weaker Dirac test.  Banks's
global-form comparison uses the full character lattice, which is the convention
adopted below.

A cocharacter of a maximal torus in $G_\Gamma$ lifts to a path in the maximal
torus of $\bar G$.  The lifted path begins at the identity and ends at some
$\gamma_m\in\Gamma$ after one circuit.  Conversely, such a path projects to a
closed cocharacter in the quotient.  Pairing it with a descended weight is
integral because that weight is trivial on the endpoint $\gamma_m$.  In lattice
language, the Dirac magnetic lattice is the lattice dual to
$\Lambda_e(G_\Gamma)$.

These cocharacters describe all charges compatible with an Abelian Dirac
string.  A smooth monopole obeys the further topological condition
\[
  [m]\in\ker\!\left[\pi_1(H)\to\pi_1(G_\Gamma)\right].
\]
It says that the transition loop around the monopole contracts through the
full gauge group and can be filled by a regular core.  This is Banks's
distinction between the charge lattice visible in the low-energy Maxwell
theory and the sublattice realized by nonsingular solitons.

For $SU(2)$, normalize the Cartan so that fundamental weights are the integers
$\lambda=\pm1$ and adjoint weights are $0,\pm2$.  The electric lattice is
$\mathbb Z$, and its dual cocharacters are $m\in\mathbb Z$.  Since
$SU(2)$ is simply connected, every such loop lies in the kernel and the unit
smooth monopole has $m=1$.

For $SO(3)=SU(2)/\mathbb Z_2$, only the even weights descend:

  \Lambda_e(SO(3))=2\mathbb Z.
\]
The dual Dirac lattice is then $m\in\tfrac12\mathbb Z$.  A path with

are even multiples of the minimal quotient cocharacter.  The minimal smooth
monopole is therefore twice the charge demanded by adjoint electric matter
alone, and it also satisfies Dirac quantization for a possible fundamental
$SU(2)$ weight.

\BanksImplicitSolution{I-CH10-021}
In the $D=4$ Lorentzian setup of the exercise, the fields of a static point
dyon $a$ are
\[
  \mathbf E_a(\mathbf x)=\frac{e_a}{4\pi}
  \frac{\mathbf x-\mathbf r_a}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf r_a|^3},
  \qquad
  \mathbf B_a(\mathbf x)=\frac{g_a}{4\pi}
  \frac{\mathbf x-\mathbf r_a}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf r_a|^3}.
\]
For each $a$, $\mathbf E_a$ and $\mathbf B_a$ are parallel, so its self-term
in $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ vanishes.  Only
$\mathbf E_i\times\mathbf B_j$ and
$\mathbf E_j\times\mathbf B_i$ remain.

It is enough to evaluate one electric--magnetic cross-term.  Put the magnetic
charge $g$ at the origin and the electric charge $e$ at
$R\widehat{\mathbf z}$.  In spherical coordinates about the monopole,
\[
  \mathbf B=\frac{g}{4\pi r^2}\widehat{\mathbf r},
  \qquad
  E_\theta=\frac{eR\sin\theta}{4\pi
  (r^2+R^2-2rR\cos\theta)^{3/2}}.
\]
Axial symmetry leaves only $L_z$.  The vector products give
\[
  [\mathbf r\times(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)]_z
  =-\frac{g}{4\pi r}E_\theta\sin\theta.
\]
After the azimuthal integral,
\[
  L_z=-\frac{egR}{8\pi}
  \int_0^\infty r\,\dd r\int_0^\pi\dd\theta\,
  \frac{\sin^3\theta}
  {(r^2+R^2-2rR\cos\theta)^{3/2}}.
\]
Set $u=\cos\theta$.  The needed radial integral is
\[
  \int_0^\infty
  \frac{r\,\dd r}{(r^2+R^2-2rRu)^{3/2}}
  =\frac1{R(1-u)}.
\]
One way to check it is to shift $r-Ru$ and integrate the two resulting
terms separately.  Therefore the double integral is
\[
  \frac1R\int_{-1}^{1}\dd u\,
  \frac{1-u^2}{1-u}=\frac2R,
\]
and
\[
  \mathbf L_{e,g}=-\frac{eg}{4\pi}\widehat{\mathbf z}.
\]
The vector points from the electric charge toward the magnetic charge.  It is
independent of their separation.

For the pair in the exercise,
$\widehat{\mathbf r}$ points from dyon $i$ to dyon $j$.  The first cross-term
therefore contributes $e_i g_j\widehat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi)$.  Interchanging the
sources reverses the unit vector, so the second contributes
$-e_jg_i\widehat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi)$.  Hence
\[
  \mathbf L_{\rm em}
  =\frac{e_i g_j-e_jg_i}{4\pi}\widehat{\mathbf r}.
\]
The total static field momentum vanishes, making this angular momentum
independent of a common shift of the spatial origin.

The mechanical orbital angular momentum of the relative coordinate is
perpendicular to $\widehat{\mathbf r}$.  Thus the projection of the total
angular momentum along that direction is fixed by the field:
\[
  \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}
  =\frac{e_i g_j-e_jg_i}{4\pi}.
\]
Quantum angular momentum has half-integer projections, so
\[
  \frac{e_i g_j-e_jg_i}{4\pi}=\frac{n_{ij}}2,
  \qquad n_{ij}\in\mathbb Z.
\]
This is
\[
  e_i g_j-e_jg_i=2\pi n_{ij}.
\]
For a purely electric particle paired with a purely magnetic monopole it
reduces to $eg=2\pi n$, the Dirac condition.
