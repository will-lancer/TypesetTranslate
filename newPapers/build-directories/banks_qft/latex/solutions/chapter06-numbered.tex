\BanksSolution{6.1}
Let the incoming electron and photon momenta be $p$ and $k$, and let the
outgoing momenta be $p'$ and $k'$. Thus
$p+k=p'+k'$, with $p^2=p'^2=-m^2$ and $k^2=k'^2=-\mu^2$ in massive QED.
Write $\epsilon=\epsilon(k,\lambda)$ for the incoming polarization and
$\epsilon'=\epsilon(k',\lambda')$ for the outgoing one.  There are two
orderings of the photon vertices on the fermion line.  The corresponding
invariant amplitude is
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal M={}&(-\ii e)^2\bar u(p')\biggl[
 \slashed{\epsilon}'^{\,*}
 \frac{-\ii(\slashed p+\slashed k+m)}{(p+k)^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}
 \slashed{\epsilon}\\
 &\hspace{3.0cm}+
 \slashed{\epsilon}
 \frac{-\ii(\slashed p-\slashed k'+m)}{(p-k')^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}
 \slashed{\epsilon}'^{\,*}\biggr]u(p).
\end{align*}
The first term is the $s$-channel ordering, in which the electron first
absorbs the incoming photon.  The second is the crossed, $u$-channel
ordering.  The two terms are required because the photons are identical.

For a massless photon, replacing either polarization by its momentum makes
the sum vanish.  For example, use
\begin{align*}
 \slashed k\,u(p)&=(\slashed p+\slashed k-m)u(p),\\
 \bar u(p')\slashed k'&=\bar u(p')(\slashed p+\slashed k-m),
\end{align*}
and $p+k=p'+k'$ to cancel the two internal propagators.  The remaining
external Dirac equations give zero.  This is the tree-level Ward identity;
for a massive vector the same formula is used with its physical
polarizations.

\BanksSolution{6.2}
Let $p$ and $P$ be the initial electron and heavy-fermion momenta, and let
$p'$ and $P'$ be the final momenta.  Put
\[
 q=p-p'=P'-P,
 \qquad s=-(p+P)^2,\qquad t=-q^2,\qquad u=-(p-P')^2,
\]
so that $s+t+u=2m^2+2M^2$.  The leading diagram is the $t$-channel vector
exchange graph, and gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal M=(-\ii e)^2\bar u(p')\gamma^\mu u(p)
 \frac{-\ii(\eta_{\mu\nu}+q_\mu q_\nu/\mu^2)}
 {q^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}
 \bar u(P')\gamma^\nu u(P).
\end{equation*}
Both currents are conserved:
\[
 q_\mu\bar u(p')\gamma^\mu u(p)=0,
 \qquad
 q_\nu\bar u(P')\gamma^\nu u(P)=0.
\]
The longitudinal term therefore drops out.  Crossing the outgoing heavy
antifermion in the annihilation process of the text into the initial state,
and crossing the corresponding incoming antifermion into the final state,
turns the $s$-channel graph of Figure 6.2 into this $t$-channel graph.  In
the invariant variables this is the usual interchange of the annihilation
channel with the momentum-transfer channel, with the $v$ spinors continued
to the corresponding $u$ spinors.

The spin sums give
\begin{align*}
 \overline{|\mathcal M|^2}
 &=\frac{e^4}{(t-\mu^2)^2}\frac14
 \operatorname{tr}\!\left[(\slashed p'+m)\gamma^\mu
 (\slashed p+m)\gamma^\nu\right]
 \operatorname{tr}\!\left[(\slashed P'+M)\gamma_\mu
 (\slashed P+M)\gamma_\nu\right]\\
 &=\frac{2e^4}{(t-\mu^2)^2}
 \left[(s-m^2-M^2)^2+(u-m^2-M^2)^2
 +2t(m^2+M^2)\right].
\end{align*}
Here the bar means an average over the two initial spins and a sum over the
two final spins.  The following phase-space and solid-angle formulas are the
\(D=4\) specialization.  In the center-of-mass frame the magnitude of the incoming
and outgoing three-momenta is
\[
 p_{\rm cm}=\frac{\lambda^{1/2}(s,m^2,M^2)}{2\sqrt{s}},
 \qquad
 t=-2p_{\rm cm}^{\,2}(1-\cos\theta),
\]
where $\lambda(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz$.  The two-body phase
space formula consequently gives
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}
 =\frac{1}{64\pi^2s}\,\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}.
\end{equation*}
For a heavy target initially at rest, take $M\to\infty$ while keeping the
electron energy $E=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$ and velocity
$v=|\mathbf p|/E$ fixed.  Recoil is then suppressed by $1/M$, and
\[
 t=-4|\mathbf p|^2\sin^2\frac\theta2+O(M^{-1}).
\]
The leading part of the invariant result is
\[
 \overline{|\mathcal M|^2}
 =\frac{16e^4M^2}{(\mu^2+4|\mathbf p|^2\sin^2(\theta/2))^2}
 \left(E^2-|\mathbf p|^2\sin^2\frac\theta2\right)+O(M).
\]
Using the heavy-target phase space limit gives
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}
 =\frac{4\alpha^2
 \left(E^2-|\mathbf p|^2\sin^2(\theta/2)\right)}
 {\left[\mu^2+4|\mathbf p|^2\sin^2(\theta/2)\right]^2}.
\end{equation*}
For a massless exchanged photon and a unit target charge this becomes
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}
 =\frac{\alpha^2}{4|\mathbf p|^2v^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}
 \left[1-v^2\sin^2\frac\theta2\right].
\end{equation*}
The bracket is the spin-$\frac12$ recoil-free, or Mott, factor.  In the
nonrelativistic limit it tends to one and the expression is Rutherford's
formula.  A nucleus of charge $Z e$ multiplies the cross section by $Z^2$;
finite nuclear recoil and size give corrections outside the stated limit.

\BanksSolution{6.3}
Put
\[
 \mathcal D_0=-\ii\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-M,
 \qquad
 \mathcal D=\mathcal D_0-V,
 \qquad
 S_0=\mathcal D_0^{-1}.
\]
The field-independent determinant can be separated, and the formal
large-mass expansion is
\begin{equation*}
 \log\operatorname{Det}(\mathcal D_0-V)
 =\log\operatorname{Det}\mathcal D_0
 -\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n\Tr\bigl[(S_0V)^n\bigr].
\end{equation*}
The trace includes spacetime, Dirac, and internal indices.  In position
space, its $n$th term is
\begin{align*}
 \Tr[(S_0V)^n]
 ={}&\int\prod_{j=1}^{n}\dd^D x_j\,
 \operatorname{tr}\bigl[S_0(x_1-x_2)V(x_2)S_0(x_2-x_3)V(x_3)\cdots\\
 &\hspace{4.6cm}\cdots S_0(x_n-x_1)V(x_1)\bigr].
\end{align*}
This is the closed-fermion-loop expansion.  With
$V(x)=\ii A_\mu(x)\gamma^\mu+\ii\phi(x)$, the term with $n$ insertions
contains every ordered product of the external matrices; their order must
not be rearranged when $A_\mu$ and $\phi$ fail to commute.

For a slowly varying field, write each insertion in momentum space.  If
$Q_j=q_1+\cdots+q_j$, then
\begin{align*}
 \Tr[(S_0V)^n]
 ={}&\int\frac{\dd^D k}{(2\pi)^D}
 \prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\dd^D q_j}{(2\pi)^D}
 (2\pi)^D\delta^D\!\left(\sum_jq_j\right)\\
 &\times\operatorname{tr}\bigl[S_0(k)V(q_1)S_0(k+Q_1)V(q_2)\cdots
 S_0(k+Q_{n-1})V(q_n)\bigr].
\end{align*}
The derivative expansion is obtained by Taylor expanding every shifted
propagator,
\[
 S_0(k+Q)=S_0(k)+Q_\mu\partial_{k_\mu}S_0(k)
 +\frac12Q_\mu Q_\nu\partial_{k_\mu}\partial_{k_\nu}S_0(k)+\cdots.
\]
After $k=M\ell$, a term with $r$ external derivatives carries the expected
inverse powers of $M$ after the local counterterms have been chosen.  This
also makes the condition $|q_j|\ll M$ precise.

The same organization can be made manifestly covariant with a proper-time
expansion.  After Wick rotation and an inessential convention-dependent
redefinition of the scalar coupling, write the Euclidean operator as
\[
 \mathscr D_E=\gamma^\mu D_\mu+M+\Phi,
 \qquad D_\mu=\partial_\mu+\ii A_\mu,
 \qquad \Phi=\Phi^\dagger,
\]
where $\Phi$ denotes the Hermitian Euclidean source obtained after absorbing
the conventional factor $\ii$ into the continuation of $\phi$.  Thus it
corresponds to the source's $\ii\phi$ term, with the signs changed together
if the opposite Euclidean convention is used.  Its squared operator has Laplace form
\begin{equation*}
 \mathscr D_E^\dagger\mathscr D_E=-D^2+M^2+\mathcal E,
 \qquad
 \mathcal E=2M\Phi+\Phi^2-\gamma^\mu D_\mu\Phi
 -\frac{\ii}{2}\gamma^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu},
\end{equation*}
where $F_{\mu\nu}=[D_\mu,D_\nu]/\ii$.  The parity-even part of the
determinant is therefore organized as
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{Re}\log\operatorname{Det}\mathscr D_E
 & =\frac12\Tr\log\bigl(-D^2+M^2+\mathcal E\bigr)\\
 & =-\frac12\int_0^\infty\frac{\dd s}{s}\,
 \Tr\exp\bigl[-s(-D^2+M^2+\mathcal E)\bigr],
\end{align*}
up to a field-independent normalization.  The real part is the
parity-even part; the phase of the determinant is treated separately.  For
small $s$,
\begin{align*}
 \Tr\ee^{-s(-D^2+M^2+\mathcal E)}
 \sim{}&\frac{\ee^{-sM^2}}{(4\pi s)^{D/2}}\int\dd^D x\,\operatorname{tr}\biggl[\mathbf1-s\mathcal E\\
 &+s^2\left(\frac12\mathcal E^2-\frac1{12}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
 -\frac16D^2\mathcal E\right)+O(s^3)\biggr].
\end{align*}
The $s$ integrals generate the local terms in powers of $A$, $\phi$, and
their derivatives.  In \(D=4\), the terms of dimension four, such as \(F^2\),
\((D\phi)^2\), and \(\phi^4\), are logarithmic and require renormalization;
in general \(D\), the logarithmic terms have dimension \(D\).  Higher-dimensional
terms are suppressed by powers of \(M\).  The phase of the
determinant, which can contain parity-odd terms, is obtained from the
corresponding unsquared operator.  This gives a systematic expansion for
arbitrary Hermitian matrix-valued $A_\mu$ and $\phi$.

\BanksSolution{6.4}
Let $\varphi$ be a charged scalar of mass $m_s$ and charge $Qe$.  For
\(e^-(p_-)+e^+(p_+)\to\varphi(k_-)+\varphi^*(k_+)\), put
$q=p_-+p_+=k_-+k_+$ and $s=-q^2$.  The scalar--scalar--photon vertex gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal M=(-\ii e)\bar v(p_+)\gamma^\mu u(p_-)
 \frac{-\ii(\eta_{\mu\nu}+q_\mu q_\nu/\mu^2)}
 {q^2+\mu^2-\ii\epsilon}
 \bigl[\ii eQ(k_--k_+)^\nu\bigr].
\end{equation*}
The scalar current is transverse because
$q\cdot(k_--k_+)=k_-^2-k_+^2=0$, so the longitudinal propagator term
vanishes.  Neglecting the electron mass, the spin-averaged square is
\begin{align*}
 \overline{|\mathcal M|^2}
 &=\frac{e^4Q^2}{(s-\mu^2)^2}
 \left[p_-^\mu p_+^\nu+p_-^\nu p_+^\mu
 -\eta^{\mu\nu}(p_-\cdot p_+)\right]
 (k_--k_+)_\mu(k_--k_+)_\nu\\
 &=\frac{e^4Q^2s^2}{2(s-\mu^2)^2}\,\beta^2\sin^2\theta,
 \qquad
 \beta=\sqrt{1-\frac{4m_s^2}{s}}.
\end{align*}
The displayed phase-space and solid-angle formulas are the \(D=4\)
specialization.  The two-body phase space has $|\mathbf k|/|\mathbf p|=\beta$, and hence
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}
 =\frac{\alpha^2Q^2s}{8(s-\mu^2)^2}
 \beta^3\sin^2\theta.
\end{equation*}
For a massless photon this is
$\alpha^2Q^2\beta^3\sin^2\theta/(8s)$.  Integrating over the solid angle
gives the useful check
\[
 \sigma_{\rm tot}=\frac{\pi\alpha^2Q^2s}{3(s-\mu^2)^2}\,\beta^3
 \longrightarrow \frac{\pi\alpha^2Q^2}{3s}\,\beta^3
 \quad(\mu\to0).
\]
The extra power of $\beta$ relative to fermion pair production comes from
the momentum carried by the scalar electromagnetic current.

\BanksSolution{6.5}
For a particle of charge $e$, define
$\boldsymbol\pi=\mathbf p-e\mathbf A$ and use the Dirac Hamiltonian
\[
 H=-\beta m+eA_0+\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol\pi.
\]
In the Dirac basis write the positive-energy spinor as
$\psi=(\varphi,\chi)^T$, with \(\chi\) the large lower component.  At
momenta and fields small compared with $m$, the upper component obeys
\[
 \varphi=\frac{\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi}{2m}\chi
 +O(m^{-2}).
\]
Substitution into the lower-component equation gives
\begin{align*}
 H_{\rm eff}
 &=m+eA_0+\frac{(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2}{2m}
 +O(m^{-2})\\
 &=m+eA_0+\frac{\boldsymbol\pi^2}{2m}
 -\frac{e}{2m}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf B+O(m^{-2}),
\end{align*}
where $[\pi_i,\pi_j]=+\ii e\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ was used.  Since
$\mathbf S=\boldsymbol\sigma/2$, the spin-field interaction is
\[
 H_{\rm mag}=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B,
 \qquad
 \boldsymbol\mu=\frac{e}{m}\mathbf S
 =g\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf S.
\]
Thus the minimally coupled Dirac particle has $g=2$.

The on-shell electromagnetic vertex can be written, in the conventions of
the text, as
\begin{equation*}
 \bar u(p+q)\Gamma^\mu(p,q)u(p)
 =\bar u(p+q)\left[F_1(q^2)\gamma^\mu
 +F_2(q^2)\frac{\gamma^{\mu\nu}q_\nu}{2m}
 +F_3(q^2)q^\mu\right]u(p).
\end{equation*}
Current conservation gives $F_3=0$.  The Gordon identity in the same
convention is
\[
 \bar u(p+q)\gamma^{\mu\nu}q_\nu u(p)
 =\bar u(p+q)\bigl[(2p+q)^\mu+2m\gamma^\mu\bigr]u(p).
\]
Consequently the static matrix element is
\begin{equation*}
 \bar u\Gamma^\mu u
 =\bar u\left[(F_1+F_2)\gamma^\mu
 +F_2\frac{(2p+q)^\mu}{2m}\right]u.
\end{equation*}
The electric charge fixes $F_1(0)$, while the spin-dependent magnetic term
contains $F_1(0)+F_2(0)$.  Therefore, for a particle whose charge is used
to normalize $F_1(0)=1$,
\begin{equation*}
 g=2\bigl[1+F_2(0)\bigr],
 \qquad
 F_2(0)=\frac{g-2}{2}.
\end{equation*}
More generally the charge-normalized relation is
$g=2[F_1(0)+F_2(0)]/F_1(0)$.

\BanksSolution{6.6}
Start with the product of Schwinger representations:
\[
 \frac1{A_1\cdots A_n}
 =\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dd s_i\,
 \exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_iA_i\right).
\]
Set
\[
 s_i=x_i s,\qquad s=\sum_i s_i,
 \qquad x_i\geq0,\qquad \sum_i x_i=1.
\]
The change of variables has the simplex Jacobian
\[
 \prod_{i=1}^{n}\dd s_i
 =s^{n-1}\dd s\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dd x_i\,
 \delta\!\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right).
\]
Thus
\begin{align*}
 \frac1{A_1\cdots A_n}
 &=\int_0^\infty\dd s\,s^{n-1}
 \int\prod_i\dd x_i\,\delta\!\left(1-\sum_i x_i\right)
 \exp\left[-s\sum_i x_iA_i\right]\\
 &=(n-1)!\int\prod_i\dd x_i\,
 \delta\!\left(1-\sum_i x_i\right)
 \frac1{\left(\sum_i x_iA_i\right)^n},
\end{align*}
because $\int_0^\infty\dd s\,s^{n-1}e^{-sB}=(n-1)!/B^n$ for $B>0$.
This is the Feynman identity used in the text, with the integration over
the $(n-1)$-simplex understood.

\BanksSolution{6.7}
Let $\mathcal M_0$ be the amplitude for scattering in the external field
with no emitted photon.  The infrared part of the virtual one-loop graphs
comes from the region in which the loop photon momentum $k$ is soft.  In
that region the adjacent fermion propagators reduce to eikonal factors, so
the virtual correction factorizes as
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal M_{\rm virt}^{\rm IR}
 &=-\frac12\mathcal M_0\,e^2
 \int_{\mu<|\mathbf k|<\Lambda}\frac{\dd^{D-1}\mathbf k}
 {(2\pi)^{D-1} 2\omega_{\mathbf k}}\\
 &\quad\left(\frac{p'}{p'\cdot k}-\frac{p}{p\cdot k}\right)^2
 +\text{terms finite as }\mu\to0,
\end{align*}
where $\omega_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+\mu^2}$ and the Lorentz contraction is
understood with
\[
 \sum_\lambda\epsilon^\mu(k,\lambda)\epsilon^{\nu*}(k,\lambda)
 =\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{k^\mu k^\nu}{\mu^2}.
\]
Since
$p\cdot k=-\omega_{\mathbf k}(p^0-\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf k})$, the
radial part in \(D=4\) is
\[
 \int_{\mu}^{\Lambda}\frac{\dd\omega_{\mathbf k}}{\omega_{\mathbf k}}
 =\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}+O(\mu^2).
\]
For a genuine change in the charged-particle trajectory the angular factor
is nonzero.  We may therefore write the virtual contribution to the
probability as
\[
 \delta P_0^{\rm IR}=-\mathcal C(p,p')\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu},
 \qquad \mathcal C(p,p')>0,
\]
with $\mathcal C$ fixed by the displayed angular eikonal integral.  The
unresolved no-photon probability is infrared divergent order by order.

For one additional photon of momentum $k$ and polarization $\epsilon$, the
soft theorem gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal M_1(k,\epsilon)
 =e\mathcal M_0\left[
 \frac{p'\cdot\epsilon^*}{p'\cdot k}
 -\frac{p\cdot\epsilon^*}{p\cdot k}\right]
 +O(k^0).
\end{equation*}
After summing polarizations and integrating over photons with
$\mu<\omega_{\mathbf k}<E$, the corresponding real-emission probability is
\[
 \delta P_1^{\rm IR}=+\mathcal C(p,p')\ln\frac{E}{\mu}
 +\text{terms finite as }\mu\to0.
\]
The upper limit $E$ can be any fixed experimental soft-energy resolution;
it need not vanish with the photon mass.  Adding the two experimentally
indistinguishable alternatives gives
\begin{align*}
 P_0+P_1
 &=P_{\rm Born}\left[1-\mathcal C\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}
 +\mathcal C\ln\frac{E}{\mu}+\text{finite}\right]\\
 &=P_{\rm Born}\left[1-\mathcal C\ln\frac{\Lambda}{E}
 +\text{finite}\right],
\end{align*}
which has a finite $\mu\to0$ limit.  Photons with energies above the
resolution are treated with the exact hard-emission matrix element and do
not introduce a new infrared singularity.  At higher orders every set of
soft photons whose total energy is below the resolution must be included;
the same eikonal factor exponentiates, which is the inclusive version of
the Bloch--Nordsieck cancellation.  Faddeev--Kulish states reorganize this
same soft physics at the amplitude level.
