\subsection{Supersymmetry Algebras}

% Preserve the source's typographic distinction between two-component,
% upright Weyl supercharges and four-component, italic Majorana supercharges.
\newcommand{\WeylQ}{\mathrm{Q}}
\newcommand{\MajoranaQ}{Q}

Consider a general graded Lie algebra of symmetry generators that commute
with the \(S\)-matrix. If \(Q\) is any of the fermionic symmetry generators,
then so will be \(U^{-1}(\Lambda)QU(\Lambda)\), where \(U(\Lambda)\) is the
quantum mechanical operator corresponding to an arbitrary homogeneous
Lorentz transformation \(\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\). Therefore
\(U^{-1}(\Lambda)QU(\Lambda)\) is a linear combination of the complete set
of fermionic symmetry generators, and hence this set of generators must
furnish a representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group. The individual
generators may therefore be classified according to the irreducible
representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group to which they belong.

As described in Section 5.6, the representations of the homogeneous Lorentz
group furnished by any set of operators can be specified by giving their
commutation relations with generators \(\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B\),
defined by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A\equiv\frac12(\mathbf J+i\mathbf K),
\qquad
\mathbf B\equiv\frac12(\mathbf J-i\mathbf K),
\tag{25.2.1}\label{eq:25.2.1}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf J\) and \(\mathbf K\) are the Hermitian generators of
rotations and boosts, respectively. These satisfy the commutation relations
\begin{equation}
[A_i,A_j]=\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}A_k,
\qquad
[B_i,B_j]=\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}B_k,
\qquad
[A_i,B_j]=0,
\tag{25.2.2}\label{eq:25.2.2}
\end{equation}
where \(i\), \(j\), and \(k\) run over the values \(1,2,3\), and
\(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is totally antisymmetric, with
\(\epsilon_{123}=+1\). Thus representations of the homogeneous Lorentz
group are labelled, like states with two independent spins, by a pair of
integers or half-integers \(A\) and \(B\), with elements of the
representation labelled by a pair of indices \(a\) and \(b\), which run by
unit steps from \(-A\) to \(+A\) and from \(-B\) to \(+B\), respectively.
More specifically, a set of \((2A+1)(2B+1)\) operators \(Q_{ab}^{AB}\)
that form an \((A,B)\) representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group
satisfies the commutation relations
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf A,Q_{ab}^{AB}]
=-\sum_{a'}\mathbf J_{aa'}^{(A)}Q_{a'b}^{AB},
\qquad
[\mathbf B,Q_{ab}^{AB}]
=-\sum_{b'}\mathbf J_{bb'}^{(B)}Q_{ab'}^{AB},
\tag{25.2.3}\label{eq:25.2.3}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf J^{(j)}\) is the spin three-vector matrix for angular
momentum \(j\):
\begin{equation}
\left(J_1^{(j)}\mathbin{\pm}iJ_2^{(j)}\right)_{\sigma'\sigma}
=\delta_{\sigma',\sigma\pm1}
\sqrt{(j\mp\sigma)(j\pm\sigma+1)},
\qquad
\left(J_3^{(j)}\right)_{\sigma'\sigma}
=\delta_{\sigma'\sigma}\sigma.
\tag{25.2.4}\label{eq:25.2.4}
\end{equation}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.4}, it follows that\footnote{We use a dagger for
the Hermitian adjoint of an operator, and an asterisk for the complex
conjugate of a number. The dagger \(\dagger\) will also be used for the
transpose of the matrix formed from the Hermitian adjoints of operators or
complex conjugates of numbers.}
\begin{equation}
-\left(\mathbf J^{(j)}\right)_{\sigma',\sigma}^{*}
=(-1)^{\sigma'-\sigma}
\left(\mathbf J^{(j)}\right)_{-\sigma',-\sigma}.
\tag{25.2.5}\label{eq:25.2.5}
\end{equation}
Thus if \(Q_\sigma^j\) are a set of operators that transform according to
the spin \(j\) representation of the rotation group, then so are
\((-1)^{j-\sigma}Q_{-\sigma}^{j\dagger}\). Also,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.1} shows that
\(\mathbf A^\dagger=\mathbf B\). By taking Hermitian adjoints in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.3}, we see that the Hermitian adjoint
\(Q_{ab}^{AB\dagger}\) of operators that transform according to the \((A,B)\)
representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group are related by a similarity
transformation to operators \(\overline Q_{ba}^{BA}\) that transform
according to the \((B,A)\) representation:
\begin{equation}
Q_{ab}^{AB\dagger}
=(-1)^{A-a}(-1)^{B-b}\overline Q_{-b,-a}^{BA}.
\tag{25.2.6}\label{eq:25.2.6}
\end{equation}

The Haag--Lopuszanski--Sohnius theorem~\hyperref[ch25-ref-1]{[1]} states in
part that the fermion symmetry generators can only belong to the
\((0,1/2)\) and \((1/2,0)\) representations. As we have seen, the Hermitian
adjoint of a \((0,1/2)\) or \((1/2,0)\) operator is a linear combination
respectively of \((1/2,0)\) or \((0,1/2)\) operators, so the complete set
of fermionic symmetry operators may be divided into \((0,1/2)\) generators
\(\WeylQ_{ar}\) (with the superscript \(0\,\frac12\) omitted) and their
\((1/2,0)\) Hermitian adjoints \(\WeylQ_{ar}^\dagger\), where \(a\) is a
spinor
index running over the values \(\pm1/2\), and \(r\) is used to distinguish
different two-component generators with the same Lorentz transformation
properties.\footnote{We are using roman instead of italic letters for the
two-component Weyl spinors \(\WeylQ_{ar}\), to distinguish them from
four-component Dirac spinors that will be introduced later in this section.
There is a notation due to van der Waerden, according to which a
\((0,1/2)\) operator like \(\WeylQ\) is written with dotted indices, as
\(\WeylQ_{\dot a}\), while \((1/2,0)\) operators are written with undotted
indices. We will not use this notation here, but will instead explicitly
indicate which two-component spinors transform according to the \((0,1/2)\)
or \((1/2,0)\) representations of the homogeneous Lorentz group.} The
theorem further states that the fermionic generators may be defined so as
to satisfy the anticommutation relations
\begin{equation}
\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}^\dagger\}
=2\delta_{rs}\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu,
\tag{25.2.7}\label{eq:25.2.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}\}=e_{ab}Z_{rs},
\tag{25.2.8}\label{eq:25.2.8}
\end{equation}
where \(P_\mu\) is the four-momentum operator, the
\(Z_{rs}=-Z_{sr}\) are bosonic symmetry generators, and \(\sigma_\mu\)
and \(e\) are the \(2\times2\) matrices (with rows and columns labelled
\(+1/2,-1/2\)):
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\sigma_1=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma_2=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma_3=
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},
\\
\sigma_0=
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
e=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{gathered}
\tag{25.2.9}\label{eq:25.2.9}
\end{equation}
Finally, the fermionic generators commute with energy and momentum:
\begin{equation}
[P_\mu,\WeylQ_{ar}]=[P_\mu,\WeylQ_{ar}^\dagger]=0,
\tag{25.2.10}\label{eq:25.2.10}
\end{equation}
and the \(Z_{rs}\) and \(Z_{rs}^\dagger\) are a set of central charges of this
algebra, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
0
&=[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}]
=[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}^\dagger]
=[Z_{rs},Z_{tu}]
=[Z_{rs},Z_{tu}^\dagger]
\\
&=[Z_{rs}^\dagger,\WeylQ_{at}]
=[Z_{rs}^\dagger,\WeylQ_{at}^\dagger]
=[Z_{rs}^\dagger,Z_{tu}^\dagger].
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.2.11}\label{eq:25.2.11}
\end{equation}

To prove these results, let us start by considering non-vanishing fermionic
symmetry generators that belong to some \((A,B)\) irreducible representation
of the homogeneous Lorentz group, and that therefore may be labelled
\(Q_{ab}^{AB}\), where \(a\) and \(b\) run by unit steps from \(-A\) to
\(+A\) and from \(-B\) to \(+B\), respectively. As already mentioned, the
Hermitian adjoint is related to operators belonging to the \((B,A)\)
representation by Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.6}, so the anticommutator of these
operators must take the form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\{Q_{ab}^{AB},Q_{a'b'}^{AB\dagger}\}
={}&(-1)^{A-a'}(-1)^{B-b'}
\sum_{C=|A-B|}^{A+B}
\sum_{D=|A-B|}^{A+B}
\sum_{c=-C}^{C}
\sum_{d=-D}^{D}
\\
&{}\cdot C_{AB}(Cc;a,-b')C_{AB}(Dd;-a',b)X_{cd}^{CD},
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.2.12}\label{eq:25.2.12}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{AB}(j\sigma;ab)\) is the usual Clebsch--Gordan coefficient for
coupling spins \(A\) and \(B\) to form spin \(j\), and \(X_{cd}^{CD}\) is
the \((c,d)\)-component of an operator that transforms according to the
\((C,D)\) representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group. Using the
well-known unitarity properties of the Clebsch--Gordan coefficients, we may
express the operator \(X_{cd}^{CD}\) in terms of these anticommutators:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X_{cd}^{CD}
={}&
\sum_{a=-A}^{A}
\sum_{b=-B}^{B}
\sum_{a'=-A}^{A}
\sum_{b'=-B}^{B}
(-1)^{A-a'}(-1)^{B-b'}
\\
&{}\cdot C_{AB}(Cc;a,-b')C_{AB}(Dd;-a',b)
\{Q_{ab}^{AB},Q_{a'b'}^{AB\dagger}\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.2.13}\label{eq:25.2.13}
\end{equation}
Not all of these operators are necessarily non-zero. But the only non-zero
Clebsch--Gordan coefficients \(C_{AB}(j\sigma,ab)\) for
\(j=\sigma=A+B\) and \(j=-\sigma=A+B\) are the ones for
\(a=A,b=B\) and \(a=-A,b=-B\), respectively, which both have the value
unity, so by taking \(C=D=c=-d=A+B\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.13}, we find
that
\begin{equation}
X_{A+B,-A-B}^{A+B,A+B}
=(-1)^{2B}\{Q_{A,-B}^{AB},Q_{A,-B}^{AB\dagger}\}.
\tag{25.2.14}\label{eq:25.2.14}
\end{equation}

This cannot vanish unless \(Q_{A,-B}^{AB}=0\), which would imply (taking
commutators with the `lowering' operators \(A_1-iA_2\) and `raising'
operators \(B_1+iB_2\)) that all the \(Q_{ab}^{AB}\) vanish. Hence if
there are any non-vanishing \((A,B)\) fermionic generators, then their
anticommutators with their adjoints must at least involve non-zero bosonic
symmetry generators belonging to the \((A+B,A+B)\) representation.

Now, the Coleman--Mandula theorem tells us that the bosonic symmetry
generators consist of the \((1/2,1/2)\) generators \(P_\mu\) of
translations, the \((1,0)+(0,1)\) generators \(J_{\mu\nu}\) of proper
Lorentz transformations, and perhaps the \((0,0)\) generators \(T_A\) of
various internal symmetries. (Recall that symmetric traceless tensors of rank
\(N\) transform according to the representation \((N/2,N/2)\),
antisymmetric tensors of rank 2 transform according to the representation
\((1,0)+(0,1)\), while Dirac fields transform according to the
representation \((1/2,0)+(0,1/2)\).) Hence the fermionic symmetry generators
can only belong to representations \((A,B)\) with \(A+B\leq1/2\). These
operators turn bosons into fermions and vice-versa, so they cannot be
scalars, leaving only the \((1/2,0)\) and \((0,1/2)\) representations, as
was to be shown. Labelling the linearly independent \((0,1/2)\) fermionic
generators as \(\WeylQ_{ar}\), the anticommutator
\(\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}^\dagger\}\) belongs to the representation
\((0,1/2)\times(1/2,0)=(1/2,1/2)\), and therefore must be proportional to
the only \((1/2,1/2)\) bosonic symmetry generator, the momentum four-vector
\(P_\mu\). Lorentz invariance dictates that the form of this relation must
be
\begin{equation}
\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}^\dagger\}
=2N_{rs}\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu,
\tag{25.2.15}\label{eq:25.2.15}
\end{equation}
where \(N_{rs}\) is a numerical matrix.

To see this, we use the isomorphism of the Lorentz group (or more properly
its covering group) with the group \(SL(2,\mathbb C)\) of two-dimensional
unimodular complex matrices \(\lambda\), discussed in Section 2.7. The
effect of a Lorentz transformation \(\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\) on the
\((0,1/2)\) fermionic generators is
\begin{equation}
U^{-1}(\Lambda)\WeylQ_{ar}U(\Lambda)
=\sum_b\lambda_{ab}\WeylQ_{br},
\tag{25.2.16}\label{eq:25.2.16}
\end{equation}
where \(\Lambda\) is the Lorentz transformation defined by
\begin{equation}
\lambda\sigma_\mu\lambda^\dagger
=\Lambda^\nu{}_\mu\sigma_\nu.
\tag{25.2.17}\label{eq:25.2.17}
\end{equation}
We can check that Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.16} applies for \((0,1/2)\) operators
by noting that for an infinitesimal Lorentz transformation
\(\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu=\delta^\mu{}_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu\), with
\(\omega_{\mu\nu}=-\omega_{\nu\mu}\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.17} is satisfied
for
\[
\lambda
=\mathbf{1}
+\frac12\left[\frac12i\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_{ij}+\omega_{k0}\right]\sigma_k,
\]
while\footnote{Here \(K_i\) is defined as \(J_{i0}\). There was a mistake
in the first two printings of Volume I: \(K_i\) was defined as \(J^{i0}\)
in Sections 2.4, 3.3, and 3.5, but as \(J_{i0}\) in Sections 5.6 and 5.9,
with \(\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B\) throughout given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.1}.}
\[
U(\Lambda)
=\mathbf{1}+\frac12i\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}
=\mathbf{1}+\frac12i\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_{ij}J_k-i\omega_{i0}K_i.
\]
(Repeated latin indices \(i,j,k\) are summed over values \(1,2,3\).) In
this case, by equating the coefficients of \(\omega_{ij}\) and
\(\omega_{i0}\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.16}, we find that
\[
[\mathbf J,\WeylQ_a]
=-\frac12\sum_b\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ab}\WeylQ_b,
\qquad
[\mathbf K,\WeylQ_a]
=-\frac12i\sum_b\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ab}\WeylQ_b,
\]
or, equivalently,
\[
[\mathbf B,\WeylQ_a]
=-\frac12\sum_b\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ab}\WeylQ_b,
\qquad
[\mathbf A,\WeylQ_a]=0,
\]
which shows that an operator satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.16} belongs to
the \((0,1/2)\) representation. Now, the \(\sigma_\mu\) form a complete
set of \(2\times2\) matrices, so we can put the anticommutator
\(\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}^\dagger\}\) in the form
\(N^\mu_{rs}(\sigma_\mu)_{ab}\), where \(N^\mu_{rs}\) is some matrix of
operators. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.2.16} and \eqref{eq:25.2.17} show that these
operators are four-vectors, in the sense that
\(U^{-1}(\Lambda)N^\mu U(\Lambda)=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu N^\nu\), and so by
the Coleman--Mandula theorem they must be proportional to \(P^\mu\), the
only four-vector of bosonic symmetry operators. Setting
\(N^\mu_{rs}=2P^\mu N_{rs}\) then gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.15}.

Now we will apply a linear transformation to the \(\WeylQ_{ar}\), to put
their anticommutators in the form \eqref{eq:25.2.7}. For this purpose, we
need to establish that the matrix \(N_{rs}\) is Hermitian and
positive-definite. That it is Hermitian follows immediately by taking the
Hermitian adjoint of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.15}. To see that it is
positive-definite, recall that the \(\WeylQ_{ar}\) are taken linearly
independent, so for any non-zero linear combination
\(Q\equiv\sum_r d_a c_r\WeylQ_{ar}\) there must be some state
\(\ket{\Psi}\) that \(Q\) does not annihilate. Taking the expectation
value of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.15} in this state implies that
\[
2\bra{\Psi}
\sum_{ab}\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu d_a d_b^*
\ket{\Psi}
\sum_{rs}c_rc_s^*N_{rs}
=\bra{\Psi}\{Q,Q^\dagger\}\ket{\Psi}>0.
\]
This shows immediately that \(\sum_{rs}c_rc_s^*N_{rs}\) cannot vanish for
any \(c_r\) that are not all zero, so \(N_{rs}\) is either
positive-definite or negative-definite. The operator
\(\sum_{ab}(\sigma_\mu)_{ab}P^\mu d_ad_b^*\) is positive on the space of
physical states with \(-P^\mu P_\mu\geq0\) and \(P^0>0\), so the matrix
\(N_{rs}\) must be positive-definite.\footnote{This argument may be turned
around. Assuming a supersymmetry with \(N_{rs}\) positive-definite, as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.7}, we can deduce that \(P^0>0\) for all
states~\hyperref[ch25-ref-2]{[2]}. However, this conclusion is not valid
when gravitation is taken into account, and unless gravitation is taken
into account a shift in the energy of all states by the same amount would
have no physical effects.}

We may now define new fermionic generators
\[
\WeylQ'_{ar}\equiv\sum_sN^{-1/2}_{rs}\WeylQ_{as},
\]
for which the anticommutators take the form
\[
\{\WeylQ'_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}^{\prime\dagger}\}
=2\delta_{rs}\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu.
\]
From now on we shall assume that the fermionic generators are defined in
this way, and drop the primes, so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.7} is satisfied.

Next we must show that the \(\WeylQ_{ar}\) commute with the momentum
four-vector \(P_\mu\). The commutator of a \((1/2,1/2)\) operator like
\(P_\mu\) with a \((0,1/2)\) operator like \(\WeylQ\) can only be a
\((1/2,0)\) or \((1/2,1)\) operator, but we have seen that there are no
\((1/2,1)\) symmetry generators, so the commutator of \(P_\mu\) with
\(\WeylQ\) can only be proportional to the \((1/2,0)\) symmetry generator
\(\WeylQ^\dagger\). Lorentz invariance requires this relation to take the form
\begin{equation}
[\mathcal M_{ab},\WeylQ_{cr}]
=\sum_s e_{ac}K_{rs}\WeylQ_{bs}^\dagger,
\tag{25.2.18}\label{eq:25.2.18}
\end{equation}
where \(K\) is a numerical matrix, and \(\mathcal M\) is the matrix of
operators
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M\equiv\sigma_\mu P^\mu.
\tag{25.2.19}\label{eq:25.2.19}
\end{equation}
(The matrix \(e_{ac}\) is the Clebsch--Gordan coefficient for coupling two
spins \(1/2\) to give zero spin.) It is straightforward then to calculate
that
\begin{equation}
\left[
\mathcal M_{-\frac12,-\frac12},
\left[
\mathcal M_{-\frac12,-\frac12},
\{\WeylQ_{\frac12 r},\WeylQ_{\frac12 s}^\dagger\}
\right]
\right]
=-4(\mathcal M)_{-\frac12,-\frac12}(KK^\dagger)_{rs}.
\tag{25.2.20}\label{eq:25.2.20}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.7}, the left-hand side is a linear combination
of multiple commutators \([P_\mu,[P_\nu,P_\lambda]]\), all of which
vanish, while \((\mathcal M)_{-1/2,-1/2}\) is non-zero for generic
momenta, so \(KK^\dagger=0\), and therefore \(K=0\), which with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.18} shows that
\([P_\mu,\WeylQ_{ar}]=0\). The Hermitian adjoint gives
\([P_\mu,\WeylQ_{ar}^\dagger]=0\).

Now we can take up the anticommutator of two \(\WeylQ\)s. The
anticommutator of two \((0,1/2)\) symmetry generators must be a linear
combination of \((0,1)\) and \((0,0)\) symmetry generators. The
Coleman--Mandula theorem tells us that the only \((0,1)\) symmetry
generators are linear combinations of the generators \(J_{\nu\lambda}\)
of proper homogeneous Lorentz transformations, but since the
\(\WeylQ\)s commute with \(P_\mu\) so must their anticommutators, and
Eq.~(2.4.13) shows that no linear combination of the \(J_{\nu\lambda}\)
commutes with \(P_\mu\). This leaves just \((0,0)\) operators, which
commute with both \(P_\mu\) and \(J_{\mu\nu}\). Lorentz invariance then
requires the anticommutators of the \(\WeylQ\)s with each other to take
the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.8}. The internal symmetry generators
\(Z_{rs}\) are antisymmetric in \(r\) and \(s\) because the whole
expression must be symmetric under interchange of \(r\) and \(a\) with
\(s\) and \(b\), and the matrix \(e_{ab}\) is antisymmetric in \(a\) and
\(b\).

It now only remains to show that the \(Z\)s are central charges. It follows
immediately from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.2.8} and \eqref{eq:25.2.10} that
\begin{equation}
[P_\mu,Z_{rs}]=0.
\tag{25.2.21}\label{eq:25.2.21}
\end{equation}
Next consider the generalized Jacobi identity \eqref{eq:25.1.5} involving
two \(\WeylQ\)s and a \(\WeylQ^\dagger\):
\[
0
=[\{\WeylQ_{ar},\WeylQ_{bs}\},\WeylQ_{ct}^\dagger]
+[\{\WeylQ_{bs},\WeylQ_{ct}^\dagger\},\WeylQ_{ar}]
+[\{\WeylQ_{ct}^\dagger,\WeylQ_{ar}\},\WeylQ_{bs}].
\]
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.2.7} and \eqref{eq:25.2.10} show that the second and
third terms vanish, so
\begin{equation}
[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{ct}^\dagger]=0.
\tag{25.2.22}\label{eq:25.2.22}
\end{equation}
Finally, consider the generalized Jacobi identity for a \(Z\), a
\(\WeylQ\), and a \(\WeylQ^\dagger\):
\[
0
=-[Z_{rs},\{\WeylQ_{at},\WeylQ_{bu}^\dagger\}]
+\{\WeylQ_{bu}^\dagger,[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}]\}
-\{\WeylQ_{at},[\WeylQ_{bu}^\dagger,Z_{rs}]\}.
\]
The first and third terms vanish because of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.2.21} and
\eqref{eq:25.2.22}, respectively, so we are left with the second term
\begin{equation}
\{\WeylQ_{bu}^\dagger,[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}]\}=0.
\tag{25.2.23}\label{eq:25.2.23}
\end{equation}
Now, \([Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}]\) is a \((0,1/2)\) symmetry generator, so it
must be a linear combination of the \(\WeylQ\)s:
\begin{equation}
[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}]
=\sum_uM_{rstu}\WeylQ_{au}.
\tag{25.2.24}\label{eq:25.2.24}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.23} then reads
\[
\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu M_{rstu}=0,
\]
for all \(a,b,r,s,t\), and \(u\). Since the operator
\(\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu\) is not zero, we conclude that \(M_{rstu}=0\), so
that
\begin{equation}
[Z_{rs},\WeylQ_{at}]=0.
\tag{25.2.25}\label{eq:25.2.25}
\end{equation}
Using the anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:25.2.8} and its adjoint
together with the commutation relations \eqref{eq:25.2.22} and
\eqref{eq:25.2.25} and their adjoints then gives
\begin{equation}
[Z_{rs},Z_{tu}]
=[Z_{rs},Z_{tu}^\dagger]
=[Z_{rs}^\dagger,Z_{tu}^\dagger]=0,
\tag{25.2.26}\label{eq:25.2.26}
\end{equation}
which finishes the proof of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.11}, and with it the proof
of the Haag--Lopuszanski--Sohnius theorem.

Of course, the fact that the \(Z_{rs}\) are central charges of the
supersymmetry algebra does not rule out the possibility that there may be
\emph{other} Abelian or non-Abelian internal symmetries. Let \(T_A\) span
the complete Lie algebra of bosonic internal symmetries. Then
\([T_A,\WeylQ_{ar}]\) is a \((0,1/2)\) symmetry generator, so it must be
a linear combination of the \(\WeylQ\)s:
\begin{equation}
[T_A,\WeylQ_{ar}]
=-\sum_s(t_A)_{rs}\WeylQ_{as}.
\tag{25.2.27}\label{eq:25.2.27}
\end{equation}
From the Jacobi identity for two \(T\)s and a \(\WeylQ\), we learn that
the \(t_A\) matrices furnish a representation of the internal symmetry
algebra
\begin{equation}
[t_A,t_B]=i\sum_C C^C_{AB}t_C,
\tag{25.2.28}\label{eq:25.2.28}
\end{equation}
where the coefficients \(C^C_{AB}\) are the structure constants of the
internal symmetry algebra
\begin{equation}
[T_A,T_B]=i\sum_C C^C_{AB}T_C.
\tag{25.2.29}\label{eq:25.2.29}
\end{equation}
The \(Z_{rs}\) will then be central charges not only of the superalgebra
consisting of the \(\WeylQ\)s, \(\WeylQ^\dagger\)s, \(P_\mu\), \(Z\)s,
and \(Z^\dagger\)s, but also of the larger symmetry superalgebra that in
addition
contains all the \(T_A\). To see this, note from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:25.2.27} and \eqref{eq:25.2.8} that
\[
[T_A,Z_{rs}]
=-\sum_{r'}(t_A)_{rr'}Z_{r's}
-\sum_{s'}(t_A)_{ss'}Z_{rs'},
\]
so the \(Z_{rs}\) form an \emph{invariant} Abelian subalgebra of the whole
bosonic symmetry algebra. But recall that in proving the Coleman--Mandula
theorem we found that the complete Lie algebra of internal bosonic
symmetries, which in our case is spanned by the \(T_A\), is isomorphic to
a direct sum of a compact semi-simple Lie algebra and several \(U(1)\)
algebras. The only invariant Abelian subalgebras of such a Lie algebra are
spanned by \(U(1)\) generators, so the \(Z_{rs}\) must be \(U(1)\)
generators, and hence commute with all the \(T_A\).

Even though the \(Z\)s commute with all symmetry generators, they are not
just numbers; they are quantum operators, whose value may vary from state
to state. In fact, the \(Z\)s must obviously take the value zero for a
supersymmetric vacuum state, which is annihilated by all supersymmetry
generators, but they need not vanish in general. In Section 27.9 we will
see how the \(Z\)s may be calculated in gauge theories with extended
supersymmetry.

In the absence of central charges, the supersymmetry algebra
\eqref{eq:25.2.7}, \eqref{eq:25.2.8} is invariant under a group \(U(N)\)
of internal symmetries
\begin{equation}
\WeylQ_{ar}\longrightarrow\sum_sV_{rs}\WeylQ_{as},
\tag{25.2.30}\label{eq:25.2.30}
\end{equation}
with \(V_{rs}\) an \(N\times N\) unitary (not necessarily unimodular)
matrix. This is known as \(R\)-symmetry. This symmetry may or may not be
a good symmetry of the action, and if it is then it may be violated by
anomalies, or it may be spontaneously broken, or it may be a good symmetry
of nature.

A supersymmetry algebra where \(r,s\), etc. run over \(N>1\) values is
known as an \emph{\(N\)-extended supersymmetry}. Where there is just one
\(\WeylQ\), the condition \(Z_{rs}=-Z_{sr}\) tells us that the \(Z\)s
vanish, yielding a simpler form of the anticommutation relations
\begin{equation}
\{\WeylQ_a,\WeylQ_b^\dagger\}=2\sigma^\mu_{ab}P_\mu,
\tag{25.2.31}\label{eq:25.2.31}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\{\WeylQ_a,\WeylQ_b\}=0.
\tag{25.2.32}\label{eq:25.2.32}
\end{equation}
This is known as the case of \emph{simple supersymmetry}, or \(N=1\)
supersymmetry. In this case the \(R\)-symmetry transformations are \(U(1)\)
phase transformations
\begin{equation}
\WeylQ_a\longrightarrow\exp(i\varphi)\WeylQ_a,
\tag{25.2.33}\label{eq:25.2.33}
\end{equation}
with \(\varphi\) a real phase.

For several purposes it is convenient to combine the \((0,1/2)\) operators
\(\WeylQ_{ar}\) together with \((1/2,0)\) operators, which according to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.6} may be taken as
\(e_{ab}\WeylQ_{br}^\dagger\), into
four-component Majorana spinor generators \(\MajoranaQ_{\alpha r}\),
defined as
\begin{equation}
\MajoranaQ_r\equiv
\begin{pmatrix}
e\WeylQ_r^\dagger\\
\WeylQ_r
\end{pmatrix},
\tag{25.2.34}\label{eq:25.2.34}
\end{equation}
or more explicitly
\[
\MajoranaQ_{1r}=\WeylQ_{-\frac12 r}^\dagger,
\qquad
\MajoranaQ_{2r}=-\WeylQ_{\frac12 r}^\dagger,
\qquad
\MajoranaQ_{3r}=\WeylQ_{\frac12 r},
\qquad
\MajoranaQ_{4r}=\WeylQ_{-\frac12 r}.
\]
This is a Majorana spinor in the sense that
\[
\MajoranaQ_r=-\beta\epsilon\gamma_5\MajoranaQ_r^*,
\]
where \(\beta\), \(\epsilon\), and \(\gamma_5\) are \(4\times4\) matrices
that may be written as the \(2\times2\) block matrices:
\[
\beta=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\epsilon=
\begin{pmatrix}
e&0\\
0&e
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\gamma_5=
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf{1}&0\\
0&-\mathbf{1}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
(The properties of Majorana spinors are reviewed in the appendix to
Chapter 26.) The form \eqref{eq:25.2.34} is chosen in accordance with the
usual notation for the four-component Dirac representation of the
homogeneous Lorentz group, in which according to Eq.~(5.4.4) the rotation
and boost generators are represented according to Eqs.~(5.4.19) and
(5.4.20) by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal J_i
=\frac12
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_i&0\\
0&\sigma_i
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathcal K_i
=-\frac i2
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_i&0\\
0&-\sigma_i
\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{25.2.35}\label{eq:25.2.35}
\end{equation}
With Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.1}, this shows that the operators \(\mathbf A\)
and \(\mathbf B\) act only on the top two and bottom two components of
the Dirac spinor, respectively, which is why we use the \((0,1/2)\)
operators \(\WeylQ_{ar}\) as the bottom rather than the top components in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.34}.

In this four-component notation, the fundamental anticommutation relations
\eqref{eq:25.2.31} and \eqref{eq:25.2.32} for simple supersymmetry read
\begin{equation}
\{\MajoranaQ,\overline{\MajoranaQ}\}
=2
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-e(\sigma_\mu P^\mu)^T e\\
\sigma_\mu P^\mu&0
\end{pmatrix}
=-2iP_\mu\gamma^\mu.
\tag{25.2.36}\label{eq:25.2.36}
\end{equation}
Our notation for Dirac matrices is reviewed in the Preface to this volume;
here we need only recall that
\begin{equation}
\gamma^0=-i\beta
=-i
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\sigma_0\\
\sigma_0&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
=-i
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
-\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0
\end{pmatrix},
\tag{25.2.37}\label{eq:25.2.37}
\end{equation}
while \(e\boldsymbol{\sigma}^T e=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\),
\(e\sigma_0e=-\sigma_0\), and as usual
\(\overline{\MajoranaQ}=\MajoranaQ^\dagger\beta\). The presence of central
charges will change this formula in the case of extended supersymmetry;
instead of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.36}, we have
\begin{equation}
\{\MajoranaQ_r,\overline{\MajoranaQ}_s\}
=-2iP_\mu\gamma^\mu\delta_{rs}
+\left(\frac{\mathbf{1}+\gamma_5}{2}\right)Z_{sr}^\dagger
+\left(\frac{\mathbf{1}-\gamma_5}{2}\right)Z_{rs}.
\tag{25.2.38}\label{eq:25.2.38}
\end{equation}
The analysis presented here for the case of four spacetime dimensions will
be repeated, in a somewhat less explicit form, for general spacetime
dimensions in Chapter 32. As we will see there, the supersymmetry generators
always belong to the fundamental spinor representation of the
higher-dimensional Lorentz group, even in theories with extended objects
that allow the construction of bosonic symmetry generators other than those
allowed by the Coleman--Mandula theorem.

\end{center}

In theories of massless particles that are invariant under the conformal
symmetry algebra \eqref{eq:24.B.34}--\eqref{eq:24.B.35} there are two
additional bosonic symmetry generators \(D\) and \(K_\mu\) that can appear
on the right-hand side of the supersymmetry anticommutation relations.
These new generators have the Lorentz transformation properties of a scalar
and a vector, respectively, just like \(Z_{rs}\) and \(P_\mu\), so once
again the fermionic generators must belong to the fundamental \((1/2,0)\)
spinor representation of the Lorentz algebra, and its Hermitian adjoint,
the \((0,1/2)\) representation. It is convenient to classify all generators
also according to their commutators with the dilation generator \(D\); an
operator \(X\) is said to have dimensionality \(a\) if
\begin{equation}
[X,D]=iaX.
\tag{25.2.39}\label{eq:25.2.39}
\end{equation}
Inspection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.34} shows that the bosonic symmetry
generators \(J^{\mu\nu}\), \(P^\mu\), \(K^\mu\), and \(D\) have
dimensionalities \(0,+1,-1\), and \(0\), respectively. Also, the
generators of any Lie group of internal symmetries have dimensionality
0. The anticommutator of a fermionic generator of dimensionality \(a\)
with its adjoint is a positive-definite bosonic operator of dimensionality
\(2a\), so since the only positive-definite bosonic symmetry generators
are linear combinations of the components of \(P_\mu\) and \(K_\mu\), the
only fermionic symmetry generators have dimensionalities \(+1/2\) and
\(-1/2\).

The \((0,1/2)\) fermionic symmetry generators of dimensionality \(1/2\)
and their adjoints may again be assembled into Majorana spinors
\(\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}\), with
\begin{equation}
\{\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha},\overline{\MajoranaQ}_{s\beta}\}
=-2iP_\mu(\gamma^\mu)_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{rs},
\tag{25.2.40}\label{eq:25.2.40}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[P_\mu,\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}]=0,
\tag{25.2.41}\label{eq:25.2.41}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[D,\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}]
=-\frac12i\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}.
\tag{25.2.42}\label{eq:25.2.42}
\end{equation}
(Note that central charges are not allowed here, because their
dimensionality would be 0, not \(+1\).) The commutators of the \(K_\mu\)
with the \(\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}\) are linear combinations of Majorana
fermionic symmetry generators \(\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\alpha}\), which Lorentz
invariance allows us to write in the form
\begin{equation}
[K^\mu,\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}]
=i(\gamma^\mu)_{\alpha\beta}\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\beta}.
\tag{25.2.43}\label{eq:25.2.43}
\end{equation}
(An arbitrary factor on the right-hand side has been absorbed into the
normalization of \(\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\beta}\). The phase of the right-hand
side is chosen so that \(\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\beta}\) will satisfy the standard
reality condition (26.A.2) for Majorana spinors.) The
\(\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\beta}\) have dimensionality \(+1/2-1=-1/2\), so
\begin{equation}
[D,\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\alpha}]
=+\frac12i\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\alpha}.
\tag{25.2.44}\label{eq:25.2.44}
\end{equation}
Taking the commutator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.43} with \(P^\nu\) and using
the commutator of \(K^\mu\) with \(P^\nu\) given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:24.B.34} gives
\begin{equation}
[P^\nu,\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\alpha}]
=-i(\gamma^\nu)_{\alpha\beta}\MajoranaQ_{r\beta}.
\tag{25.2.45}\label{eq:25.2.45}
\end{equation}
We see that the \(\MajoranaQ\)s and \(\MajoranaQ^\#\)s are paired. By
taking the commutator of the anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:25.2.40}
with \(K_\mu\) we find the anticommutators of the
\(\MajoranaQ^\#\)s and \(\MajoranaQ\)s:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\{\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\alpha},\overline{\MajoranaQ}_{s\beta}\}
={}&2iD\delta_{rs}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
+2J_{\mu\nu}\delta_{rs}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}
\\
&{}+O_{rs}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
+O'_{rs}(\gamma_5)_{\alpha\beta},
\end{aligned}
\tag{25.2.46}\label{eq:25.2.46}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}\equiv-i[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]/4\), and
\(O_{rs}\) and \(O'_{rs}\) are Lorentz invariant operators of zero
dimensionality with
\begin{equation}
O_{rs}=-O_{sr},
\qquad
O'_{rs}=+O'_{sr}.
\tag{25.2.47}\label{eq:25.2.47}
\end{equation}
Taking the commutator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.43} with \(K_\nu\) and using
the fact that \([K_\nu,K_\mu]=0\), we find that
\((\gamma^\mu)_{\alpha\beta}[K^\nu,\MajoranaQ_{r\beta}]\) is symmetric in
\(\mu\) and \(\nu\), which with a little algebra tells us that
\begin{equation}
[K^\nu,\MajoranaQ_{r\beta}]=0.
\tag{25.2.48}\label{eq:25.2.48}
\end{equation}
Also, taking the commutator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.46} with \(K_\nu\)
gives
\begin{equation}
\{\MajoranaQ^\#_{r\alpha},
\overline{\MajoranaQ^\#}_{s\beta}\}
=+2iK_\mu(\gamma^\mu)_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{rs}.
\tag{25.2.49}\label{eq:25.2.49}
\end{equation}
Finally, taking the commutator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.2.46} with
\(\MajoranaQ_{t\gamma}\) shows that \(O_{rs}\) and \(O'_{rs}\) act as the
generators of the \(R\)-symmetry group \(U(N)\), with the left- and
right-handed parts of \(\MajoranaQ_{r\alpha}\) transforming according to
the representations \(\mathbf N\) and \(\overline{\mathbf N}\),
respectively, while the \(P_\mu\), \(K_\mu\) and \(D\) are
\(U(N)\)-invariant. The \(U(N)\) commutation relations of these generators
with each other and with other generators together with
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:24.B.34}, \eqref{eq:24.B.35},
\eqref{eq:25.2.40}--\eqref{eq:25.2.49} and the commutators of
\(J_{\mu\nu}\) and \(D\) with the various generators constitute the
\emph{superconformal algebra}. One of the outstanding differences between
this algebra and that of ordinary simple or \(N\)-extended supersymmetry is
that the \(U(N)\) symmetry is not merely an outer automorphism of the
algebra that may or may not be a symmetry of the action---it is a part of
the superconformal algebra, which therefore must be a symmetry of any
action that is supersymmetric and conformally invariant.
