  \SupplementarySolution{1}{Superspace Delta Functions and Chiral Projection}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Grassmann Delta Saturation.}
    Choose a leaf of the tree and call its adjacent vertex \(v\).  The
      corresponding propagator contains
      \(\delta^4(\theta_{\rm leaf}-\theta_v)\), so integration over the leaf
      coordinate simply replaces it by \(\theta_v\).  Repeating this operation
      removes one vertex and one delta function at each step, leaving
      \[
        \int d^4\theta\,\mathcal I(\theta)
      \]
      at the final vertex.  This proves the assertion by induction on the
      number of vertices.  A line that closes a loop supplies another
      \(\delta^4(\theta-\theta')\) after the two coordinates have already
      been identified.  Its undifferentiated value is
      \(\delta^4(0)=0\), because a fermionic delta function is a product of
      four Grassmann differences.  A nonzero loop therefore requires four
      spinor derivatives, distributed by the \(D\)-algebra, to saturate that
      remaining delta function.
    \item \emph{Chiral Projection Test.}
    The algebra quoted below Eq.~\eqref{eq:30.2.16} gives
      \[
        \mathcal D_R^2\mathcal D_L^2\mathcal D_R^2
          =-16\Box\mathcal D_R^2 .
      \]
      Hence
      \[
        \mathcal P_L^2
          =\frac{\mathcal D_L^2
            (\mathcal D_R^2\mathcal D_L^2\mathcal D_R^2)}
            {(16\Box)^2}
          =-\frac{\mathcal D_L^2\mathcal D_R^2}{16\Box}
          =\mathcal P_L .
      \]
      Three right-handed superderivatives vanish by antisymmetry, so
      \(\mathcal D_R\mathcal P_L=0\); the image is left-chiral.
      The inverse \(\Box^{-1}\) is defined with a boundary prescription on
      modes of nonzero four-momentum.  Harmonic or exactly zero-momentum modes
      lie in the kernel of \(\Box\) and must either be fixed separately or
      removed from the projected function space.
    \item \emph{Potential-Field Redundancy.}
    Since there are only two independent right-handed Grassmann
      derivatives,
      \[
        \delta\Phi=\mathcal D_R^2\mathcal D_RX=0.
      \]
      Thus \(S\) contains a redundancy analogous to the longitudinal part of
      an ordinary vector potential.  Couple it to a source through
      \(I_J=\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,SJ+\mathrm{H.c.}\).  Superspace integration
      by parts gives
      \[
        \delta I_J
          =\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,(\mathcal D_RX)J
          =-\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,X\mathcal D_RJ
      \]
      for bosonic \(X\).  Invariance for arbitrary \(X\) therefore requires
      \(\mathcal D_RJ=0\).  Equivalently, only the projected chiral part of a
      source couples to the physical combination \(\Phi=\mathcal D_R^2S\).
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{2}{Projectors, Gauge Fixing, and Propagator Kernels}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Projector Completeness.}
    The proof of \(\mathcal P_L^2=\mathcal P_L\) was given in
      Supplementary Exercise S.30.1, part (b); complex conjugation gives
      \(\mathcal P_R^2=\mathcal P_R\).  Products such as
      \(\mathcal P_L\mathcal P_R\) contain three consecutive derivatives of
      one chirality and vanish.  Consequently
      \[
        \mathcal P_T^2
          =(\mathbf1-\mathcal P_L-\mathcal P_R)^2
          =\mathcal P_T,\qquad
        \mathcal P_T\mathcal P_L
          =\mathcal P_T\mathcal P_R=0,
      \]
      while their sum is the identity by definition.  Under
      \(V\to V+\Omega+\Omega^*\), a chiral \(\Omega\) belongs to the image of
      \(\mathcal P_L\) and \(\Omega^*\) to that of \(\mathcal P_R\).  Thus
      \(\mathcal P_TV\) is the gauge-invariant sector.
    \item \emph{Weighted Vector Gauge.}
    Orthogonality makes the inversion algebraic on the three projected
      subspaces.  The inverse kernel is
      \[
        \Delta_\alpha^V
          =-\frac1{\Box}
           \left[
             \mathcal P_T+\alpha(\mathcal P_L+\mathcal P_R)
           \right]\delta^8(z-z').
      \]
      Multiplication by the stated quadratic operator gives
      \(\mathcal P_T+\mathcal P_L+\mathcal P_R=\mathbf1\).  If
      \(J=\mathcal P_TJ\) and \(J'=\mathcal P_TJ'\), then
      \[
        J\Delta_\alpha^VJ'
          =-\frac1{\Box}J\mathcal P_TJ',
      \]
      because both longitudinal projectors annihilate either source.
      Therefore physical amplitudes are independent of \(\alpha\), although
      off-shell Green functions of \(V\) need not be.
    \item \emph{Massive Chiral Kernel.}
    In the basis \((\Phi,\Phi^*)\), the diagonal entries of the quadratic
      kernel are the chiral mass insertions \(m,m^*\), while the off-diagonal
      entries are the projected kinetic operator.  Multiplying the kernel by
      its conjugate block form diagonalizes it and produces
      \(\Box-|m|^2\).  Thus the mixed propagator has the structure
      \[
        \Delta_{\Phi\Phi^*}
          =\frac14\mathcal D_R^2\mathcal D_L^{\prime\,2}
           \Delta_{F,m}(x-x')\delta^4(\theta-\theta'),
        \qquad
        (\Box-|m|^2)\Delta_{F,m}=-\delta^4 ,
      \]
      while the same-chirality kernels contain \(m\mathcal D_R^2\) or
      \(m^*\mathcal D_L^2\) divided by the same operator.  Every component
      therefore has the common momentum-space denominator
      \(p^2+|m|^2-i\epsilon\), displaying equal scalar and fermion masses;
      the auxiliary component still contributes only numerator contact
      pieces in addition to this pole.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{3}{D-Algebra in a One-Loop Supergraph}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Derivative Transfer Rule.}
    Translation invariance of the Berezin integral implies that the
      integral of a total superderivative vanishes.  If \(A\) has Grassmann
      parity \(|A|\), the graded Leibniz rule gives
      \[
        0=\int d^4\theta\,\mathcal D(AB)
         =\int d^4\theta\,
           \left[(\mathcal DA)B+(-1)^{|A|}A\mathcal DB\right].
      \]
      Therefore
      \[
        \int d^4\theta\,(\mathcal DA)B
          =-(-1)^{|A|}\int d^4\theta\,A\mathcal DB .
      \]
      For two fermionic superfields \(A,B\), \(|A|=1\), so the right-hand
      side has a plus sign.  Applied to a translation-invariant propagator,
      this rule is equivalent to
      \(\mathcal D_z\Delta(z-z')=-\mathcal D_{z'}\Delta(z-z')\), with the
      additional graded sign supplied when the derivative crosses a
      fermionic external factor.
    \item \emph{One-Loop Self-Energy Algebra.}
    One chiral and one antichiral cubic vertex are required by line
      orientation.  Move their spinor derivatives onto one internal line.
      The identity
      \(\mathcal D_R^2\mathcal D_L^2\mathcal D_R^2
      =-16\Box\mathcal D_R^2\) cancels one propagator denominator, while the
      two fermionic delta functions identify the vertex coordinates.  The
      remaining structure is therefore
      \[
        \Gamma_{\rm div}^{(1)}
          \propto |g|^2
          \int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,\Phi^*(x,\theta)\Phi(x,\theta)
          \int\frac{d^dp}{(2\pi)^d}
            \frac1{p^2(p+k)^2}.
      \]
      The momentum integral has a logarithmic pole.  Because the external
      fields occur with the full \(d^4\theta\) measure, the counterterm is a
      wavefunction or Kahler correction proportional to
      \([\Phi^*\Phi]_D\); it cannot renormalize the chiral superpotential.
    \item \emph{Vanishing Vacuum Loop.}
    First collapse a spanning tree of propagators.  All vertex Grassmann
      coordinates then become a single \(\theta\), but each remaining loop
      line contributes a fermionic delta function evaluated at coincident
      arguments.  Without external superfields, integration by parts can
      move all surplus spinor derivatives around the graph; there is no
      external factor on which to leave them.  At least one loop delta
      therefore remains unsaturated and supplies
      \(\delta^4(0)=0\).  Equivalently, if the derivatives saturate every
      loop delta, the last overall factor is independent of \(\theta\), and
      \(\int d^4\theta\,1=0\).  Hence a supersymmetric vacuum graph vanishes
      before any bosonic momentum integration, making the component
      boson--fermion cancellation manifest.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{4}{Spurion Holomorphy and Perturbative Non-Renormalization}
Give each \(\Phi^i\) arbitrary formal flavor charges \(q_i\) and
  \(R\)-charges \(r_i\).  Invariance is restored by assigning
  \[
    q(m_{ij})=-q_i-q_j,\qquad
    R(m_{ij})=2-r_i-r_j,
  \]
  and
  \[
    q(y_{ijk})=-q_i-q_j-q_k,\qquad
    R(y_{ijk})=2-r_i-r_j-r_k.
  \]
  The couplings can therefore be treated as chiral backgrounds without
  changing the symmetry bookkeeping.

  A Wilsonian \(F\)-term must be a local holomorphic function of the
  chiral fields and chiral spurions, have mass dimension three, and
  have \(R\)-charge two.  The formal flavor symmetries exclude terms
  with unmatched indices or inverse powers that are singular at weak
  coupling.  Analyticity around \(m=y=0\), together with the limit in
  which each interaction is turned off, fixes the allowed coefficients
  to their tree values.  Thus, perturbatively,
  \[
    W_{\rm Wilsonian}=W_{\rm tree}.
  \]
  The same argument allows renormalization of the real \(D\)-term
  \[
    \int d^4\theta\,
       Z_i{}^j(\mu)\,\Phi^{\dagger i}\Phi_j .
  \]
  On rescaling to canonical fields, \(m\) and \(y\) acquire the
  corresponding anomalous-dimension factors.  Hence physical
  parameters run even though the holomorphic Wilsonian
  superpotential does not.  Finally, massless propagation can make the
  1PI action nonlocal; apparent chiral terms generated by infrared
  singularities do not contradict the local Wilsonian statement.

  \SupplementarySolution{5}{Tensor Beta Functions for Superpotential Operators}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Yukawa Beta Tensor.}
    Write the bare fields as
      \(\Phi_{B\,i}=(Z^{1/2})_i{}^j\Phi_j\).  Since the holomorphic tensor
      has no independent vertex counterterm, its scale dependence comes
      entirely from the three factors of \(Z^{-1/2}\) needed to express the
      bare superpotential in canonical fields.  Defining
      \(\gamma_i{}^j=\mu(dZ^{1/2}/d\mu)(Z^{-1/2})_i{}^j\), differentiation
      gives
      \[
        \beta_{Y\,ijk}
          =\gamma_i{}^\ell Y_{\ell jk}
           +\gamma_j{}^\ell Y_{i\ell k}
           +\gamma_k{}^\ell Y_{ij\ell}.
      \]
      The right-hand side is symmetric in \(i,j,k\), as required, and
      contains only external-leg renormalization.  This is the
      renormalization-group form of the supergraph non-renormalization
      theorem.
    \item \emph{Mass-Matrix Running.}
    The same external-leg argument now acts on two indices.  At fixed bare
      mass tensor,
      \[
        \beta_{M\,ij}
          =\gamma_i{}^kM_{kj}+\gamma_j{}^kM_{ik},
      \]
      or in matrix notation
      \(\beta_M=\gamma M+M\gamma^{\mathsf T}\).  If
      \(\Phi'=U\Phi\) with constant unitary \(U\), then
      \(M'=U^*MU^\dagger\) and
      \(\gamma'=U\gamma U^\dagger\).  Substitution gives
      \[
        \beta_{M'}=U^*\beta_MU^\dagger,
      \]
      so the equation transforms in the same way as the mass tensor.
      Symmetry of \(M\) is also preserved because the two terms are
      transposes of one another.
    \item \emph{Higher-Operator Running.}
    Every field in the operator must be converted from bare to canonical
      normalization.  With a symmetric coefficient tensor this yields
      \[
        \beta_{C\,i_1\cdots i_n}
          =\sum_{r=1}^{n}
           \gamma_{i_r}{}^j
           C_{i_1\cdots i_{r-1}j i_{r+1}\cdots i_n}.
      \]
      The explicit power \(M^{3-n}\) is fixed if the matching scale is held
      constant; a running matching parameter would add its ordinary
      dimensional contribution separately.  The derivation never used
      power-counting renormalizability.  It used only chirality and the
      absence of a perturbative \(\mathcal F\)-term counterterm, so it
      remains valid for \(n>3\) order by order in the effective-field-theory
      expansion.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{6}{Anomalous Dimensions and Group Factors}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{One-Loop Anomalous Dimension.}
    Flavor covariance permits the Yukawa combination only in the form
      \(Y_{imn}Y^{*jmn}\).  Gauge invariance permits the self-energy group
      factor only as \(C_2(R_i)\delta_i{}^j\).  With the standard
      normalization \(W=Y_{ijk}\Phi_i\Phi_j\Phi_k/6\), one finds
      \[
        \gamma_i{}^j
          =\frac1{16\pi^2}
           \left[
             \frac12Y_{imn}Y^{*jmn}
             -2g^2C_2(R_i)\delta_i{}^j
           \right].
      \]
      The positive Yukawa sign reflects matter-field screening from the
      chiral loop.  The gauge contribution has the opposite sign because
      vector exchange antiscreens the chiral wavefunction in this
      convention.  Both terms are Hermitian in flavor space and
      dimensionless, providing independent checks.
    \item \emph{Casimir Conversion.}
    By definition,
      \[
        \sum_A T_R^AT_R^A=C_2(R)\mathbf1_R,
        \qquad
        \operatorname{tr}_R(T_R^AT_R^B)=T(R)\delta^{AB}.
      \]
      Taking the trace of the first equation gives
      \(C_2(R)\dim R\).  Summing the second equation over \(A=B\) gives
      \(T(R)\dim G\), proving equality.  For \(SU(N)\),
      \(\dim G=N^2-1\), \(\dim\mathbf N=N\), and
      \(T(\mathbf N)=1/2\).  Hence
      \[
        C_2(\mathbf N)=\frac{N^2-1}{2N}.
      \]
      The two sides of the trace identity are then both
      \((N^2-1)/2\).
    \item \emph{Abelian Charge Factor.}
    A chiral superfield of charge \(q\) couples through
      \(\Phi^*e^{-2qV}\Phi\).  Expanding to second order supplies either two
      linear vertices, each proportional to \(q\), or one quadratic seagull
      vertex proportional to \(q^2\).  Thus every one-loop gauge
      self-energy contribution carries \(q^2\).  The non-Abelian product
      \(T^AT^A=C_2(R)\mathbf1\) is therefore replaced by \(q^2\).
      A term linear in \(q\) would contain one external vector insertion or
      an unpaired gauge vertex; it cannot form the neutral chiral two-point
      function and is also forbidden by invariance under charge conjugation
      \(q\to-q\), \(V\to-V\).
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{7}{Field Redefinitions and Effective-Operator Running}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Field-Redefinition Contribution.}
    Differentiate
      \(\Phi_B=Z^{1/2}\Phi\) at fixed \(\Phi_B\):
      \[
        \mu\frac{d\Phi}{d\mu}
          =-(Z^{-1/2}\mu\,dZ^{1/2}/d\mu)\Phi
          =-\gamma\Phi .
      \]
      A coefficient multiplying \(n\) chiral fields must compensate the
      running of every factor.  Therefore its beta function is the sum of
      \(n\) terms, with \(\gamma\) acting on one tensor index at a time.
      There is no extra vertex term because a loop supergraph cannot
      generate an \(\mathcal F\)-term.  This derivation also shows why
      flavor mixing must be treated by matrix multiplication rather than
      by assigning a separate scalar anomalous dimension to each field.
    \item \emph{EFT Triangularity.}
    Insertions of an operator \(M^{-k}\mathcal O_{4+k}\) carry an explicit
      negative power of the heavy scale.  A mass-independent regulator
      cannot turn this factor into \(M^0\); doing so would require a
      compensating positive power of \(M\), which is absent from the light
      theory.  Hence such an insertion contributes only at order \(M^{-k}\)
      or lower and cannot alter the leading renormalizable counterterms.
      Conversely, loops built solely from renormalizable vertices contain
      ultraviolet local terms of arbitrarily high field or derivative
      order when expanded in external momenta.  Those terms have precisely
      the form of higher-dimensional operators and therefore renormalize
      their coefficients.  The mixing matrix is triangular in powers of
      \(1/M\).
    \item \emph{Holomorphic versus Canonical Coupling.}
    For one field, write the holomorphic interaction as
      \(Y_h\Phi^3/6\).  The canonical field
      \(\Phi_{\mathrm c}=Z^{1/2}\Phi\) gives
      \[
        Y_{\mathrm c}=Y_hZ^{-3/2}.
      \]
      Holomorphy and the non-renormalization theorem imply
      \(\mu\,dY_h/d\mu=0\), but differentiating the last equation yields
      \[
        \beta_{Y_{\mathrm c}}
          =-3Y_{\mathrm c}\,
           \mu\frac{d\ln Z^{1/2}}{d\mu}.
      \]
      If the anomalous dimension is defined with the opposite sign for
      field running, this is conventionally written
      \(\beta_Y=3\gamma Y\).  Thus the Wilsonian holomorphic coefficient can
      be fixed while the measurable coupling runs through the normalization
      of its external legs.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{8}{Background Hessian and the One-Loop Kähler Term}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Background Hessian.}
    Put \(\Phi_{\rm total}=\Phi+\varphi\).  Expanding the superpotential,
      \[
        W(\Phi+\varphi)
          =W(\Phi)+W'(\Phi)\varphi
           +\frac12\Psi\,\varphi^2
           +\frac16W'''(\Phi)\varphi^3+\cdots,
        \qquad \Psi=W''(\Phi).
      \]
      The quadratic action contains the mixed kinetic term
      \(\varphi^*\varphi\) and the diagonal chiral entries
      \(\Psi\varphi^2/2\), \(\Psi^*\varphi^{*2}/2\).  Thus, schematically,
      \[
        H=
        \begin{pmatrix}
          \Psi\,\delta_L & K_{LR}\\
          K_{RL} & \Psi^*\,\delta_R
        \end{pmatrix}.
      \]
      A connected diagram containing a cubic quantum vertex has at least
      two loops: contracting its three quantum legs requires another cubic
      vertex and gives \(I=3,V=2\), hence \(L=2\).  The one-loop action is
      therefore determined entirely by this Hessian.
    \item \emph{Logarithm of the Hessian.}
    A bosonic Gaussian integral with quadratic operator \(H\) contributes
      \((\det H)^{-1/2}\).  Dividing by the background-independent
      determinant \(H_0\) and taking the logarithm gives
      \[
        \Gamma^{(1)}
          =\frac{i}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\ln(H/H_0).
      \]
      Writing \(H=H_0(1+X)\) produces
      \[
        \Gamma^{(1)}
          =\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
           \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\operatorname{Tr}X^n .
      \]
      The factor \(1/n\) removes the \(n\) cyclic choices of a starting
      vertex around a ring, while the Gaussian \(1/2\) removes reversal or
      double counting of the quadratic contraction.  Chiral orientation
      may split the expression into a term and its complex conjugate, but
      the combined symmetry factor is unchanged.
    \item \emph{Kahler Dimensional Check.}
    A full-superspace integrand must have mass dimension two because
      \(d^4\theta\) has dimension two.  The only derivative-free,
      phase-invariant background combination with this dimension is
      \(\Psi^*\Psi\).  A loop can introduce the renormalization scale only
      through a dimensionless logarithm.  Consequently
      \[
        K^{(1)}
          =c\,\Psi^*\Psi
           \left[
             \ln\!\left(\frac{\Psi^*\Psi}{\mu^2}\right)-c_0
           \right].
      \]
      Here \(c\) is fixed by the loop integral and \(c_0\) is
      scheme-dependent because it multiplies the local counterterm
      \(\Psi^*\Psi\).  No holomorphic function of \(\Psi\) contributes to
      the full superspace integral except through a Kahler transformation.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{9}{Momentum Integration and Regulator Ordering}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Logarithmic Momentum Integral.}
    In Euclidean signature,
      \[
        \frac{dJ}{dM^2}
          =\mu^{2\epsilon}
           \int\frac{d^{4-2\epsilon}p}{(2\pi)^{4-2\epsilon}}
           \frac1{p^2+M^2}
          =\frac{\mu^{2\epsilon}}{(4\pi)^{2-\epsilon}}
           \Gamma(-1+\epsilon)(M^2)^{1-\epsilon}.
      \]
      With the modified scale \(\bar\mu\), this is
      \[
        \frac{dJ}{dM^2}
          =-\frac{M^2}{16\pi^2}
           \left[
             \frac1\epsilon+1-\ln\!\left(\frac{M^2}{\bar\mu^2}\right)
           \right]+O(\epsilon).
      \]
      Integrating with respect to \(M^2\) gives
      \[
        J(M^2)
          =-\frac{M^4}{32\pi^2}
           \left[
             \frac1\epsilon+\frac32
             -\ln\!\left(\frac{M^2}{\bar\mu^2}\right)
           \right]+\text{constant}.
      \]
      The integration constant is field independent and can be discarded.
    \item \emph{Regulator Ordering Warning.}
    Fubini's theorem permits exchange of integrations only for an
      absolutely convergent integral.  A proper-time representation may
      contain separate ultraviolet and infrared divergent pieces whose
      sum is finite.  Rescaling variables or integrating momentum before
      proper time changes how those pieces are paired, so different orders
      can leave different finite remainders.  The safe prescription is to
      keep a common regulator---for example \(d=4-2\epsilon\) together with
      the same \(i\epsilon\) or proper-time contour---through every
      integration and superspace partial integration.  Combine all terms
      in the domain where they converge, analytically continue the combined
      expression, subtract its local pole, and only then take
      \(\epsilon\to0\).  The resulting finite coefficient is independent
      of intermediate integration order.
  \end{enumerate}

  \SupplementarySolution{10}{Higher-Derivative Terms, Scaling, and Decoupling}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Four-Derivative Invariant.}
    Chirality requires left derivatives on \(\Phi\) and right derivatives
      on \(\Phi^*\).  Lorentz scalar contraction and Grassmann parity require
      an even number of each.  The lowest real candidate is therefore
      \[
        (\mathcal D_L\Phi)^2(\mathcal D_R\Phi^*)^2
        \,\mathcal G\!
        \left(\Phi,\Phi^*,\mathcal D_L^2\Phi,
          \mathcal D_R^2\Phi^*\right),
      \]
      plus terms related by superspace integration by parts.  It contains
      two derivatives of each chirality and is manifestly bosonic.  If all
      spinor derivatives of the background vanish, the expression vanishes,
      so it is distinct from the ordinary Kahler potential
      \(K(\Phi,\Phi^*)\).  Fewer derivatives cannot form a real scalar with
      both chiralities and the required Grassmann parity.
    \item \emph{Auxiliary-Field Order.}
    With fermions and spacetime derivatives set to zero, the chiral
      expansion has
      \(\mathcal D_L\Phi\sim\theta_L\mathcal F\) and
      \(\mathcal D_R\Phi^*\sim\theta_R\mathcal F^*\).  Hence
      \[
        (\mathcal D_L\Phi)^2(\mathcal D_R\Phi^*)^2
          \sim
          (\theta_L^T\epsilon\theta_L)
          (\theta_R^T\epsilon\theta_R)
          |\mathcal F|^4 .
      \]
      The \(d^4\theta\) integral selects this coefficient.  Its leading
      bosonic contribution is therefore quartic in the auxiliary field,
      multiplied by the scalar-dependent value of \(\mathcal G\).  The
      ordinary Kahler term instead begins with
      \(K_{\phi\phi^*}|\mathcal F|^2\).  This higher-order dependence is why
      the new invariant is classified as an effective auxiliary-field
      potential.
    \item \emph{Massless Scaling Form.}
    Each factor \(\mathcal D\Phi\) has dimension \(3/2\), so the four-factor
      numerator has dimension six.  A full-superspace integrand must have
      dimension two; the denominator must therefore have dimension four.
      In the massless theory the only phase-neutral algebraic choice is
      \[
        \frac{
          (\mathcal D_L\Phi)^2(\mathcal D_R\Phi^*)^2
        }{(\Phi^*\Phi)^2}.
      \]
      The numerator and denominator are separately invariant under constant
      phases \(\Phi\to e^{i\tau}\Phi\), and their dimensions are six and
      four, respectively.  The integrand thus has dimension two, while
      \(d^4x\,d^4\theta\) has dimension \(-2\), producing a dimensionless
      action as required.
    \item \emph{Heavy-Field Decoupling.}
    Let \(X=|m+\lambda\Phi|^2=|m|^2+\delta X\) and
      \(F(X)=X[\ln(X/\mu^2)-2]\).  Then
      \(F'(|m|^2)=\ln(|m|^2/\mu^2)-1\) and
      \(F''(|m|^2)=|m|^{-2}\).  Expanding to second order, the terms linear
      in \(\Phi\), as well as the pure \(\Phi^2\) and \(\Phi^{*2}\) terms,
      are chiral plus antichiral Kahler transformations and vanish under
      the full superspace integral.  The mixed term is
      \[
        F(X)\supset
          |\lambda|^2
          \ln\!\left(\frac{|m|^2}{\mu^2}\right)\Phi^*\Phi .
      \]
      Multiplying by the overall one-loop coefficient gives the threshold
      wavefunction correction.  It grows only logarithmically with the
      heavy mass and is absorbed by matching the light-field normalization
      at the scale \(|m|\).
  \end{enumerate}

\end{enumerate}
