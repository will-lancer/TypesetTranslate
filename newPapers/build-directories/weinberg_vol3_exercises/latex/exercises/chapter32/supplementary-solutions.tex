\chapterbackmatter{Solutions to Supplementary Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \SupplementarySolution{1}{Fermionic Clifford Module}
  Choose a vacuum \(\lvert0\rangle\) annihilated by every \(a_i\).
  Anticommutation makes each creator nilpotent and fixes an ordered
  basis
  \[
    (a_1^\dagger)^{n_1}\cdots(a_n^\dagger)^{n_n}
      \lvert0\rangle,\qquad n_i\in\{0,1\}.
  \]
  The \(n\) independent binary choices give \(2^n\) states.  A state
  with \(r=\sum_i n_i\) has fermion parity \((-1)^r\), so the even and
  odd counts are
  \[
    \sum_{r\ {\rm even}}\binom nr
      =\frac{(1+1)^n+(1-1)^n}{2}=2^{n-1},
  \]
  \[
    \sum_{r\ {\rm odd}}\binom nr
      =\frac{(1+1)^n-(1-1)^n}{2}=2^{n-1}
  \]
  for \(n>0\).  Applying annihilators reduces any nonzero invariant
  subspace to the vacuum, after which the creators regenerate the
  whole basis; hence the module is irreducible.

  \SupplementarySolution{2}{Weyl Projectors in Even Dimension}
  Let
  \[
    \gamma_*=\alpha\,\gamma^0\gamma^1\cdots\gamma^{d-1},
  \]
  where the phase \(\alpha\) is chosen so that \(\gamma_*^2=1\).
  Moving any \(\gamma^\mu\) through the other \(d-1\) matrices gives an
  odd number of sign changes, and therefore
  \[
    \{\gamma_*,\gamma^\mu\}=0.
  \]
  It follows that
  \(P_\pm=(1\pm\gamma_*)/2\) obey
  \(P_\pm^2=P_\pm\), \(P_+P_-=0\), and \(P_++P_-=1\).

  Since \(\gamma^\mu\) maps the \(+1\) eigenspace of \(\gamma_*\)
  invertibly onto the \(-1\) eigenspace, the two spaces have equal
  dimension.  A Dirac spinor in \(d=2m\) has dimension \(2^m\), so each
  Weyl spinor has dimension \(2^{m-1}\).  The identity
  \(\gamma^\mu P_\pm=P_\mp\gamma^\mu\) also shows why a vector gamma
  matrix reverses chirality.

  \SupplementarySolution{3}{Reality Conditions Modulo Eight}
  Complex-conjugating
  \(B\gamma^\mu B^{-1}=(\gamma^\mu)^*\) and substituting the original
  relation back gives
  \[
    (B^*B)\gamma^\mu(B^*B)^{-1}=\gamma^\mu .
  \]
  Irreducibility and Schur's lemma imply \(B^*B=\epsilon\mathbf1\).
  Rescaling \(B\) fixes \(\epsilon=\pm1\).

  A reality condition \(\psi^*=B\psi\) is consistent only if applying
  it twice returns \(\psi\):
  \[
    \psi=B^*\psi^*=B^*B\psi=\epsilon\psi.
  \]
  Thus \(\epsilon=+1\) permits a Majorana spinor.  When
  \(\epsilon=-1\), introduce an even number of spinors and an
  antisymmetric matrix \(\Omega\) with \(\Omega^2=-1\):
  \[
    (\psi^i)^*=B\,\Omega^i{}_j\psi^j.
  \]
  The two minus signs then cancel on the second conjugation.  A
  six-dimensional Weyl spinor is pseudoreal, so one such spinor cannot
  satisfy an ordinary Majorana condition; a symplectic Majorana-Weyl
  pair is the minimal real supercharge.

  \SupplementarySolution{4}{Symmetric Gamma-Matrix Bilinears}
  Reversing the order in an antisymmetrized product requires
  \(p(p-1)/2\) interchanges.  Applying
  \((\gamma^\mu)^T=-C\gamma^\mu C^{-1}\) to every factor gives
  \[
    \bigl(\gamma^{[\mu_1\cdots\mu_p]}C\bigr)^T
      =\eta_C(-1)^{p(p+1)/2}
        \gamma^{[\mu_1\cdots\mu_p]}C .
  \]
  A convention with the opposite sign in the defining charge
  conjugation relation changes the displayed exponent by \(p\), but
  not the method.

  Because \(\{Q^i_\alpha,Q^j_\beta\}\) is symmetric under simultaneous
  interchange \((\alpha,i)\leftrightarrow(\beta,j)\), a symmetric
  spinor matrix must multiply a charge symmetric in \(i,j\), whereas
  an antisymmetric spinor matrix must multiply a charge antisymmetric
  in \(i,j\).  For \(N=1\), only the symmetric spinor matrices survive.

  \SupplementarySolution{5}{Six-Dimensional Automorphism Group}
  Let \(\rho\) be the antilinear pseudoreality map on a Weyl spinor,
  with \(\rho^2=-1\).  A real structure on \(2p\) copies is
  \[
    {\cal R}=\rho\otimes\Omega,\qquad
    \Omega^T=-\Omega,\qquad \Omega^2=-1,
  \]
  so that \({\cal R}^2=+1\).  A unitary transformation \(U\) on the
  copy index preserves this condition precisely when
  \[
    U^T\Omega U=\Omega .
  \]
  The compact unitary group satisfying this equation is \(USp(2p)\).
  Supercharges of opposite chirality have separate symplectic forms,
  so the full automorphism group of six-dimensional \((p,q)\)
  supersymmetry is
  \[
    USp(2p)\times USp(2q).
  \]

  \SupplementarySolution{6}{Why Supercharges Are Spinors}
  Let an odd generator contain a component of helicity \(s>1/2\).
  Commuting it with rotations produces all components from \(-s\) to
  \(+s\).  Acting on a one-particle state therefore produces states
  whose helicities differ by \(s\).  Because the odd generators close
  on translations and act nontrivially on scattering states, repeated
  products generate progressively larger helicity differences unless
  \(s=1/2\).  Fermi nilpotence limits repeated use of one component,
  but Lorentz covariance supplies independent components, and
  multiparticle tensor products then propagate the excessive spins
  through scattering channels.

  The conclusion uses the Coleman--Mandula/Haag--Lopuszanski--Sohnius
  assumptions: a nontrivial analytic \(S\)-matrix, finitely many
  one-particle species below any fixed mass, and ordinary
  finite-particle asymptotic states.  Under these assumptions the only
  non-scalar odd extension compatible with Lorentz symmetry is a
  spinor supercharge.  Free theories or theories with infinitely many
  higher-spin states need not satisfy the hypotheses.

  \SupplementarySolution{7}{Null-Momentum Stabilizer}
  Choose
  \(k^\mu=(E,0,\ldots,0,E)\).  Its connected Lorentz stabilizer is
  \[
    ISO(d-2)=SO(d-2)\ltimes\mathbb R^{d-2}.
  \]
  The rotations act on the \(d-2\) transverse directions.  The
  remaining generators are null rotations: they change transverse
  basis vectors by multiples of \(k^\mu\) while leaving \(k^\mu\)
  itself fixed.

  Nontrivial unitary representations of the translation subgroup have
  a continuous orbit under \(SO(d-2)\), producing continuous-spin
  representations with infinitely many polarization states.  A
  finite-spin particle representation must therefore make these null
  translations act trivially.  Its physical polarizations transform
  only under
  \[
    \boxed{SO(d-2)}.
  \]
  In \(d=4\), this reduces to \(SO(2)\), whose label is helicity.

  \SupplementarySolution{8}{Binomial Multiplet Counting}
  Let \(n={\cal N}/4\).  A basis state is specified by the subset of the
  \(n\) creators that acts on the Clifford vacuum.  Choosing \(r\) of
  them gives
  \[
    d_r=\binom nr.
  \]
  Summing over all levels yields
  \[
    \sum_{r=0}^n\binom nr=2^n.
  \]
  Even \(r\) has the same statistics as the vacuum and odd \(r\) has
  the opposite statistics.  The binomial identities at \(x=\pm1\)
  give
  \[
    \sum_{r\ {\rm even}}\binom nr
      =\sum_{r\ {\rm odd}}\binom nr=2^{n-1}.
  \]
  Tensoring with a Clifford vacuum of dimension \(d_0\) multiplies all
  three counts by \(d_0\) and preserves the equality.

  \SupplementarySolution{9}{The Thirty-Two-Supercharge Bound}
  In a null frame the active supercharges provide
  \({\cal N}/4\) creation operators.  Their spinor weights change the
  chosen helicity in steps whose total range is \({\cal N}/8\).
  A multiplet that begins at \(+2\) and contains nothing below \(-2\)
  must satisfy
  \[
    2-\frac{{\cal N}}8\geq-2,
    \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
    {\cal N}\leq32.
  \]
  Moving the Clifford vacuum shifts every weight in the multiplet by
  the same amount; it does not reduce their total separation.
  Therefore a multiplet containing an ordinary graviton and respecting
  the spin-two ceiling cannot evade the bound by relabeling its
  vacuum.

  \SupplementarySolution{10}{Eleven-Dimensional State Count}
  A symmetric tensor of \(SO(9)\) has \(9\cdot10/2=45\) components.
  Removing its trace leaves
  \[
    45-1=44.
  \]
  A three-form has
  \[
    \binom93=84
  \]
  components.  The \(SO(9)\) spinor has dimension \(16\), so a
  vector-spinor begins with \(9\cdot16=144\) components.  Gamma trace
  maps it onto an unconstrained \(16\)-component spinor; subtracting
  that image leaves
  \[
    144-16=128.
  \]
  Thus the bosonic and fermionic polarization counts agree:
  \[
    44+84=128.
  \]

  \SupplementarySolution{11}{Branching from \(SO(9)\) to \(SO(8)\)}
  Split the nine-dimensional vector index as \(I=(i,9)\).  A traceless
  symmetric tensor decomposes as
  \[
    {\bf44}\longrightarrow{\bf35}_v\oplus{\bf8}_v\oplus{\bf1}.
  \]
  These are the ten-dimensional graviton, Kaluza--Klein vector, and
  scalar.  Splitting the indices of a three-form gives
  \[
    {\bf84}\longrightarrow{\bf56}_v\oplus{\bf28},
  \]
  corresponding to a three-form and a two-form.

  The \(SO(9)\) spinor branches as
  \({\bf16}\to{\bf8}_s\oplus{\bf8}_c\).  Performing the same split on
  the gamma-traceless vector-spinor gives
  \[
    {\bf128}\longrightarrow
      {\bf56}_s\oplus{\bf56}_c
      \oplus{\bf8}_s\oplus{\bf8}_c.
  \]
  These are two gravitini of opposite chirality and two dilatini of
  opposite chirality.  Together the branchings reproduce the complete
  type-IIA massless supergravity multiplet.

  \SupplementarySolution{12}{Type-IIB Polarization Balance}
  For the little group \(SO(8)\), the graviton has
  \(8\cdot9/2-1=35\) polarizations.  Each two-form has
  \(\binom82=28\), and a scalar has one.  A four-form has
  \(\binom84=70\) components; self-duality retains \(35\).  Hence
  \[
    n_B=35+2(28)+2+35=128.
  \]

  A gamma-traceless vector-spinor of fixed chirality has
  \(8\cdot8-8=56\) polarizations, and a Weyl spinor has \(8\).
  Therefore
  \[
    n_F=2(56)+2(8)=128.
  \]
  The four-form self-duality condition is essential: without it the
  bosonic count would exceed the fermionic one by \(35\).

  \SupplementarySolution{13}{Kaluza--Klein Momentum as Central Charge}
  Split the eleven-dimensional index as \(M=(\mu,10)\).  The momentum
  term in the eleven-dimensional algebra is
  \[
    \{Q,Q^T\}\supset i\Gamma^M C P_M
      =i\Gamma^\mu C P_\mu+i\Gamma^{10}C P_{10}.
  \]
  Ten-dimensional Lorentz transformations act only on \(\mu\).
  Therefore \(P_{10}\) has no ten-dimensional Lorentz index and is a
  scalar.  Translation invariance along the circle makes it commute
  with all ten-dimensional Poincare generators.

  Under the decomposition into two ten-dimensional Majorana-Weyl
  supercharges of opposite chirality, \(\Gamma^{10}\) is the chirality
  operator.  The second term is consequently the scalar central-charge
  entry of the type-IIA algebra.  The geometric origin also shows why
  this central charge is conserved: it is ordinary momentum in the
  higher-dimensional theory.

  \SupplementarySolution{14}{The Kaluza--Klein BPS Tower}
  Periodicity around a circle of radius \(R\) quantizes momentum:
  \[
    P_{10}=\frac nR,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
  \]
  A state massless in eleven dimensions obeys
  \(P_M P^M=0\), so from the ten-dimensional viewpoint
  \[
    m_n^2=P_{10}^2,\qquad
    \boxed{m_n=\frac{|n|}{R}}.
  \]
  Since \(Z=P_{10}\), this saturates \(m\geq|Z|\).

  Saturation makes sixteen of the thirty-two real supercharges null.
  The other sixteen form eight creation-annihilation pairs, giving
  \(2^8=256\) states.  At each nonzero \(n\), these transform as
  \({\bf44}\oplus{\bf84}\) bosons and \({\bf128}\) fermions of the
  massive little group \(SO(9)\).  The levels \(n\) and \(-n\) carry
  opposite central charge and are exchanged by charge conjugation.

  \SupplementarySolution{15}{IIA and IIB Chirality}
  In a representation adapted to ten dimensions, the
  eleven-dimensional gamma matrices consist of the ten
  \(\Gamma^\mu\) plus the ten-dimensional chirality matrix
  \(\Gamma^{10}=\Gamma_*\).  Projecting an eleven-dimensional Majorana
  spinor with
  \[
    Q_\pm=\frac12(1\pm\Gamma_*)Q
  \]
  gives two real sixteen-component ten-dimensional Weyl spinors.
  Because the two projectors have opposite eigenvalues, the result is
  the nonchiral type-IIA pair.

  Type IIB instead has two real Weyl supercharges of the same
  ten-dimensional chirality.  An ordinary circle reduction cannot
  change the algebraic fact that a single eleven-dimensional spinor
  decomposes into opposite chiralities.  Thus it yields IIA, not IIB.
  IIB is related after a further compactification by T-duality, which
  reverses one right-moving spacetime chirality.

  \SupplementarySolution{16}{The Emerging Eleventh Dimension}
  The first Kaluza--Klein excitation has mass \(m_{\rm KK}=1/R_{11}\).
  Equating it to the D0-brane mass gives
  \[
    \frac1{R_{11}}=\frac1{g_s\ell_s},
    \qquad\boxed{R_{11}=g_s\ell_s}.
  \]
  The \(n\)-D0 threshold states then have masses
  \(|n|/(g_s\ell_s)=|n|/R_{11}\), exactly the Kaluza--Klein tower.

  As \(g_s\to\infty\) at fixed string length, \(R_{11}\) grows and the
  spacing \(1/R_{11}\) collapses.  The tower that was heavy in weakly
  coupled ten-dimensional string theory becomes the continuum of
  momentum in a decompactifying eleventh dimension.

  \SupplementarySolution{17}{Reducing the Eleven-Dimensional Three-Form}
  Write the eleven-dimensional potential as
  \[
    A_{MNP}\longrightarrow
      A_{\mu\nu\rho},\qquad
      B_{\mu\nu}=A_{\mu\nu\,10}.
  \]
  These are the type-IIA Ramond--Ramond three-form and
  Neveu--Schwarz two-form.  Components with two circle indices vanish
  by antisymmetry.

  The same index split applies to the algebraic charges:
  \[
    Z_{MN}\longrightarrow Z_{\mu\nu},\ Z_{\mu\,10},
  \]
  \[
    Z_{M_1\cdots M_5}\longrightarrow
      Z_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_5},\
      Z_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_4\,10}.
  \]
  Together with \(P_{10}\), these become the scalar, one-, two-,
  four-, and five-form structures allowed by the IIA algebra, subject
  to the ten-dimensional chirality and Hodge-duality relations.

  \SupplementarySolution{18}{Nine-Dimensional Unification}
  A ten-dimensional Weyl spinor has sixteen real components.  After
  restriction to \(\operatorname{Spin}(1,8)\), the two
  ten-dimensional chiral spinor
  representations both become the same sixteen-component
  nine-dimensional Majorana representation: nine dimensions has no
  chirality operator independent of the gamma matrices.

  Thus both reductions contain two nine-dimensional Majorana
  supercharges.  A sign or interchange in the second supercharge,
  accompanied by an \(R\)-symmetry basis change among the scalar and
  vector charges, maps the reduced IIA algebra to the reduced IIB
  algebra.  T-duality realizes precisely this map: it exchanges circle
  momentum and winding and flips the chirality convention of one
  ten-dimensional supercharge.  The equality of the nine-dimensional
  algebras is therefore the representation-theoretic shadow of
  IIA/IIB T-duality.

  \SupplementarySolution{19}{Topological \(p\)-Brane Charge}
  On a spatial worldvolume \(\Sigma_p\), define
  \[
    Z^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_p}
      =T_p\int_{\Sigma_p}
        dX^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dX^{\mu_p}.
  \]
  Varying the embedding and using \(d^2=0\) gives
  \[
    \delta Z^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_p}
      =T_p\int_{\Sigma_p}d\!\left[
        \sum_{r=1}^p(-1)^{r-1}\delta X^{\mu_r}
        dX^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\widehat{dX^{\mu_r}}
        \cdots\wedge dX^{\mu_p}\right].
  \]
  Stokes' theorem turns this into an integral over
  \(\partial\Sigma_p\).  It therefore vanishes for a closed brane or
  for variations fixed at infinity.  The charge depends only on the
  homology or asymptotic winding class, not on local deformations.
  It transforms as a spacetime \(p\)-form and may appear in the
  supercharge anticommutator when the appropriate gamma-matrix
  bilinear is symmetric.

  \SupplementarySolution{20}{Completeness of the \(p\)-Form Basis}
  For a spinor space of dimension \(D_s\), the matrices
  \[
    {\bf1},\quad\gamma^\mu,\quad
    \gamma^{[\mu\nu]},\quad\ldots
  \]
  are mutually orthogonal under the trace pairing when their ranks
  differ.  Their formal total number is
  \[
    \sum_{p=0}^d\binom dp=2^d,
  \]
  which matches the matrix algebra of a Dirac spinor in even \(d\).
  In odd \(d\), rank \(p\) and rank \(d-p\) are Hodge-dual matrices, so
  only half are independent: \(2^{d-1}\), exactly the dimension of the
  matrix algebra of an irreducible \(2^{(d-1)/2}\)-component spinor.
  Hence the independent antisymmetrized products span every spinor
  matrix.

  Multiplication by the volume gamma matrix relates rank \(p\) to rank
  \(d-p\); on an irreducible chiral space one retains only one member
  of each dual pair.  Finally,
  \(\{Q_\alpha,Q_\beta\}\) is symmetric in \(\alpha,\beta\).
  Expanding it in the complete gamma basis therefore keeps exactly
  those \(\gamma^{[p]}C\) that are symmetric, with internal-index
  antisymmetry compensating when extended supersymmetry is present.
  No additional bosonic spinor structure is possible.

  \SupplementarySolution{21}{The \(528\) Components of the M-Algebra}
  A symmetric real \(32\times32\) matrix has
  \[
    \frac{32\cdot33}{2}=528
  \]
  independent entries.  In eleven dimensions the symmetric
  gamma-matrix bilinears have ranks \(p=1,2,5\).  Their component
  counts are
  \[
    \binom{11}{1}=11,\qquad
    \binom{11}{2}=55,\qquad
    \binom{11}{5}=462.
  \]
  Since
  \[
    11+55+462=528,
  \]
  they exhaust the symmetric anticommutator:
  \[
    \{Q,Q\}\sim
      \Gamma^M P_M+\Gamma^{MN}Z_{MN}
       +\Gamma^{M_1\cdots M_5}Z_{M_1\cdots M_5}.
  \]
  The two- and five-form terms are the M2- and M5-brane charges.

  \SupplementarySolution{22}{Hodge-Dual Charge Redundancy}
  Let \(\Gamma_*\) denote the normalized product of all \(d\) gamma
  matrices.  Reordering the product gives
  \[
    \gamma^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_p}
      =\frac{\sigma_{p,d}}{(d-p)!}
       \epsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_p\nu_1\cdots\nu_{d-p}}
       \gamma_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{d-p}}\Gamma_*,
  \]
  where \(\sigma_{p,d}\) is a convention-dependent phase and sign.
  In odd dimension \(\Gamma_*\) is proportional to the identity; on an
  even-dimensional Weyl spinor it is the fixed eigenvalue \(\pm1\).

  Consequently a coefficient multiplying a rank-\(p\) matrix can be
  rewritten as the Hodge dual coefficient multiplying rank \(d-p\).
  The two descriptions use the same spinor matrix and cannot be
  independent central extensions.  At middle degree in even
  dimension, the decomposition instead becomes self-dual plus
  anti-self-dual, and chirality selects the appropriate part.

  \SupplementarySolution{23}{Which Potential Couples to a Brane?}
  The worldvolume has coordinates
  \(\xi^a\), \(a=0,\ldots,p\).  Pulling back a spacetime
  \((p+1)\)-form gives the reparametrization-invariant coupling
  \[
    S_{\rm WZ}
      =q_p\int_{\Sigma_{p+1}}X^*A_{p+1}
      =\frac{q_p}{(p+1)!}\int d^{p+1}\xi\,
       \epsilon^{a_0\cdots a_p}
       A_{\mu_0\cdots\mu_p}
       \partial_{a_0}X^{\mu_0}\cdots
       \partial_{a_p}X^{\mu_p}.
  \]
  Under \(A_{p+1}\to A_{p+1}+d\Lambda_p\), its variation is a boundary
  term.  Thus the electric source for a \((p+1)\)-form potential is a
  brane with \(p\) spatial dimensions.

  On a time slice, the corresponding conserved topological quantity
  has \(p\) spatial spacetime indices.  It therefore enters the
  supersymmetry algebra as a \(p\)-form charge (with time components
  of the covariant charge related by the BPS rest-frame choice).

  \SupplementarySolution{24}{M2 and M5 BPS Projectors}
  For a static planar M2, the relevant rest-frame terms can be arranged
  as
  \[
    \{Q,Q\}\propto T_2\bigl(1\mp\Gamma^{012}\bigr).
  \]
  Because \((\Gamma^{012})^2=1\) in the physical convention, positivity
  gives the tension-charge bound, and saturation requires
  \[
    \Gamma^{012}\epsilon=\pm\epsilon .
  \]
  The matrix is traceless and has eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\) with
  equal multiplicity.  Thus \((1\pm\Gamma^{012})/2\) has rank \(16\).

  For an M5 along \(x^1,\ldots,x^5\), the same argument gives
  \[
    \Gamma^{012345}\epsilon=\pm\epsilon .
  \]
  This matrix also squares to one and is traceless on the
  thirty-two-component Majorana spinor, so its projector again has
  rank \(16\).  Each isolated planar M2 or M5 is therefore half-BPS.

  \SupplementarySolution{25}{Dp-Brane Worldvolume Degrees of Freedom}
  A massless gauge field in \(p+1\) dimensions has
  \[
    (p+1)-2=p-1
  \]
  physical polarizations when \(p\geq1\).  A Dp-brane also breaks
  translations in the \(9-p\) transverse spatial directions, producing
  one scalar for each broken translation.  Hence
  \[
    n_B=(p-1)+(9-p)=8.
  \]
  This is also the dimensional reduction of the eight physical
  polarizations of a ten-dimensional super-Yang--Mills vector.

  The formula \(D-2\) for a massless vector assumes \(D\geq2\), so it
  cannot be continued literally to \(p=0\).  On a worldline \(A_0\) is
  a Lagrange multiplier and the D0 transverse coordinates are quantum
  mechanical variables rather than propagating massless waves.  Their
  counting must instead be done in the constrained Hamiltonian system;
  it does not contradict the light-cone open-string count used for
  \(p\geq1\).

  \SupplementarySolution{26}{BPS Tension, Charge, and No Force}
  In the brane rest frame, positivity of the supercharge
  anticommutator gives an energy-density bound
  \[
    T\geq |q|.
  \]
  A supersymmetric brane has a nonzero spinor annihilated by the
  corresponding projector, so an eigenvalue of the anticommutator
  vanishes.  The inequality is saturated:
  \(T=|q|\), after choosing the common normalization of the
  gravitational and Ramond--Ramond kinetic terms.

  The long-range exchange between two parallel identical branes has
  two parts.  Graviton and dilaton exchange is attractive and
  proportional to \(T^2\); exchange of the Ramond--Ramond
  \((p+1)\)-form between like charges is repulsive and proportional to
  \(q^2\).  BPS equality makes the magnitudes equal, so the static force
  cancels.  Reversing one charge produces a brane--antibrane pair; the
  form-field force then becomes attractive and the cancellation is
  lost.

  \SupplementarySolution{27}{Even and Odd D-Branes}
  A Dp-brane preserves supersymmetry parameters satisfying a condition
  of the schematic form
  \[
    \epsilon_2=\Gamma^{0\cdots p}{\cal K}_p\epsilon_1,
  \]
  where \({\cal K}_p\) is the appropriate chirality/Pauli-matrix
  factor.  A product of \(p+1\) gamma matrices reverses
  ten-dimensional chirality when \(p+1\) is odd and preserves it when
  \(p+1\) is even.

  In IIA, \(\epsilon_1\) and \(\epsilon_2\) have opposite chirality, so
  the projector requires \(p+1\) odd, or \(p\) even.  In IIB they have
  the same chirality, so \(p+1\) must be even, or \(p\) odd.  Thus
  \[
    \text{IIA}:p=0,2,4,6,8,\qquad
    \text{IIB}:p=-1,1,3,5,7,9,
  \]
  subject to the usual interpretation of the instantonic and
  space-filling cases.

  \SupplementarySolution{28}{The Open-String Vector Multiplet}
  The Ramond ground state is a ten-dimensional spinor.  The GSO
  projection keeps one Majorana-Weyl chirality, with sixteen real
  components.  The massless Dirac equation and its associated
  light-cone constraint halve this to eight physical fermionic
  polarizations.

  The Neveu--Schwarz sector supplies the eight bosonic modes counted in
  Solution~25.  Hence the massless spectrum has
  \[
    8_B+8_F
  \]
  states.  It is the dimensional reduction to \(p+1\) dimensions of
  the ten-dimensional \(N=1\) super-Yang--Mills multiplet: a gauge
  field, \(9-p\) adjoint scalars, and their gaugino partners.  For one
  brane the gauge group is \(U(1)\); coincident branes promote it to a
  nonabelian \(U(N)\) multiplet.

  \SupplementarySolution{29}{T-Duality Changes Brane Dimension}
  T-duality interchanges
  \(\partial_\tau X\) and \(\partial_\sigma\widetilde X\).  Therefore a
  Neumann condition
  \(\partial_\sigma X=0\) becomes a Dirichlet condition
  \(\partial_\tau\widetilde X=0\), while a Dirichlet condition becomes
  Neumann.

  Dualizing a direction parallel to a Dp-brane converts one Neumann
  coordinate to Dirichlet.  The brane loses that worldvolume direction
  and becomes a D\((p-1)\)-brane.  Dualizing a transverse direction
  converts one Dirichlet coordinate to Neumann, so the brane gains a
  worldvolume direction and becomes a D\((p+1)\)-brane.  Because one
  spacetime chirality is reversed at the same time, this map exchanges
  the even-\(p\) IIA spectrum with the odd-\(p\) IIB spectrum.

  \SupplementarySolution{30}{Brane Threshold Bound}
  In the rest frame, write the central-charge contribution as a
  Hermitian matrix \({\cal Z}\) on the supercharge space.  A unitary
  transformation diagonalizes it, and positivity of
  \[
    \{Q,Q^\dagger\}\sim M{\bf1}-{\cal Z}
  \]
  requires
  \[
    M\geq\lambda_{\max}({\cal Z})
  \]
  together with the corresponding inequality for the opposite signs.
  Equivalently, \(M\) is no smaller than the largest absolute charge
  eigenvalue.  Saturation makes the associated eigenspace of
  supercharges annihilate the state and determines the preserved
  fraction.

  If compatible constituent projectors make the largest eigenvalue the
  sum of the separate charge magnitudes, then
  \(M_{\rm BPS}=\sum_i M_i\).  The constituents may separate at no
  energy cost: this is a threshold or marginal bound state.  For
  charges entering a combined eigenvalue such as
  \(\sqrt{q_1^2+q_2^2}\), the saturated mass is smaller than
  \(|q_1|+|q_2|\).  The positive difference is binding energy, and the
  configuration is a nonthreshold BPS bound state.
\end{enumerate}
