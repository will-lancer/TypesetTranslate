\chapterbackmatter{Solutions to Weinberg Exercises}
\ExerciseEditorialNote

\begin{enumerate}
  \WeinbergSolution{1}
  There is an important qualification hidden in the requested field
  content.  An ordinary (long) massive \(N=2\) multiplet cannot have this
  content: it has four independent rest-frame fermionic creation operators.
  The proposed multiplet can only be a half-BPS multiplet.  Write
  \[
    Z_{12}=2m u,\qquad |u|=1 .
  \]
  At the bound \(m=|Z_{12}|/2\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:25.5.20} says, on this
  irreducible multiplet,
  \[
    \WeylQ_{a1}=u\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_{b2}^{\dagger},
    \qquad
    \WeylQ_{a2}=-u\sum_b e_{ab}\WeylQ_{b1}^{\dagger}.
    \tag{1}
  \]
  Thus the second supersymmetry is not an additional independent oscillator.
  For parameters \(\epsilon_{ar}\), define
  \[
    \varepsilon_b\equiv
      \epsilon_{b1}-u\sum_a\epsilon_{a2}^{*}e_{ab}.
    \tag{2}
  \]
  Substitution of (1) in
  \(\sum_{ar}(\epsilon_{ar}^{*}\WeylQ_{ar}
  \epsilon_{ar}\WeylQ_{ar}^{\dagger})\) reduces it precisely to the
  simple-supersymmetry generator with parameter \(\varepsilon\).

  Let the two complex scalar fields be denoted by \(\phi\) and
  \(\mathcal F\), and form the spinor \(\zeta_a\) as in Section 26.1.
  Repeating the graded-Jacobi calculation there, now with (2), gives
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    \delta\phi
      &=-\ii\varepsilon_a e_{ab}\zeta_b,\\
    \delta\zeta_a
      &=-2\varepsilon_b^*(\sigma^\mu e)_{ba}\partial_\mu\phi
        -2\ii\varepsilon_a\mathcal F,\\
    \delta\mathcal F
      &=-\varepsilon_b^*\sigma^\mu_{ba}\partial_\mu\zeta_a .
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{3}
  \]
  (Repeated two-component indices are summed.)  In four-component notation,
  with \(\alpha\) formed from \(\varepsilon\), these are
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.1.21} after
  \[
    \phi=\frac{A+\ii B}{\sqrt2},\qquad
    \mathcal F=\frac{F-\ii G}{\sqrt2}.
  \]
  For a free massive multiplet the invariant combinations discussed after
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.1.22} may be set to zero, so
  \(\mathcal F=-m\phi^*\).  Hence \(\mathcal F\) is auxiliary rather than a
  second propagating complex scalar.  If ``two complex scalars'' is instead
  read as two independent propagating complex fields, no such multiplet
  exists: it fails both the rest-frame oscillator count and (1).

  \WeinbergSolution{2}
  Put \(x_T^\mu=(\Lambda_T)^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu=(-x^0,\mathbf x)\), and use the
  conventional Majorana time-reversal matrix
  \[
    B_T\equiv-\gamma_5\mathcal C=\epsilon,\qquad
    B_T^{-1}\gamma^{\mu *}B_T
       =(\Lambda_T)^\mu{}_\nu\gamma^\nu .
  \]
  An overall intrinsic time-reversal phase \(\eta_T\) is allowed.  With the
  harmless choice of phase in which
  \(T^{-1}S(x,\theta)T=\eta_T S(x_T,B_T\theta)\), comparison with
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.2.10} gives
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    C^T(x)&=\eta_T C(x_T),&
    \omega^T(x)&=\eta_T B_T\omega(x_T),\\
    M^T(x)&=-\eta_T M(x_T),&
    N^T(x)&=\eta_T N(x_T),\\
    V_T^\mu(x)&=-\eta_T(\Lambda_T)^\mu{}_\nu V^\nu(x_T),&
    \lambda^T(x)&=-\eta_T B_T\lambda(x_T),\\
    D^T(x)&=\eta_TD(x_T).&&
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{4}
  \]
  Reversing the convention for whether \(T\) or \(T^{-1}\) appears first
  replaces \(B_T\) by \(B_T^{-1}=-B_T\); that common sign is absorbed into
  \(\eta_T\).  The relative signs in (4) are invariant.

  Antiunitarity complex-conjugates \(i\), while \(B_T\) preserves the
  chirality of a massless spinor.  Applying it to
  \(\SuperD_R\Phi=0\) therefore gives
  \(\SuperD_R\Phi^T=0\): the time reverse of a left-chiral superfield is
  again left-chiral, not right-chiral.  Likewise
  \(\SuperD_R^2\mathcal J=\SuperD_L^2\mathcal J=0\) is mapped into itself,
  so the time reverse of a linear superfield is linear.

  \WeinbergSolution{3}
  Since \(d(\Phi)=1\), an \(\mathcal F\)-term raises dimension by one and a
  \(D\)-term raises it by two.  Up to spacetime and superspace integration
  by parts, complex conjugation, and terms whose \(D\)-component vanishes
  because they are chiral, the complete list is
  \[
  \begin{split}
    &c_1[\Phi^4]_{\mathcal F}+\mathrm{c.c.},\\
    &c_2[\Phi^2\Phi^*]_D+\mathrm{c.c.},\\
    &c_3[\Phi\Box\Phi]_{\mathcal F}+\mathrm{c.c.}.
  \end{split}
  \tag{5}
  \]
  The last line may equivalently be represented by a \(D\)-term containing
  two spinor superderivatives, for example
  \([(\overline{\SuperD}\Phi)(\SuperD\Phi)]_D\), with the chiral projections
  and normalization understood.  Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.3.31} and superspace
  integration by parts show that these are the same higher-derivative
  operator class.  Pure \([\Phi^3]_D\) and its conjugate vanish, while one
  ordinary derivative cannot make a Lorentz scalar.  Thus (5) contains no
  duplicates and exhausts dimension five.

  \WeinbergSolution{4}
  Write \(x=\phi_3\).  The three derivatives of the superpotential are
  \[
    f_1=x^2,\qquad f_2=x^2+a,\qquad
    f_3=2x(\phi_1+\phi_2).
  \]
  They cannot all vanish: \(f_1=0\) would imply \(x=0\), but then
  \(f_2=a\ne0\).  Supersymmetry is therefore broken.  The potential is
  \[
    V=|x|^4+|x^2+a|^2
      +4|x|^2|\phi_1+\phi_2|^2 .
  \]
  For fixed \(y=|x|^2\), the phase of \(x\) can be chosen so that
  \(x^2=-\operatorname{sgn}(a)y\).  Hence
  \[
    V\ge y^2+(y-|a|)^2
      =2\left(y-\frac{|a|}{2}\right)^2+\frac{a^2}{2}.
  \]
  Equality holds for
  \[
    x^2=-\frac a2,\qquad \phi_1+\phi_2=0,
    \qquad V_{\min}=\frac{a^2}{2}.
  \]
  The difference \(\phi_1-\phi_2\) is a classical flat direction.
  At a minimum,
  \(f_1=-a/2,\ f_2=a/2,\ f_3=0\).  The normalized zero mode of the
  fermion mass matrix, and therefore the goldstino, is
  \[
    \psi_G=\frac{\psi_2-\psi_1}{\sqrt2},
  \]
  up to an overall Majorana phase.  No \(\psi_3\) component occurs.

  \WeinbergSolution{5}
  It is useful first to separate a universal component calculation from the
  particular form of the symmetry.  Define the real moment-map function
  \[
    \mu(\phi,\phi^*)\equiv
      K_n(\phi,\phi^*)\,\mathcal T_{nm}\phi_m
      =K_{\bar n}(\phi,\phi^*)\,\mathcal T_{mn}\phi_m^*,
    \tag{6}
  \]
  and denote its ordinary derivatives by
  \(\mu_n,\mu_{\bar n},\mu_{nm},\ldots\).  Expanding
  \(\mathcal J=\mu(\Phi,\Phi^*)\) gives
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    C^{\mathcal J}&=\mu,\\
    \omega^{\mathcal J}
      &=-\ii\sqrt2\,\gamma_5
        \left(\mu_n\psi_{nL}+\mu_{\bar n}\psi_{nR}\right),\\
    A_L&\equiv
      \mu_n\mathcal F_n
      -\frac12\mu_{nm}(\bar\psi_n\psi_{mL}),\\
    A_R&\equiv
      \mu_{\bar n}\mathcal F_n^*
      -\frac12\mu_{\bar n\bar m}(\bar\psi_n\psi_{mR}),\\
    M^{\mathcal J}&=\ii(A_L-A_R),\qquad
    N^{\mathcal J}=-(A_L+A_R),\\
    V_\mu^{\mathcal J}
      &=-\ii\left(\mu_n\partial_\mu\phi_n
               -\mu_{\bar n}\partial_\mu\phi_n^*\right)
        +\ii\mu_{n\bar m}
          (\bar\psi_{mR}\gamma_\mu\psi_{nL}).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{7}
  \]
  All quantities on the right are evaluated at \((\phi,\phi^*)\).
  Formula (6) makes every \(\mu\)-derivative in (7) an explicit derivative
  of \(K\) followed by multiplication by
  \(\mathcal T_{nm}\phi_m\); no new function has been introduced.

  The auxiliaries in (7) are fixed by the component form of
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.6.15}--\eqref{eq:26.6.16}.  With
  \(\mathcal G_{n\bar m}=K_{n\bar m}\),
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal G_{n\bar m}\mathcal F_m^*
      &=-f_n+\frac12K_{n\bar m\bar\ell}
        (\bar\psi_m\psi_{\ell R}),\\
    \mathcal G_{m\bar n}\mathcal F_m
      &=-f_n^*+\frac12K_{m\ell\bar n}
        (\bar\psi_m\psi_{\ell L}).
  \end{aligned}
  \tag{8}
  \]
  Using (8), invariance
  \(f_n\mathcal T_{nm}\phi_m=0\), and its conjugate gives
  \(A_L=A_R=0\).  The remaining components consequently are
  \[
    M^{\mathcal J}=N^{\mathcal J}=0,\qquad
    \lambda^{\mathcal J}=-\sl{\partial}\omega^{\mathcal J},\qquad
    D^{\mathcal J}=-\Box C^{\mathcal J},
    \qquad \partial^\mu V_\mu^{\mathcal J}=0.
    \tag{9}
  \]
  Equations (6)--(9) give every component and display explicitly where both
  \(K\) and \(f\) enter.

  \WeinbergSolution{6}
  Denote the right-hand side of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:26.7.18} and
  \eqref{eq:26.7.10} by
  \[
    \mathscr S^\mu\equiv S^\mu+
      \frac{\sqrt2}{3}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]\,
      \partial_\nu\sum_n
      (\phi_n\psi_{nR}+\phi_n^*\psi_{nL}).
    \tag{10}
  \]
  Expanding Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.7.21} to first order in \(\theta\), reducing
  the spinor bilinears with the identities of the chapter appendix, and
  using
  \(\mathcal F_n^*=-f_n\) and the Dirac equations
  \eqref{eq:26.7.17}, gives
  \[
    \omega_\mu^\Theta
      =-\frac12\mathscr S_\mu
       +\frac16\gamma_\mu\gamma^\nu\mathscr S_\nu .
    \tag{11}
  \]
  This is the useful compact form of the otherwise lengthy
  \(\theta\)-linear coefficient.  It includes the derivative terms from the
  first two products in (26.7.21), the \(f_n\)-terms from the auxiliaries,
  and the improvement term from the superderivative product.

  Since \(\gamma_\mu\gamma^\mu=4\), Eq. (11) implies
  \[
    \gamma^\mu\omega_\mu^\Theta
      =\frac16\gamma^\mu\mathscr S_\mu .
  \]
  Therefore
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    -2\omega^{\Theta\mu}
      +2\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\omega_\nu^\Theta
    &=
      \mathscr S^\mu-\frac13\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\mathscr S_\nu
      \frac13\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\mathscr S_\nu\\
    &=\mathscr S^\mu,
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  which is exactly Eq.~\eqref{eq:26.7.20}.
\end{enumerate}
