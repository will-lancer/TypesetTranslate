We now have all the pieces needed to motivate the introduction of quantum
fields~\hyperref[ref:5.1]{[1]}. In the course of this construction, we shall encounter some of
the most remarkable and universal consequences of the union of relativity
with quantum mechanics: the connection between spin and statistics, the
existence of antiparticles, and various relationships between particles and
antiparticles, including the celebrated CPT theorem.

\subsection{Free Fields}

We have seen in Chapter 3 that the $S$-matrix will be Lorentz-invariant if
the interaction can be written as
\begin{equation}
  H_I(t)=\int \dd^3x\,\mathcal{H}_I(\mathbf{x},t),
  \tag{5.1.1}\label{eq:5.1.1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_I$ is a scalar, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
  U_0(\Lambda,a)\mathcal{H}_I(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,a)
  =\mathcal{H}_I(\Lambda x+a),
  \tag{5.1.2}\label{eq:5.1.2}
\end{equation}
and satisfies the additional condition:
\begin{equation}
  [\mathcal{H}_I(x),\mathcal{H}_I(x')]=0
  \quad\text{for}\quad (x-x')^2\geq0.
  \tag{5.1.3}\label{eq:5.1.3}
\end{equation}
As we shall see, there are more general possibilities, but none of them
are very different from this. (For the present we are leaving it as an open
question whether $\Lambda$ here is restricted to a proper orthochronous Lorentz
transformation, or can also include space inversions.) In order to facilitate
also satisfying the cluster decomposition principle we are going to construct
$\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ out of creation and annihilation operators, but here we face
a problem: as shown by Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.2.12}, under Lorentz transformations each
such operator is multiplied by a matrix that depends on the momentum
carried by that operator. How can we couple such operators together
to make a scalar? The solution is to build $\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ out of fields---both
annihilation fields $\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)$ and creation fields $\psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)$:
\begin{equation}
  \psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)=\sum_{\sigma n}\int \dd^3p\,
  u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n},
  \tag{5.1.4}\label{eq:5.1.4}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)=\sum_{\sigma n}\int \dd^3p\,
  v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n},
  \tag{5.1.5}\label{eq:5.1.5}
\end{equation}
with coefficients\footnote{A reminder: the labels $n$ and $\sigma$ run over all
different particle species and spin $z$-components, respectively.}
$u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ and $v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ chosen so that
under Lorentz transformations each field is multiplied with a
position-independent matrix:
\begin{equation}
  U_0(\Lambda,a)\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,a)
  =\sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda^{-1})
  \psi^{(+)}_{\bar\ell}(\Lambda x+a),
  \tag{5.1.6}\label{eq:5.1.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  U_0(\Lambda,a)\psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,a)
  =\sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda^{-1})
  \psi^{(-)}_{\bar\ell}(\Lambda x+a).
  \tag{5.1.7}\label{eq:5.1.7}
\end{equation}
(We might, in principle, have different transformation matrices $D^\pm$ for the
annihilation and creation fields, but as we shall see, it is always possible
to choose the fields so that these matrices are the same.) By applying a
second Lorentz transformation $\bar\Lambda$, we find that
\[
  D(\Lambda^{-1})D(\bar\Lambda^{-1})=D((\bar\Lambda\Lambda)^{-1}),
\]
so taking $\Lambda_1=(\Lambda)^{-1}$ and $\Lambda_2=(\bar\Lambda)^{-1}$,
we see that the $D$-matrices furnish a \textit{representation} of the homogeneous
Lorentz group:
\begin{equation}
  D(\Lambda_1)D(\Lambda_2)=D(\Lambda_1\Lambda_2).
  \tag{5.1.8}\label{eq:5.1.8}
\end{equation}
There are many such representations, including the scalar $D(\Lambda)=\mathbf{1}$,
the vector $D(\Lambda)^\mu{}_{\nu}=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$, and a host of tensor
and spinor representations. These particular representations are irreducible,
in the sense that it is not possible by a choice of basis to reduce all
$D(\Lambda)$ to the same block-diagonal form, with two or more blocks. However,
we do not require at this point that $D(\Lambda)$ be irreducible; in general it
is a block-diagonal matrix with an arbitrary array of irreducible representations
in the blocks. That is, the index $\ell$ here includes a label that runs over
the types of particle described and the irreducible representations in the
different blocks, as well as another that runs over the components of the
individual irreducible representations. Later we will separate these fields
into irreducible fields that each describe only a single particle species
(and its antiparticle) and transform irreducibly under the Lorentz group.

Once we have learned how to construct fields satisfying the Lorentz
transformation rules \eqref{eq:5.1.6} and \eqref{eq:5.1.7}, we will be able to construct the
interaction density as
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{H}_I(x)={}&\sum_{NM}\sum_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N}
  \sum_{\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}
  g_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N,\,\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\psi^{(-)}_{\ell'_1}(x)\cdots\psi^{(-)}_{\ell'_N}(x)
  \psi^{(+)}_{\ell_1}(x)\cdots\psi^{(+)}_{\ell_M}(x),
  \tag{5.1.9}\label{eq:5.1.9}
\end{align}
and this will be a scalar in the sense of Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.2} if the constant
coefficients $g_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N,\,\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}$ are chosen to be
Lorentz covariant, in the sense that for all $\Lambda$:
\begin{align}
  &\sum_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N}\sum_{\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}
  D_{\ell'_1\bar\ell'_1}(\Lambda^{-1})\cdots
  D_{\ell'_N\bar\ell'_N}(\Lambda^{-1})
  D_{\ell_1\bar\ell_1}(\Lambda^{-1})\cdots
  D_{\ell_M\bar\ell_M}(\Lambda^{-1})
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot g_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N,\,\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}
  =g_{\bar\ell'_1\cdots\bar\ell'_N,\,\bar\ell_1\cdots\bar\ell_M}.
  \tag{5.1.10}\label{eq:5.1.10}
\end{align}
(Note that we do not include derivatives here, because we regard the derivatives
of components of these fields as just additional sorts of field components.)
The task of finding coefficients $g_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N,\,\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}$
that satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.10} is no different in principle (and not much more
difficult in practice) than that of using Clebsch--Gordan coefficients to
couple together various representations of the three-dimensional rotation
group to form rotational scalars. Later we will be able to combine creation
and annihilation fields so that this density also commutes with itself at
space-like separations.

Now, what shall we take as the coefficient functions
$u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ and $v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$?
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.2.12} and its adjoint give the transformation rules\footnote{This is for
massive particles. The case of zero mass will be taken up in Section 5.9.}
for the annihilation and creation operators
\begin{align}
  &U_0(\Lambda,b)a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n}U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,b)
  =\exp\bigl(\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  \sqrt{\frac{(\Lambda p)^0}{p^0}}
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\sum_{\bar\sigma}
  D^{(j_n)}_{\sigma\bar\sigma}\bigl(W^{-1}(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  a_{\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n},
  \tag{5.1.11}\label{eq:5.1.11}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  &U_0(\Lambda,b)a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n}U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,b)
  =\exp\bigl(-\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  \sqrt{\frac{(\Lambda p)^0}{p^0}}
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\sum_{\bar\sigma}
  D^{(j_n)*}_{\sigma\bar\sigma}\bigl(W^{-1}(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n},
  \tag{5.1.12}\label{eq:5.1.12}
\end{align}
where $j_n$ is the spin of particles of species $n$, and $\mathbf{p}_\Lambda$
is the three-vector part of $\Lambda p$. (We have used the unitarity of the
rotation matrices $D^{(j_n)}$ to put both Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.11} and \eqref{eq:5.1.12} in the
form shown here.) Also, as we saw in Section 2.5 the volume element
$\dd^3p/p^0$ is Lorentz-invariant, so we can replace $\dd^3p$ in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.4}
and \eqref{eq:5.1.5} with $\dd^3(\Lambda p)p^0/(\Lambda p)^0$. Putting this all together,
we find
\begin{align*}
  &U_0(\Lambda,b)\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,b)
  =\sum_{\sigma\bar\sigma n}\int \dd^3(\Lambda p)\,
  u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)
  \\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\bigl(\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  D^{(j_n)}_{\sigma\bar\sigma}\bigl(W^{-1}(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  \sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  a_{\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n}
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
  &U_0(\Lambda,b)\psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)U_0^{-1}(\Lambda,b)
  =\sum_{\sigma\bar\sigma n}\int \dd^3(\Lambda p)\,
  v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)
  \\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\bigl(-\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  D^{(j_n)*}_{\sigma\bar\sigma}\bigl(W^{-1}(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  \sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n}.
\end{align*}
We see that in order for the fields to satisfy the Lorentz transformation
rules \eqref{eq:5.1.6} and \eqref{eq:5.1.7}, it is necessary and sufficient that
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda^{-1})
  u_{\bar\ell}(\Lambda x+b;\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\sigma,n)
  ={}&\sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}D^{(j_n)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
  \bigl(W^{-1}(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  \\
  &\cdot\exp\bigl(+\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\bar\sigma,n)
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda^{-1})
  v_{\bar\ell}(\Lambda x+b;\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\sigma,n)
  ={}&\sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}D^{(j_n)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
  \bigl(W^{-1}(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  \\
  &\cdot\exp\bigl(-\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\bar\sigma,n),
\end{align*}
or somewhat more conveniently
\begin{align}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}u_\ell(\Lambda x+b;\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n)
  D^{(j_n)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}\bigl(W(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  ={}&\sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  \notag\\[-2pt]
  &\cdot\sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda)
  \exp\bigl(\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  u_{\bar\ell}(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n),
  \tag{5.1.13}\label{eq:5.1.13}
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}v_\ell(\Lambda x+b;\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n)
  D^{(j_n)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}\bigl(W(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  ={}&\sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  \notag\\[-2pt]
  &\cdot\sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda)
  \exp\bigl(-\ii(\Lambda p)\cdot b\bigr)
  v_{\bar\ell}(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n).
  \tag{5.1.14}\label{eq:5.1.14}
\end{align}
These are the fundamental requirements that will allow us to calculate the
$u_\ell$ and $v_\ell$ coefficient functions in terms of a finite number of
free parameters.

\subsubsection*{Translations}

We will use Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.13} and \eqref{eq:5.1.14} in three steps, considering in turn
three different types of proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation.
First we consider Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.13} and \eqref{eq:5.1.14} with $\Lambda=\mathbf{1}$ and
$b$ arbitrary. We see immediately that $u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ and
$v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ must take the form
\begin{equation}
  u_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}e^{\ii p\cdot x}
  u_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n),
  \tag{5.1.15}\label{eq:5.1.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  v_\ell(x;\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}e^{-\ii p\cdot x}
  v_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n),
  \tag{5.1.16}\label{eq:5.1.16}
\end{equation}
so the fields are Fourier transforms:
\begin{equation}
  \psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)=\sum_{\sigma,n}(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int \dd^3p\,
  u_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)e^{\ii p\cdot x}a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n},
  \tag{5.1.17}\label{eq:5.1.17}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)=\sum_{\sigma,n}(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int \dd^3p\,
  v_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)e^{-\ii p\cdot x}a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n}.
  \tag{5.1.18}\label{eq:5.1.18}
\end{equation}
(The factors $(2\pi)^{-3/2}$ could be absorbed into the definition of
$u_\ell$ and $v_\ell$, but it is conventional to show them explicitly in
these Fourier integrals.) Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.15} and \eqref{eq:5.1.16}, we see that
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.13} and \eqref{eq:5.1.14} are satisfied if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}u_\ell(\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n)
  D^{(j_n)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}\bigl(W(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  =\sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  \sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda)
  u_{\bar\ell}(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)
  \tag{5.1.19}\label{eq:5.1.19}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}v_\ell(\mathbf{p}_\Lambda,\bar\sigma,n)
  D^{(j_n)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}\bigl(W(\Lambda,p)\bigr)
  =\sqrt{\frac{p^0}{(\Lambda p)^0}}
  \sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(\Lambda)
  v_{\bar\ell}(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)
  \tag{5.1.20}\label{eq:5.1.20}
\end{equation}
for arbitrary homogeneous Lorentz transformations $\Lambda$.

\subsubsection*{Boosts}

Next take $\mathbf{p}=0$ in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.19} and \eqref{eq:5.1.20}, and let $\Lambda$
be the standard boost $L(q)$ that takes a particle of mass $m$ from rest to
some four-momentum $q^\mu$. Then $L(p)=\mathbf{1}$, and
\[
  W(\Lambda,p)\equiv L^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda L(p)
  =L^{-1}(q)L(q)=\mathbf{1}.
\]
Hence in this special case, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.19} and \eqref{eq:5.1.20} give
\begin{equation}
  u_\ell(\mathbf{q},\sigma,n)=(m/q^0)^{1/2}
  \sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(L(q))u_{\bar\ell}(0,\sigma,n)
  \tag{5.1.21}\label{eq:5.1.21}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  v_\ell(\mathbf{q},\sigma,n)=(m/q^0)^{1/2}
  \sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(L(q))v_{\bar\ell}(0,\sigma,n).
  \tag{5.1.22}\label{eq:5.1.22}
\end{equation}
In other words, if we know the quantities $u_\ell(0,\sigma,n)$ and
$v_\ell(0,\sigma,n)$ for zero momentum, then for a given representation
$D(\Lambda)$ of the homogeneous Lorentz group, we know the functions
$u_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ and $v_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ for all
$\mathbf{p}$. (Explicit formulas for the matrices $D_{\bar\ell\ell}(L(q))$
will be given for arbitrary representations of the homogeneous Lorentz group
in Section 5.7.)

\subsubsection*{Rotations}

Next, take $\mathbf{p}=0$, but this time let $\Lambda$ be a Lorentz
transformation with $\mathbf{p}_\Lambda=0$; that is, take $\Lambda$ as a
rotation $R$. Here obviously $W(\Lambda,p)=R$, and so Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.19} and
\eqref{eq:5.1.20} read
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}u_\ell(0,\bar\sigma,n)D^{(j_n)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}(R)
  =\sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(R)u_{\bar\ell}(0,\sigma,n)
  \tag{5.1.23}\label{eq:5.1.23}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}v_\ell(0,\bar\sigma,n)D^{(j_n)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}(R)
  =\sum_{\bar\ell}D_{\ell\bar\ell}(R)v_{\bar\ell}(0,\sigma,n),
  \tag{5.1.24}\label{eq:5.1.24}
\end{equation}
or equivalently
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}u_\ell(0,\bar\sigma,n)J^{(j_n)}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
  =\sum_{\bar\ell}\mathcal{J}_{\ell\bar\ell}u_{\bar\ell}(0,\sigma,n)
  \tag{5.1.25}\label{eq:5.1.25}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\bar\sigma}v_\ell(0,\bar\sigma,n)J^{(j_n)*}_{\bar\sigma\sigma}
  =-\sum_{\bar\ell}\mathcal{J}_{\ell\bar\ell}v_{\bar\ell}(0,\sigma,n),
  \tag{5.1.26}\label{eq:5.1.26}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}^{(j)}$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}$ are the angular-momentum
matrices in the representations $D^{(j)}(R)$ and $D(R)$, respectively. Any
representation $D(\Lambda)$ of the homogeneous Lorentz group obviously yields
a representation of the rotation group when $\Lambda$ is restricted to
rotations $R$; Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.25} and \eqref{eq:5.1.26} tell us that if the field
$\psi^{(\pm)}_\ell(x)$ is to describe particles of some particular spin $j$,
then this representation $D(R)$ must contain among its irreducible components
the spin-$j$ representation $D^{(j)}(R)$, with the coefficients
$u_\ell(0,\sigma,n)$ and $v_\ell(0,\sigma,n)$ simply describing how the spin-$j$
representation of the rotation group is embedded in $D(R)$. We shall see in
Section 5.5 that each irreducible representation of the proper orthochronous
Lorentz group contains any given irreducible representation of the rotation
group at most once, so that if the fields $\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)$ and
$\psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)$ transform irreducibly, then they are unique up to overall
scale. More generally, the number of free parameters in the annihilation or
creation fields (including their overall scales) is equal to the number of
irreducible representations in the field.

It is straightforward to show that coefficient functions
$u_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ and $v_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)$ given by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.21} and \eqref{eq:5.1.22}, with $u_\ell(0,\sigma,n)$ and
$v_\ell(0,\sigma,n)$ satisfying Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.23} and \eqref{eq:5.1.24}, will automatically
satisfy the more general requirements \eqref{eq:5.1.19} and \eqref{eq:5.1.20}. This is left as
an exercise for the reader.

Let us now return to the cluster decomposition principle. Inserting
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.17} and \eqref{eq:5.1.18} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.9} and integrating over $\mathbf{x}$,
the interaction Hamiltonian is
\begin{align}
  H_{\mathrm{int}}={}&\sum_{NM}\int \dd^3p'_1\cdots \dd^3p'_N\,
  \dd^3p_1\cdots \dd^3p_M
  \sum_{\sigma'_1\cdots\sigma'_N}\sum_{\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_M}
  \sum_{n'_1\cdots n'_N}\sum_{n_1\cdots n_M}
  \notag\\
  &\cdot a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}'_1,\sigma'_1,n'_1}\cdots
  a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}'_N,\sigma'_N,n'_N}
  a_{\mathbf{p}_M,\sigma_M,n_M}\cdots a_{\mathbf{p}_1,\sigma_1,n_1}
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\mathcal{V}_{NM}(\mathbf{p}'_1\sigma'_1n'_1\cdots
  \mathbf{p}'_N\sigma'_Nn'_N,\,\mathbf{p}_1\sigma_1n_1\cdots
  \mathbf{p}_M\sigma_Mn_M)
  \tag{5.1.27}\label{eq:5.1.27}
\end{align}
with coefficient functions given by
\begin{align}
  &\mathcal{V}_{NM}(\mathbf{p}'_1\sigma'_1n'_1\cdots,
  \mathbf{p}_1\sigma_1n_1\cdots)
  =\delta^3(\mathbf{p}'_1+\cdots-\mathbf{p}_1-\cdots)
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{NM}
  (\mathbf{p}'_1\sigma'_1n'_1\cdots,\mathbf{p}_1\sigma_1n_1\cdots),
  \tag{5.1.28}\label{eq:5.1.28}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  &\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{NM}(\mathbf{p}'_1\sigma'_1n'_1\cdots
  \mathbf{p}'_N\sigma'_Nn'_N,\,\mathbf{p}_1\sigma_1n_1\cdots
  \mathbf{p}_M\sigma_Mn_M)
  =(2\pi)^{3-3N/2-3M/2}
  \notag\\
  &\quad\cdot\sum_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N}
  \sum_{\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}
  g_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N,\,\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}
  v_{\ell'_1}(\mathbf{p}'_1,\sigma'_1,n'_1)\cdots
  v_{\ell'_N}(\mathbf{p}'_N,\sigma'_N,n'_N)
  \notag\\
  &\quad\cdot u_{\ell_1}(\mathbf{p}_1,\sigma_1,n_1)\cdots
  u_{\ell_M}(\mathbf{p}_M,\sigma_M,n_M).
  \tag{5.1.29}\label{eq:5.1.29}
\end{align}
This interaction is manifestly of the form that will guarantee that the
$S$-matrix satisfies the cluster decomposition principle: $\mathcal{V}_{NM}$
has a single delta function factor, with a coefficient
$\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{NM}$ that (at least for a finite number of field types)
has at most branch point singularities at zero particle momenta. In fact, we
could turn this argument around; any operator can be written as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.27},
and the cluster decomposition principle requires that the coefficient
$\mathcal{V}_{NM}$ may be written as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.28} as the product of a single
momentum-conservation delta function times a smooth coefficient function.
Any sufficiently smooth function (but not one containing additional delta
functions) can be expressed as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.29}.\footnote{For general functions
the indices $\ell$ and $\ell'$ may have to run over an infinite range. The
reasons for restricting $\ell$ and $\ell'$ to a finite range have to do with
the principle of renormalizability, discussed in Chapter 12.}
\textit{The cluster decomposition principle together with Lorentz invariance
thus makes it natural that the interaction density should be constructed out
of the annihilation and creation fields.}

If all we needed were to construct a scalar interaction density that
satisfied the cluster decomposition principle, then we could combine
annihilation and creation operators in arbitrary polynomials \eqref{eq:5.1.9}, with
coupling coefficients $g_{\ell'_1\cdots\ell'_N,\,\ell_1\cdots\ell_M}$ subject
only to the invariance condition \eqref{eq:5.1.10} (and a suitable reality condition).
However, for the Lorentz invariance of the $S$-matrix it is necessary also
that the interaction density satisfy the commutation condition \eqref{eq:5.1.3}.
This condition is not satisfied for arbitrary functions of the creation and
annihilation fields because
\begin{equation}
  [\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x),\psi^{(-)}_{\ell'}(y)]_\mp
  =(2\pi)^{-3}\sum_{\sigma n}\int \dd^3p\,
  u_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)v_{\ell'}(\mathbf{p},\sigma,n)
  e^{\ii p\cdot(x-y)}
  \tag{5.1.30}\label{eq:5.1.30}
\end{equation}
(with the sign $\mp$ indicating a commutator or anticommutator if the particles
destroyed and created by the components $\psi^{(+)}_\ell$ and
$\psi^{(-)}_{\ell'}$ are bosons or fermions, respectively,) and in general this
does not vanish even for $x-y$ space-like. It is obviously not possible to
avoid this problem by making the interaction density out of creation or
annihilation fields alone, for then the interaction could not be Hermitian.
The only way out of this difficulty is to combine annihilation and creation
fields in linear combinations:
\begin{equation}
  \psi_\ell(x)\equiv\kappa\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)
  +\lambda\psi^{(-)}_\ell(x),
  \tag{5.1.31}\label{eq:5.1.31}
\end{equation}
with the constants $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ and any other arbitrary constants
in the fields adjusted so that for $x-y$ space-like
\begin{equation}
  [\psi_\ell(x),\psi_{\ell'}(y)]_\mp
  =[\psi_\ell(x),\psi^\dagger_{\ell'}(y)]_\mp=0.
  \tag{5.1.32}\label{eq:5.1.32}
\end{equation}
We will see in subsequent sections of this chapter how to do this for various
irreducibly transforming fields. (By including explicit constants $\kappa$ and
$\lambda$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.31} we are leaving ourselves free to choose the overall
scale of the annihilation and creation fields in any way that seems convenient.)
The Hamiltonian density $\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ will satisfy the commutation
condition \eqref{eq:5.1.3} if it is constructed out of such fields and their adjoints,
with an even number of any field components that destroy and create fermions.

The condition \eqref{eq:5.1.32} is often described as a \textit{causality} condition,
because if $x-y$ is space-like then no signal can reach $y$ from $x$, so that a
measurement of $\psi_\ell$ at point $x$ should not be able to interfere with a
measurement of $\psi_{\ell'}$ or $\psi^\dagger_{\ell'}$ at point $y$. Such
considerations of causality are plausible for the electromagnetic field, any
one of whose components may be measured at a given spacetime point, as shown
in a classic paper of Bohr and Rosenfeld~\hyperref[ref:5.2]{[2]}. However, we will be dealing here
with fields like the Dirac field of the electron that do not seem in any sense
measurable. The point of view taken here is that Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.32} is needed for the
Lorentz invariance of the $S$-matrix, without any ancillary assumptions about
measurability or causality.

There is an obstacle to the construction of fields \eqref{eq:5.1.31} satisfying
\eqref{eq:5.1.32}. It may be that the particles that are destroyed and created by
these fields carry non-zero values of one or more conserved quantum numbers
like the electric charge. For instance, if particles of species $n$ carry a
value $q(n)$ for the electric charge $Q$, then
\[
  [Q,a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n}]=-q(n)a_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n},
\]
\[
  [Q,a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n}]=+q(n)a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p},\sigma,n}.
\]
In order that $\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ should commute with the charge operator $Q$
(or some other symmetry operator) it is necessary that it be formed out of
fields that have simple commutation relations with $Q$:
\begin{equation}
  [Q,\psi_\ell(x)]=-q_\ell\psi_\ell(x)
  \tag{5.1.33}\label{eq:5.1.33}
\end{equation}
for then we can make $\mathcal{H}_I(x)$ commute with $Q$ by constructing it as
a sum of products of fields $\psi_{\ell_1},\psi_{\ell_2},\ldots$ and adjoints
$\psi^\dagger_{m_1},\psi^\dagger_{m_2},\ldots$ such that
\[
  q_{\ell_1}+q_{\ell_2}+\cdots-q_{m_1}-q_{m_2}-\cdots=0.
\]
Now, Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.33} is satisfied for one particular component
$\psi^{(+)}_\ell(x)$ of the annihilation field if and only if all particle
species $n$ that are destroyed by the field carry the same charge
$q(n)=q_\ell$, and it is satisfied for one particular component
$\psi^{(-)}_\ell(x)$ of the creation field if and only if all particle species
$\bar n$ that are created by the field carry the charge $q(\bar n)=-q_\ell$.
We see that in order for such a theory to conserve quantum numbers like
electric charge, there must be a doubling of particle species carrying
non-zero values of such quantum numbers: if a particular component of the
annihilation field destroys a particle of species $n$, then the same component
of the creation field must create particles of a species $\bar n$, known as the
\textit{antiparticles} of the particles of species $n$, which have opposite
values of all conserved quantum numbers. \textit{This is the reason for
antiparticles.}

If the representation $D(\Lambda)$ is not irreducible, then we can adopt a
basis for the fields in which $D(\Lambda)$ breaks up into blocks along the main
diagonal, such that fields that belong to different blocks do not transform
into each other under Lorentz transformations. Also, Lorentz transformations
have no effect on the particle species. Therefore, instead of considering one
big field, including many irreducible components and many particle species,
we shall from now on restrict our attention to fields that destroy only a
single type of particle (dropping the label $n$) and create only the
corresponding antiparticle, and that transform irreducibly under the Lorentz
group (which as mentioned above may or may not be supposed to include space
inversion), with the understanding that, in general, we shall have to consider
many different such fields, some perhaps formed as the derivatives of other
fields. In the following sections we are going to finish the determination of
the coefficient functions $u_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma)$ and
$v_\ell(\mathbf{p},\sigma)$, fix the relative values of the constants $\kappa$
and $\lambda$, and deduce the relations between the properties of particles
and antiparticles, first for fields that belong to the simplest irreducible
representations of the Lorentz group, the scalar, vector, and Dirac spinor
representations. After that, we will repeat the analysis for a completely
general irreducible representation.

A word about field equations. Inspection of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:5.1.31}, \eqref{eq:5.1.17}, and
\eqref{eq:5.1.18} shows that all the components of a field of definite mass $m$ satisfy
the Klein--Gordon equation:
\begin{equation}
  (\Box-m^2)\psi_\ell(x)=0.
  \tag{5.1.34}\label{eq:5.1.34}
\end{equation}
Some fields satisfy other field equations as well, depending on whether or not
there are more field components than independent particle states.
Traditionally in quantum field theory one begins with such field equations,
or with the Lagrangian from which they are derived, and then uses them to
derive the expansion of the fields in terms of one-particle annihilation and
creation operators. In the approach followed here, we start with the particles,
and derive the fields according to the dictates of Lorentz invariance, with the
field equations arising almost incidentally as a byproduct of this construction.

A technicality must be mentioned here. According to the theorem proved in
Section 4.4, the condition that guarantees that a theory will satisfy the
cluster decomposition principle is that the interaction can be expressed as a
sum of products of creation and annihilation operators, with all creation
operators to the left of all annihilation operators, and with coefficients
that contain only a single momentum-conservation delta function. For this
reason, we should write the interaction in the `normal ordered' form
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{int}}=\int \dd^3x:\!\mathcal{H}_I
  (\psi(x),\psi^\dagger(x))\!:
  \tag{5.1.35}\label{eq:5.1.35}
\end{equation}
the colons indicating that the enclosed expression is to be rewritten
(ignoring non-vanishing commutators or anticommutators, but including minus
signs for permutations of fermionic operators) so that all creation operators
stand to the left of all annihilation operators. By using the commutation or
anticommutation relations of the fields, any such normal-ordered function of
the fields can just as well be written as a sum of ordinary products of the
fields with c-number coefficients. Rewriting
$:\!\mathcal{H}_I\!:$ in this way makes it obvious that despite the normal
ordering, $:\!\mathcal{H}_I(\psi(x),\psi^\dagger(x))\!:$ will commute with
$:\!\mathcal{H}_I(\psi(y),\psi^\dagger(y))\!:$ when $x-y$ is space-like,
if it is constructed out of fields that satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:5.1.32}, with even numbers
of any fermionic field components.
