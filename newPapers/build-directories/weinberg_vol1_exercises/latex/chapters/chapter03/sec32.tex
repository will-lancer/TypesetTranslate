\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The $S$-Matrix}{The S-Matrix}}

An experimentalist generally prepares a state to have a definite particle
content at $t\to-\infty$, and then measures what this state looks like at
$t\to+\infty$. If the state is prepared to have a particle content $\alpha$
for $t\to-\infty$, then it is the `in' state
$\InKet{\alpha}$, and if it is found to have the particle content
$\beta$ at $t\to+\infty$, then it is the `out' state
$\OutKet{\beta}$. The probability amplitude for the transition
$\alpha\to\beta$ is thus the scalar product
\begin{equation}
S_{\beta\alpha}=\OutBra{\beta}\InKet{\alpha}.
\tag{3.2.1}\label{eq:3.2.1}
\end{equation}
This array of complex amplitudes is known as the $S$-matrix~\hyperref[ref:3.2]{[2]}. If there
were no interactions then `in' and `out' states would be the same, and then
$S_{\beta\alpha}$ would be just $\delta(\alpha-\beta)$. The rate for a
reaction $\alpha\to\beta$ is thus proportional to
$|S_{\beta\alpha}-\delta(\alpha-\beta)|^2$. We shall see in detail in
Section~3.4 what $S_{\beta\alpha}$ has to do with measured rates and
cross-sections.

Perhaps it should be stressed that `in' and `out' states do not inhabit two
different Hilbert spaces. They differ only in how they are labelled: by their
appearance either at $t\to-\infty$ or $t\to+\infty$. Any `in' state can be
expanded as a sum of `out' states, with expansion coefficients given by the
$S$-matrix \eqref{eq:3.2.1}.

Since $S_{\beta\alpha}$ is the matrix connecting two sets of orthonormal
states, it must be unitary. To see this in greater detail, apply the
completeness relation \eqref{eq:3.1.5} to the `out' states, and write
\[
\int d\beta\,S^*_{\beta\gamma}S_{\beta\alpha}
=\int d\beta\,
\InBra{\gamma}\OutKet{\beta}
\;
\OutBra{\beta}\InKet{\alpha}
=\InBra{\gamma}\InKet{\alpha}.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:3.1.15}, this gives
\begin{equation}
\int d\beta\,S^*_{\beta\gamma}S_{\beta\alpha}
=\delta(\gamma-\alpha),
\tag{3.2.2}\label{eq:3.2.2}
\end{equation}
or in brief, $S^\dagger S=\mathbf{1}$. In the same way, completeness for the
`in' states gives\footnote{An alternative proof is given at the end of this
section. Note that for infinite `matrices,' the unitarity conditions
$S^\dagger S=\mathbf{1}$ and $SS^\dagger=\mathbf{1}$ are not equivalent.}
\begin{equation}
\int d\beta\,S_{\gamma\beta}S^*_{\alpha\beta}
=\delta(\gamma-\alpha),
\tag{3.2.3}\label{eq:3.2.3}
\end{equation}
or, in other words, $SS^\dagger=\mathbf{1}$.

It is often convenient instead of dealing with the $S$-matrix to work with
an operator $S$, defined to have matrix elements between \textit{free-particle}
states equal to the corresponding elements of the $S$-matrix:
\begin{equation}
\bra{\beta}S\ket{\alpha}\equiv S_{\beta\alpha}.
\tag{3.2.4}\label{eq:3.2.4}
\end{equation}
The explicit though highly formal expression \eqref{eq:3.1.13} for the `in' and `out'
states yields a formula for the $S$-operator:
\begin{equation}
S=\Omega(+\infty)^\dagger\Omega(-\infty)=U(+\infty,-\infty),
\tag{3.2.5}\label{eq:3.2.5}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
U(\tau,\tau_0)\equiv\Omega(\tau)^\dagger\Omega(\tau_0)
=\exp(iH_0\tau)\exp\bigl(-iH(\tau-\tau_0)\bigr)
\exp(-iH_0\tau_0).
\tag{3.2.6}\label{eq:3.2.6}
\end{equation}
This will be used in the next section to examine the Lorentz invariance of the
$S$-matrix, and in Sec.~3.5 to derive a formula for the $S$-matrix in
time-dependent perturbation theory.

The methods of the previous section can be used to derive a useful alternative
formula for the $S$-matrix. Let's return to Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.1.21} for the `in' state
$\InKet{\Psi_g}$, but this time take $t\to+\infty$. We must now
close the contour of integration for $E_\alpha$ in the \textit{lower}
half-$E_\alpha$-plane, and although as before the singularities in
$T^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta\alpha}$ and $g(\alpha)$ make no contribution for
$t\to+\infty$, we now do pick up a contribution from the singular factor
$(E_\alpha-E_\beta+i\epsilon)^{-1}$. The contour runs from
$E_\alpha=-\infty$ to $E_\alpha=+\infty$, and then back to
$E_\alpha=-\infty$ on a large semi-circle in the lower half-plane, so it
circles the singularity in a \textit{clockwise} direction. By the method of
residues, this contribution to the integral over $E_\alpha$ is given by the
value of the integrand at $E_\alpha=E_\beta-i\epsilon$, times a factor
$-2i\pi$. That is, in the limit $\epsilon\to0+$, for $t\to+\infty$ the
integral over $\alpha$ in \eqref{eq:3.1.21} has the asymptotic behavior
\[
\mathcal I^{\mathrm{in}}_\beta\longrightarrow
-2i\pi e^{-iE_\beta t}
\int d\alpha\,\delta(E_\alpha-E_\beta)g(\alpha)
T^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta\alpha},
\]
and hence, for $t\to+\infty$,
\[
\InKet{\Psi_g(t)}\longrightarrow
\int d\beta\,e^{-iE_\beta t}\ket{\beta}
\left[g(\beta)-2i\pi\int d\alpha\,
\delta(E_\alpha-E_\beta)g(\alpha)
T^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta\alpha}\right].
\]
But expanding \eqref{eq:3.1.19} for $\InKet{\Psi_g}$ in a complete set of
`out' states gives
\[
\InKet{\Psi_g(t)}
=\int d\alpha\,e^{-iE_\alpha t}g(\alpha)
\int d\beta\,\OutKet{\beta}S_{\beta\alpha}.
\]
Since $S_{\beta\alpha}$ contains a factor $\delta(E_\beta-E_\alpha)$, this
may be rewritten
\[
\InKet{\Psi_g(t)}
=\int d\beta\,\OutKet{\beta}e^{-iE_\beta t}
\int d\alpha\,g(\alpha)S_{\beta\alpha},
\]
and, using the defining property \eqref{eq:3.1.12} for `out' states, this has the
asymptotic behavior for $t\to+\infty$
\[
\InKet{\Psi_g(t)}\longrightarrow
\int d\beta\,\ket{\beta}e^{-iE_\beta t}
\int d\alpha\,g(\alpha)S_{\beta\alpha}.
\]
Comparing this with our previous result, we find
\[
\int d\alpha\,g(\alpha)S_{\beta\alpha}
=g(\beta)-2i\pi\int d\alpha\,
\delta(E_\alpha-E_\beta)g(\alpha)T^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta\alpha},
\]
or in other words
\begin{equation}
S_{\beta\alpha}=\delta(\beta-\alpha)
-2i\pi\delta(E_\alpha-E_\beta)T^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta\alpha}.
\tag{3.2.7}\label{eq:3.2.7}
\end{equation}
This suggests a simple approximation for the $S$-matrix: for a weak
interaction $H_{\mathrm{int}}$, we can neglect the difference between `in'
and free-particle states in \eqref{eq:3.1.18}, in which case Eq.~\eqref{eq:3.2.7} gives
\begin{equation}
S_{\beta\alpha}\simeq\delta(\beta-\alpha)
-2i\pi\delta(E_\alpha-E_\beta)
\bra{\beta}H_{\mathrm{int}}\ket{\alpha}.
\tag{3.2.8}\label{eq:3.2.8}
\end{equation}
This is known as the \textit{Born approximation}~\hyperref[ref:3.3]{[3]}. Higher-order terms are
discussed in Section~3.5.
