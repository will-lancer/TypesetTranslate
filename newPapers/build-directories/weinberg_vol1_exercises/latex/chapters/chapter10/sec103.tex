Although this pole is not in the physical region for nucleon--nucleon
scattering, the pion mass is small enough so that the pole is quite near the
physical region, and under some circumstances may dominate the scattering
amplitude, as for instance for large $\ell$ in the partial wave expansion.

Interpreted in coordinate space, a pole like this at
$(p_1-p'_1)^2=(p_2-p'_2)^2\to-m_\pi^2$ implies a force of range $1/m_\pi$.
For instance, in Yukawa's original theory~\hyperref[ref:10.2]{[2]} of nuclear
force the exchange of mesons (then assumed scalar rather than pseudoscalar)
produced a local potential of the form $\exp(-m_\pi r)/4\pi r$, which in the
first Born approximation yields an $S$-matrix for non-relativistic nucleon
scattering proportional to the Fourier transform:
\begin{align*}
&\int \dd^3\mathbf x_1\,\dd^3\mathbf x_2\,\dd^3\mathbf x'_1\,\dd^3\mathbf x'_2\,
e^{-\ii\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf p_1}e^{-\ii\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf p_2}
e^{\ii\mathbf x'_1\cdot\mathbf p'_1}e^{\ii\mathbf x'_2\cdot\mathbf p'_2}
\\
&\quad\cdot
\frac{\exp(-m_\pi|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2|)}
{4\pi|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2|}
\delta^3(\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x'_1)\delta^3(\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x'_1)
\\
&\qquad=-(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2-
\mathbf p'_1-\mathbf p'_2)\frac{1}{(\mathbf p_1-\mathbf p'_1)^2+m^2}.
\end{align*}
The factor $1/[(\mathbf p_1-\mathbf p'_1)^2+m_\pi^2]$ is just the
non-relativistic limit of the propagator
$1/[(p_1-p'_1)^2+m_\pi^2]$ in \eqref{eq:10.2.17}. (In \eqref{eq:10.2.17}
the energy transfer $p_1^0-p_1'^0$ for $|\mathbf p_1|\ll m_N$ and
$|\mathbf p'_1|\ll m_N$ equals
$[\mathbf p_1^2-\mathbf p_1'^2]/2m_N$, which is negligible compared with the
magnitude $|\mathbf p_1-\mathbf p'_1|$ of the momentum transfer.) When
Yukawa's theory was first proposed, it was generally supposed that this sort
of momentum-dependence arises from the appearance of a meson field in the
theory. It was not until the 1950s that it became generally understood that
the existence of a pole at $(p_1-p'_1)^2\to-m_\pi^2$ follows from the
existence of a pion \emph{particle} and has nothing to do with whether this is
an elementary particle with its own field in the Lagrangian.

\subsection{Field and Mass Renormalization}

We will now use a special case of the result of the previous section to
clarify the treatment of radiative corrections in the internal and external
line of general processes.

The special case that concerns us here is the one in which the four-momentum
of a single external line approaches the mass shell. (In the notation of the
previous section, this corresponds to taking $r=1$.) We will consider a
function
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
G_\ell(q_1q_2\cdots)={}&\int \dd^4x_1\,\dd^4x_2\cdots
 e^{-\ii q_1\cdot x_1}e^{-\ii q_2\cdot x_2}\cdots \\
&\quad\cdot\bra{\Omega}T\{\mathcal O_\ell(x_1),A_2(x_2),\ldots\}\ket{\Omega},
\end{split}
\tag{10.3.1}\label{eq:10.3.1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal O_\ell(x)$ is a Heisenberg-picture operator, with the Lorentz
transformation properties of some sort of free field $\psi_\ell$ belonging to
an irreducible representation of the homogeneous Lorentz group (or the Lorentz
group including space inversion for theories that conserve parity), as labelled
by the subscript $\ell$, and $A_2$, $A_3$, etc. are arbitrary
Heisenberg-picture operators. Suppose there is a one-particle state
$\ket{q_1,\sigma}$ that has non-vanishing matrix elements with the states
$\mathcal O_\ell^\dagger\ket{\Omega}$ and with $A_2A_3\cdots\ket{\Omega}$.
Then according to the theorem proved in the previous section, $G_\ell$ has a
pole at $q_1^2=-m^2$, with
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
G_\ell(q_1q_2\cdots)\to{}&
\frac{-2\ii\sqrt{\mathbf q_1^2+m^2}}{q_1^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}
(2\pi)^3\sum_\sigma
\bra{\Omega}\mathcal O_\ell(0)\ket{q_1,\sigma}
\\
&\quad\cdot\int \dd^4x_2\cdots e^{-\ii q_2\cdot x_2}\cdots
\bra{q_1,\sigma}T\{A_2(x_2)\cdots\}\ket{\Omega}.
\end{split}
\tag{10.3.2}\label{eq:10.3.2}
\end{equation}
We use Lorentz invariance to write
\begin{equation}
\bra{\Omega}\mathcal O_\ell(0)\ket{q_1,\sigma}
=(2\pi)^{-3/2}N u_\ell(\mathbf q_1,\sigma),
\tag{10.3.3}\label{eq:10.3.3}
\end{equation}
where $u_\ell(\mathbf q,\sigma)$ is (aside from the factor $(2\pi)^{-3/2}$)
the coefficient function\footnote{For instance, for a conventionally
normalized free scalar field,
$u_\ell(\mathbf q_1,\sigma)=[2\sqrt{\mathbf q_1^2+m^2}]^{-1/2}$.}
appearing in the free field $\psi_\ell$ with the same Lorentz transformation
properties as $\mathcal O_\ell$, and $N$ is a constant. (It was in order to
obtain Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.3} with a single free constant $N$ that we had to
assume that $\mathcal O_\ell$ transforms irreducibly.) We also define a
`truncated' matrix element $M_{\ell'}$ by
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&\int \dd^4x_2\cdots e^{-\ii q_2\cdot x_2}\cdots
\bra{q_1,\sigma}T\{A_2(x_2)\cdots\}\ket{\Omega}
\\
&\qquad\equiv N^{-1}(2\pi)^{-3/2}
\sum_{\ell'}u_{\ell'}^*(\mathbf q_1,\sigma)M_{\ell'}(q_2\cdots).
\end{split}
\tag{10.3.4}\label{eq:10.3.4}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.2} then reads, for $q_1^2\to-m^2$,
\begin{equation}
G_\ell\to
\frac{-2\ii\sqrt{\mathbf q_1^2+m^2}}{q_1^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}
\sum_{\sigma,\ell'}u_\ell(\mathbf q_1,\sigma)
u_{\ell'}^*(\mathbf q_1,\sigma)M_{\ell'}.
\tag{10.3.5}\label{eq:10.3.5}
\end{equation}
According to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:6.2.2} and \eqref{eq:6.2.18}, the quantity
multiplying $M_{\ell'}$ in \eqref{eq:10.3.5} is the momentum space matrix
propagator $-\ii\Delta_{\ell\ell'}(q_1)$ for the free field with the Lorentz
transformation properties of $\mathcal O_\ell$ (or at least its limiting
behavior for $q_1^2\to-m^2$), so \eqref{eq:10.3.5} allows us to identify
$M_{\ell'}$ as the sum of all graphs with external lines carrying momenta
$q_1,q_2,\ldots$ corresponding to the operators $\mathcal O_\ell,A_2,\ldots$,
but with the final propagator for the $\mathcal O_\ell$ line stripped away.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.4} is then just the usual prescription for how to calculate
the matrix element for emission of a particle from the sum of Feynman diagrams:
strip away the particle propagator, and contract with the usual external line
factor $(2\pi)^{-3/2}u_{\ell'}^*$. The only discrepancy with the usual Feynman
rules is the factor $N$.

The above theorem is a famous result due to Lehmann, Symanzik, and
Zimmerman~\hyperref[ref:10.3]{[3]}, known as the \emph{reduction formula},
which we have proved here by a somewhat different method that has allowed us
easily to generalize this result to the case of arbitrary spin. One important
aspect of this result is that it applies to any sort of operator;
$\mathcal O_\ell$ need not be some field that actually appears in the
Lagrangian, and the particle it creates may be a bound state composed of those
particles whose fields do occur in the Lagrangian. It provides an important
lesson even where $\mathcal O_\ell$ is some field $\Psi_\ell$ in the
Lagrangian: if we are to use the usual Feynman rules to calculate $S$-matrix
elements, then we should first redefine the normalization of the fields by a
factor $1/N$, so that (with apologies for the multiple use of the symbol
$\Psi$):
\begin{equation}
\bra{\Omega}\Psi_\ell(0)\ket{q,\sigma}
=(2\pi)^{-3/2}u_\ell(\mathbf q,\sigma).
\tag{10.3.6}\label{eq:10.3.6}
\end{equation}
A field normalized as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.6} is called a
\emph{renormalized field}.

The field renormalization constant $N$ shows up in another place. Suppose that
there is just one of the operators $A_2,A_3,\ldots$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.1},
and take it to be the adjoint of a member of the same field multiplet as
$\mathcal O_\ell$. Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.2} reads
\begin{align*}
&\int \dd^4x_1\int \dd^4x_2\,e^{-\ii q_1\cdot x_1}e^{-\ii q_2\cdot x_2}
\bra{\Omega}T\{\mathcal O_\ell(x_1)\mathcal O_{\ell'}^\dagger(x_2)\}
\ket{\Omega}
\\
&\quad\xrightarrow{q_1^2\to-m^2}
\frac{-2\ii\sqrt{\mathbf q_1^2+m^2}(2\pi)^3}
{q_1^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}
\sum_\sigma\bra{\Omega}\mathcal O_\ell(0)\ket{q_1,\sigma}
\\
&\hspace{46mm}\cdot\int \dd^4x_2\,e^{-\ii q_2\cdot x_2}e^{-\ii q_1\cdot x_2}
\bra{q_1,\sigma}\mathcal O_{\ell'}^\dagger(0)\ket{\Omega}
\\
&\quad=
\frac{-2\ii|N|^2\sqrt{\mathbf q_1^2+m^2}}
{q_1^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}
\sum_\sigma u_\ell(\mathbf q_1,\sigma)u_{\ell'}^*(\mathbf q_1,\sigma)
(2\pi)^4\delta^4(q_1+q_2).
\end{align*}
This is just the usual behavior of a propagator (the sum of all graphs with two
external lines) near its pole, except for the factor $|N|^2$. According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.6}, this factor is absent in the propagator of the
renormalized field $\Psi_\ell$. Thus \emph{a renormalized field is one whose
propagator has the same behavior near its pole as for a free field, and the
renormalized mass is defined by the position of the pole}.

To see how this works in practice, consider the theory of a real
self-interacting scalar field $\Phi_{\mathrm B}$, the subscript B being added
here to remind us that so far this is a `bare' (i.e., unrenormalized) field. The
Lagrangian density is taken as usual as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\frac12\partial_\mu\Phi_{\mathrm B}\partial^\mu\Phi_{\mathrm B}
-\frac12m_{\mathrm B}^2\Phi_{\mathrm B}^2-V_{\mathrm B}(\Phi_{\mathrm B}).
\tag{10.3.7}\label{eq:10.3.7}
\end{equation}
In general there would be no reason to expect that the field
$\Phi_{\mathrm B}$ would satisfy condition \eqref{eq:10.3.6}, nor that the pole
in $q^2$ would be at $-m_{\mathrm B}^2$, so let us introduce a renormalized
field and mass
\begin{equation}
\Phi\equiv Z^{-1/2}\Phi_{\mathrm B},
\tag{10.3.8}\label{eq:10.3.8}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
m^2\equiv m_{\mathrm B}^2+\delta m^2,
\tag{10.3.9}\label{eq:10.3.9}
\end{equation}
with $Z$ to be chosen so that $\Phi$ does satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.6}, and
$\delta m^2$ chosen so that the pole of the propagator is at $q^2=-m^2$. (The
use of the symbol $Z$ in this context has become conventional; there is a
different $Z$ for each field in the Lagrangian.) The Lagrangian density
\eqref{eq:10.3.7} may then be rewritten
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_0+\mathcal L_1,
\tag{10.3.10}\label{eq:10.3.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_0=-\frac12\partial_\mu\Phi\partial^\mu\Phi-\frac12m^2\Phi^2,
\tag{10.3.11}\label{eq:10.3.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_1=-\frac12(Z-1)
[\partial_\mu\Phi\partial^\mu\Phi+m^2\Phi^2]
+\frac12Z\delta m^2\Phi^2-V(\Phi),
\tag{10.3.12}\label{eq:10.3.12}
\end{equation}
where
\[
V(\Phi)\equiv V_{\mathrm B}(\sqrt Z\Phi).
\]
In calculating the corrections to the complete momentum space propagator of
the renormalized scalar field, conventionally called $\Delta'(q)$, it is
convenient to consider separately the \emph{one-particle-irreducible} graphs:
those connected graphs (excluding a graph consisting of a single scalar line)
that cannot be disconnected by cutting through any one internal scalar line.
An example is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:10.4}. It is conventional to write the
sum of all such graphs, with the two external line propagator factors
$-\ii(2\pi)^{-4}(q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)^{-1}$ omitted, as
$\ii(2\pi)^4\Pi^*(q^2)$, with the asterisk to remind us that these are
one-particle-irreducible graphs. Then the corrections to the complete
propagator are given by a sum of chains of one, two, or more of these
one-particle-irreducible subgraphs connected with the usual uncorrected

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Delta'(q)={}&[q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}

\\
&+[q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}\Pi^*(q^2)[q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}
\Pi^*(q^2)[q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}+\cdots.
\end{split}
\tag{10.3.14}\label{eq:10.3.14}
\end{equation}
Summing the geometric series, this gives
\begin{equation}
\Delta'(q)=[q^2+m^2-\Pi^*(q^2)-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}.
\tag{10.3.15}\label{eq:10.3.15}
\end{equation}
In calculating $\Pi^*$, we encounter a tree graph arising from a single
insertion of vertices corresponding to the terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.12}
proportional to $\partial_\mu\Phi\partial^\mu\Phi$ and $\Phi^2$, plus a term
$\Pi^*_{\mathrm{LOOP}}$ arising from loop graphs like that in
Figure~\ref{fig:10.4}(a):
\begin{equation}
\Pi^*(q^2)=-(Z-1)[q^2+m^2]+Z\delta m^2+\Pi^*_{\mathrm{LOOP}}(q^2).
\tag{10.3.16}\label{eq:10.3.16}
\end{equation}
The condition that $m^2$ is the true mass of the particle is that the pole of
the propagator should be at $q^2=-m^2$, so that
\begin{equation}
\Pi^*(-m^2)=0.
\tag{10.3.17}\label{eq:10.3.17}
\end{equation}
Also, the condition that the pole of the propagator at $q^2=-m^2$ should have
a unit residue (like the uncorrected propagator) is that
\begin{equation}
\left[\frac{\dd}{\dd q^2}\Pi^*(q^2)\right]_{q^2=-m^2}=0.
\tag{10.3.18}\label{eq:10.3.18}
\end{equation}
These conditions allow us to evaluate $Z$ and $\delta m^2$:
\begin{equation}
Z\delta m^2=-\Pi^*_{\mathrm{LOOP}}(0),
\tag{10.3.19}\label{eq:10.3.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Z=1+\left[\frac{\dd}{\dd q^2}\Pi^*_{\mathrm{LOOP}}(q^2)\right]_{q^2=-m^2}.
\tag{10.3.20}\label{eq:10.3.20}
\end{equation}
This incidentally shows that $Z\delta m^2$ and $Z-1$ are given by a series of
terms containing one or more coupling constant factors, justifying the
treatment of the first two terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.3.12} as part of the
interaction $\mathcal L_1$.

In actual calculations it is simplest just to say that from the loop terms
$\Pi^*_{\mathrm{LOOP}}(q^2)$ we must subtract a first-order polynomial in
$q^2$ with coefficients chosen so that the difference satisfies
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.3.17} and \eqref{eq:10.3.18}. As we shall see, this
subtraction procedure incidentally cancels the infinities that arise from the
momentum space integrals in $\Pi^*_{\mathrm{LOOP}}$. However, as this
discussion should make clear, \emph{the renormalization of masses and fields
has nothing directly to do with the presence of infinities, and would be
necessary even in a theory in which all momentum space integrals were
convergent}.

An important consequence of the conditions \eqref{eq:10.3.17} and
\eqref{eq:10.3.18} is that it is not necessary to include radiative
corrections in external lines on the mass shell. That is,
\begin{equation}
\left[
\begin{aligned}
 &\Pi^*(q^2)[q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}\\
 &\quad+\Pi^*(q^2)[q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}\Pi^*(q^2)
 [q^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon]^{-1}+\cdots
\end{aligned}
\right]_{q^2\to-m^2}=0.
\tag{10.3.21}\label{eq:10.3.21}
\end{equation}
Similar remarks apply to particles of arbitrary spin. For instance, for the
`bare' Dirac field the Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\overline\Psi_{\mathrm B}[\sl{\partial}+m_{\mathrm B}]\Psi_{\mathrm B}
-V_{\mathrm B}(\overline\Psi_{\mathrm B}\Psi_{\mathrm B}).
\tag{10.3.22}\label{eq:10.3.22}
\end{equation}
We introduce renormalized fields and masses
\begin{equation}
\Psi\equiv Z_2^{-1/2}\Psi_{\mathrm B},
\tag{10.3.23}\label{eq:10.3.23}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
m\equiv m_{\mathrm B}+\delta m.
\tag{10.3.24}\label{eq:10.3.24}
\end{equation}
(The subscript 2 on $Z_2$ is conventionally used to distinguish the
renormalization constant of a fermion field.) The Lagrangian density is then
rewritten
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_0+\mathcal L_1,
\tag{10.3.25}\label{eq:10.3.25}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_0=-\overline\Psi[\sl{\partial}+m]\Psi,
\tag{10.3.26}\label{eq:10.3.26}
\end{equation}
