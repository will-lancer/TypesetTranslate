\subsection{Calculation of the Propagator}

We now turn to a calculation of the propagator \eqref{eq:6.1.14}, an essential
ingredient in the Feynman rules that arises in the pairing of a field
$\psi_\ell(x)$ with a field adjoint $\psi_m^\dagger(y)$. Inserting Eqs.
\eqref{eq:6.1.15} and \eqref{eq:6.1.16} in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.1.14}, and using the commutation or
anticommutation relations for annihilation and creation operators, we have
immediately
\begin{align}
-i\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)={}&
 \theta(x-y)(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int d^3p\sum_\sigma
 u_\ell(\mathbf p\sigma n)u_m^*(\mathbf p\sigma n)e^{ip\cdot(x-y)}
\notag\\
&\mathbin{\pm}\theta(y-x)(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int d^3p\sum_\sigma
 v_m^*(\mathbf p\sigma n)v_\ell(\mathbf p\sigma n)e^{ip\cdot(y-x)}.
\tag{6.2.1}
\label{eq:6.2.1}
\end{align}
In the course of calculating commutators and anticommutators in Chapter 5, we
showed that
\begin{equation}
\sum_\sigma u_\ell(\mathbf p\sigma n)u_m^*(\mathbf p\sigma n)
=\left(2\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m_n^2}\right)^{-1}
 P_{\ell m}\!\left(\mathbf p,\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m_n^2}\right),
\tag{6.2.2}
\label{eq:6.2.2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\sum_\sigma v_\ell(\mathbf p\sigma n)v_m^*(\mathbf p\sigma n)
=\mathbin{\pm}\left(2\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m_n^2}\right)^{-1}
 P_{\ell m}\!\left(-\mathbf p,-\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m_n^2}\right),
\tag{6.2.3}
\label{eq:6.2.3}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\ell m}(\mathbf p,\omega)$ is a polynomial in $\mathbf p$ and
$\omega$. (Here as in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.1}, the top and bottom signs refer to bosonic
and fermionic fields, respectively.) For instance, if $\psi_\ell(x)$ and
$\psi_m(y)$ are scalar fields $\phi(x)$ and $\phi(y)$ for a particle of spin
zero, then we have simply
\begin{equation}
P(p)=1.
\tag{6.2.4}
\label{eq:6.2.4}
\end{equation}
If $\psi_\ell(x)$ and $\psi_m(y)$ are Dirac fields for a particle of spin
$\tfrac12$, then
\begin{equation}
P_{\ell m}(p)=\bigl[(-i\gamma_\mu p^\mu+m)\beta\bigr]_{\ell m},
\tag{6.2.5}
\label{eq:6.2.5}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ and $m$ are here four-valued Dirac indices. (The matrix $\beta$
appears here because we are considering the pairing of $\psi_\ell(x)$ with
$\psi_m^\dagger(y)$. It is absent in the pairing of $\psi_\ell(x)$ with
$\bar\psi_m(y)=\psi_m^\dagger(y)\beta$.) If $\psi_\ell(x)$ and $\psi_m(y)$ are
vector fields $V_\mu(x)$ and $V_\nu(y)$ for a particle of spin one, then
\begin{equation}
P_{\mu\nu}(p)=\eta_{\mu\nu}+m^{-2}p_\mu p_\nu.
\tag{6.2.6}
\label{eq:6.2.6}
\end{equation}

More generally, if $\psi_\ell(x)$ and $\psi_m(y)$ are components of fields
$\psi_{ab}(x)$ and $\psi_{\bar a\bar b}(y)$ for a particle of spin $j$, in the
irreducible $(A,B)$ and $(\bar A,\bar B)$ representations of the homogeneous
Lorentz group, then
\begin{align}
P_{ab,\bar a\bar b}(p)={}&
 \sum_{a'b'}\sum_{\bar a'\bar b'}\sum_\sigma
 C_{AB}(j\sigma,a'b')C_{\bar A\bar B}(j\sigma,\bar a'\bar b')
\notag\\
&\cdot\left[\exp\!\left(-\theta\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot
 \mathbf J^{(A)}\right)\right]_{aa'}
 \left[\exp\!\left(+\theta\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot
 \mathbf J^{(B)}\right)\right]_{bb'}
\notag\\
&\cdot\left[\exp\!\left(-\theta\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot
 \mathbf J^{(\bar A)}\right)\right]_{\bar a\bar a'}
 \left[\exp\!\left(+\theta\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot
 \mathbf J^{(\bar B)}\right)\right]_{\bar b\bar b'}.
\tag{6.2.7}
\label{eq:6.2.7}
\end{align}
where $\sinh\theta=|\mathbf p|/m$, while $a,b,\bar a,\bar b$ run by unit
steps from $-A$ to $+A$, $-B$ to $+B$, $-\bar A$ to $+\bar A$, and $-\bar B$
to $+\bar B$, respectively, and likewise for the running indices $a',b',\bar
a'$, and $\bar b'$.

Inserting Eqs. \eqref{eq:6.2.2} and \eqref{eq:6.2.3} in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.1} yields
\begin{align}
-i\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)={}&
 \theta(x-y)P_{\ell m}\!\left(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)
 \Delta_+(x-y)
\notag\\
&+\theta(y-x)P_{\ell m}\!\left(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)
 \Delta_+(y-x),
\tag{6.2.8}
\label{eq:6.2.8}
\end{align}
where $\Delta_+(x)$ is the function introduced in Chapter 5
\begin{equation}
\Delta_+(x)\equiv(2\pi)^{-3}\int d^3p\,(2p^0)^{-1}e^{ip\cdot x}
\tag{6.2.9}
\label{eq:6.2.9}
\end{equation}
in which $p^0$ is taken as $+\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$.

To go further, we must say a bit about how to extend the definition of the
polynomial $P(p)$. Eqs. \eqref{eq:6.2.2} and \eqref{eq:6.2.3} only define $P(p)$ for
four-momenta `on the mass shell', i.e., with
$p^0=\pm\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}$. Any polynomial function of such four-momentum
can always be taken as linear in $p^0$, because any power $(p^0)^{2\nu}$ or
$(p^0)^{2\nu+1}$ can be written as $(\mathbf p^2+m^2)^\nu$ or
$p^0(\mathbf p^2+m^2)^\nu$, respectively. Thus we can define a polynomial
$P^{(L)}(q)$ by the conditions that
\begin{align}
P^{(L)}(p)={}&P(p)
 \qquad\left(\text{for }p^0=\pm\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}\right),
\notag\\
P^{(L)}(q)={}&P^{(0)}(\mathbf q)+q^0P^{(1)}(\mathbf q)
 \qquad(\text{for general }q^\mu),
\tag{6.2.10}
\label{eq:6.2.10}
\end{align}
where $P^{(0,1)}$ are polynomials depending only on $\mathbf q$. We can now use
the relations
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^0}\theta(x^0-y^0)
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^0}\theta(y^0-x^0)
=\delta(x^0-y^0)
\tag{6.2.11}
\label{eq:6.2.11}
\end{equation}
(recall that $\theta(x)$ has a unit step at $x^0$, and is otherwise constant)
to move the derivative operators to the left of the $\theta$ functions in Eq.
\eqref{eq:6.2.8}
\begin{align}
\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)={}&P_{\ell m}^{(L)}\!\left(
 -i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\Delta_F(x-y)
\notag\\
&+\delta(x^0-y^0)P_{\ell m}^{(1)}(-i\boldsymbol\nabla)
 \bigl[\Delta_+(x-y)-\Delta_+(y-x)\bigr],
\tag{6.2.12}
\label{eq:6.2.12}
\end{align}
where $\Delta_F$ is the `Feynman propagator'
\begin{equation}
-i\Delta_F(x)\equiv\theta(x)\Delta_+(x)+\theta(-x)\Delta_+(-x).
\tag{6.2.13}
\label{eq:6.2.13}
\end{equation}
However, for $x^0=0$ the function $\Delta_+(x)$ is even in $\mathbf x$, since
a change $\mathbf x\to-\mathbf x$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.9} can be compensated by a
change $\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p$ in the integration variable. We can therefore
drop the second term in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.12}, and write simply
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)=P_{\ell m}^{(L)}\!\left(
 -i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\Delta_F(x-y).
\tag{6.2.14}
\label{eq:6.2.14}
\end{equation}

It will be most useful to use the expression of the Feynman propagator as a
Fourier integral. The step functions in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.13} have the Fourier
representation\footnote{To prove this, note that if $t>0$ then the contour of
integration can be closed with a large clockwise semi-circle in the lower
half-plane, so the integral picks up a contribution of $-2\pi i$ from the pole
at $s=-i\epsilon$. If $t<0$ then the contour can be closed with a large
counter-clockwise semi-circle in the upper half-plane, where the integrand is
analytic, giving an integral equal to zero.}
\begin{equation}
\theta(t)=\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
 \frac{\exp(-ist)}{s+i\epsilon}\,ds.
\tag{6.2.15}
\label{eq:6.2.15}
\end{equation}
This can be combined with the Fourier integral \eqref{eq:6.2.9} for $\Delta_+(x)$. We
introduce new integration variables, $\mathbf q=\mathbf p$,
$q^0=p^0+s$ in the first term of Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.13}, yielding
\begin{align*}
-i\Delta_F(x)={}&\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\int d^3q
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dq^0\,
 \frac{\exp(i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x-iq^0x^0)}
 {(2\pi)^3 2\sqrt{\mathbf q^2+m^2}}
\notag\\
&\cdot\left[
 \left(q^0-\sqrt{\mathbf q^2+m^2}-i\epsilon\right)^{-1}
 +\left(-q^0-\sqrt{\mathbf q^2+m^2}-i\epsilon\right)^{-1}
 \right].
\end{align*}
Combining denominators and adopting a four-dimensional notation, we have
simply
\begin{equation}
\Delta_F(x)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4q\,
 \frac{\exp(iq\cdot x)}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon},
\tag{6.2.16}
\label{eq:6.2.16}
\end{equation}
where $q^2\equiv\mathbf q^2-(q^0)^2$. (In the denominator we have replaced
$2\epsilon\sqrt{\mathbf q^2+m^2}$ with $\epsilon$, because the only important
thing about this quantity is that it is a positive infinitesimal.) This shows
incidentally that $\Delta_F$ is a Green's function for the Klein--Gordon
differential operator, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
(\Box-m^2)\Delta_F(x)=-\delta^4(x)
\tag{6.2.17}
\label{eq:6.2.17}
\end{equation}
with boundary conditions specified by the $-i\epsilon$ in the denominator: as
shown by Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.13}, $\Delta_F(x)$ for $x^0\to+\infty$ or
$x^0\to-\infty$ involves only positive or negative frequency terms,
$\exp(-ix^0\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2})$ or
$\exp(+ix^0\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2})$, respectively.

Inserting Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.16} in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.14} now gives the propagator as
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4q\,
 \frac{P_{\ell m}^{(L)}(q)e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon}.
\tag{6.2.18}
\label{eq:6.2.18}
\end{equation}
There is one obvious problem with this expression. The polynomial $P(p)$ is
Lorentz-covariant when $p$ is on the mass shell, $p^2=-m^2$, but in Eq.
\eqref{eq:6.2.18} we integrate over all $q^\mu$, not restricted to the mass shell. The
polynomial $P^{(L)}(q)$ is defined for general $q^\mu$ to be linear in $q^0$,
a condition that clearly does not respect Lorentz covariance unless the
polynomial is also linear in each spatial component $q^i$ as well. We can
instead always define our extension of the polynomial $P(p)$ to general
four-momenta $q^\mu$, which we shall call simply $P(q)$, in such a way that
$P(q)$ is Lorentz-covariant for general $q^\mu$, in the sense that
\begin{equation*}
P_{\ell m}(\Lambda q)=D_{\ell\ell'}(\Lambda)
 D^*_{mm'}(\Lambda)P_{\ell'm'}(q),
\end{equation*}
where $\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}$ is a general Lorentz transformation, and
$D(\Lambda)$ is the appropriate representation of the Lorentz group. For
instance, for scalar, Dirac, and four-vector fields, these covariant extensions
are obviously provided by just replacing $p^\mu$ with a general four-momentum
$q^\mu$ in Eqs. \eqref{eq:6.2.4}, \eqref{eq:6.2.5}, and \eqref{eq:6.2.6}. For the scalar and Dirac fields,
these are already linear in $q^0$, so here there is no difference between
$P^{(L)}(q)$ and $P(q)$:
\begin{equation}
P_{\ell m}^{(L)}(q)=P_{\ell m}(q)
 \qquad(\text{scalar, Dirac fields}).
\tag{6.2.19}
\label{eq:6.2.19}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, for the vector field of a spin one particle, the 00
components of the covariant polynomial
$P_{\mu\nu}(q)=\eta_{\mu\nu}+m^{-2}q_\mu q_\nu$ are quadratic in $q^0$, so
here there is a difference:
\begin{align}
P_{\mu\nu}^{(L)}(q)
={}&\eta_{\mu\nu}+m^{-2}\left[q_\mu q_\nu
 -\delta_\mu^0\delta_\nu^0\bigl(q_0^2-\mathbf q^2-m^2\bigr)\right]
\notag\\
={}&P_{\mu\nu}(q)+m^{-2}(q^2+m^2)\delta_\mu^0\delta_\nu^0.
\tag{6.2.20}
\label{eq:6.2.20}
\end{align}
(The extra term here is fixed by the two conditions that it must cancel the
$(q_0)^2$ term in $P_{00}(q)$, and must vanish when $q^\mu$ is on the mass
shell.) Inserting this in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.18} gives the propagator of a vector field as
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mu\nu}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4q\,
 \frac{P_{\mu\nu}(q)e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
 +m^{-2}\delta^4(x-y)\delta_\mu^0\delta_\nu^0.
\tag{6.2.21}
\label{eq:6.2.21}
\end{equation}

The first term is manifestly covariant, and the second term, though not
covariant, is local, so it can be cancelled by adding a local non-covariant
term to the Hamiltonian density. Specifically, if $V_\mu(x)$ interacts with
other fields through a term $V_\mu(x)J^\mu(x)$ in $\mathcal H_I(x)$, then the
effect of the second term in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.21} is to produce an effective
interaction
\begin{equation*}
-i\mathcal H_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)=\frac12[-iJ^\mu(x)][-iJ^\nu(x)]
 [-im^{-2}\delta_\mu^0\delta_\nu^0].
\end{equation*}
(The factors $-i$ are the usual ones which always accompany vertices and
propagators. The factor $\tfrac12$ is needed because there are two ways to pair
other fields with $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)$, differing in the interchange
of $J^\mu$ and $J^\nu$.) Thus the effect of the non-covariant second term in
Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.21} can be cancelled by adding to $\mathcal H_I(x)$ the non-covariant
term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{NC}}(x)=-\mathcal H_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)
=\frac{1}{2m^2}[J^0(x)]^2.
\tag{6.2.22}
\label{eq:6.2.22}
\end{equation}
It is the singularity of the equal-time commutators of vector fields at zero
separation that requires us to employ a wider class of interactions than those
with a scalar density. A detailed non-perturbative proof of the Lorentz
invariance of the $S$-matrix in this theory will be given in the next chapter.

It should not be thought that this is solely a phenomenon associated with
spins $j\geq1$. For instance, consider the vector field associated with a
particle of spin $j=0$, equal (as discussed in Chapter 5) to the derivative
$\partial_\lambda\phi(x)$ of a scalar field. For the pairing of this field with
a scalar $\phi^\dagger(y)$, the polynomial $P(p)$ on the mass shell is
\begin{equation}
P_\lambda(p)=ip_\lambda,
\tag{6.2.23}
\label{eq:6.2.23}
\end{equation}
while the pairing of $\partial_\lambda\phi(x)$ with
$\partial_\eta\phi^\dagger(y)$ yields a polynomial
\begin{equation}
P_{\lambda\eta}(p)=p_\lambda p_\eta.
\tag{6.2.24}
\label{eq:6.2.24}
\end{equation}
The covariant polynomials for general off-shell four-momenta $q^\mu$ are again
obtained by just substituting $q^\mu$ for $p^\mu$ in Eqs. \eqref{eq:6.2.23} and
\eqref{eq:6.2.24}. Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.23} shows that $P_\lambda(q)$ is already linear in $q_0$, so
here there is no difference between $P_\lambda(q)$ and $P_\lambda^{(L)}(q)$.
However, for Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.24} there is a difference:
\begin{align}
P_{\lambda\eta}^{(L)}(q)
={}&q_\lambda q_\eta-(q_0^2-\mathbf q^2-m^2)
 \delta_\lambda^0\delta_\eta^0
\notag\\
={}&P_{\lambda\eta}(q)+(q^2+m^2)\delta_\lambda^0\delta_\eta^0,
\tag{6.2.25}
\label{eq:6.2.25}
\end{align}
so here the propagator is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\lambda\eta}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4q\,
 \frac{q_\lambda q_\eta e^{iq\cdot x}}{q^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
 +\delta_\lambda^0\delta_\eta^0\delta^4(x-y).
\tag{6.2.26}
\label{eq:6.2.26}
\end{equation}

Just as before, the non-covariant effects of the second term may be removed by
adding to the interaction a non-covariant term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{NC}}(x)=\frac12[J^0(x)]^2,
\tag{6.2.27}
\label{eq:6.2.27}
\end{equation}
where $J^\mu(x)$ is here the current which multiplies
$\partial_\mu\phi(x)$ in the covariant part of $\mathcal H_I(x)$.

It should be clear that (at least for massive particles) the effects of
non-covariant parts of the propagator can always be cancelled in this way by
adding non-covariant local terms to the Hamiltonian density. This is because
the numerator $P_{\ell m}^{(L)}(q)$ in the propagator must equal the covariant
polynomial $P_{\ell m}(q)$ when $q^\mu$ is on the mass shell, so the difference
between $P_{\ell m}^{(L)}(q)$ and $P_{\ell m}(q)$ must contain a factor
$q^2+m^2$. This factor cancels the denominator $(q^2+m^2-i\epsilon)$ in the
contribution of this difference to Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.18}, so Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.18} always equals
a covariant term plus a term proportional to the delta function
$\delta^4(x-y)$ or its derivatives. The effect of the latter term may be
cancelled by adding to the interaction a term quadratic in the currents to
which the paired fields couple, or in their derivatives. In what follows, it
will be assumed tacitly that such a term has been included in the interaction,
and in consequence we shall use the \emph{covariant} polynomial
$P_{\ell m}(q)$ in the propagator \eqref{eq:6.2.18}, and will thus henceforth drop the
label `$L$'.

It may seem that this is a rather \emph{ad hoc} procedure. Fortunately, in the
canonical formalism discussed in the following chapter, the non-covariant term
in the Hamiltonian density needed to cancel non-covariant terms in the
propagator arises automatically. This, in fact, forms part of the motivation
for introducing the canonical formalism.

Before closing this section, it may be useful to mention some other definitions
of the propagator, equivalent to Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.1}, that appear commonly in the
 literature. First, taking the vacuum expectation value of Eq. \eqref{eq:6.1.14} gives
\begin{align}
-i\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)={}&\theta(x-y)
 \left\langle[\psi_\ell^{(+)}(x),\psi_m^{(+)\dagger}(y)]_{\mp}\right\rangle_0
\notag\\
&\mathbin{\pm}\theta(y-x)
 \left\langle[\psi_m^{(-)\dagger}(y),\psi_\ell^{(-)}(x)]_{\mp}\right\rangle_0.
\tag{6.2.28}
\label{eq:6.2.28}
\end{align}
(Here $\langle AB\cdots\rangle_0$ denotes the vacuum expectation value
$\bra{\Omega}AB\cdots\ket{\Omega}$.) Both $\psi_\ell^{(+)}(x)$ and
$\psi_m^{(-)\dagger}(y)$ annihilate the vacuum, so only one term in each
commutator or anticommutator in Eq. \eqref{eq:6.2.28} actually contributes to the
propagator:
\begin{align}
-i\Delta_{\ell m}(x,y)={}&\theta(x-y)
 \left\langle\psi_\ell^{(+)}(x)\psi_m^{(+)\dagger}(y)\right\rangle_0
\notag\\
&\mathbin{\pm}\theta(y-x)
 \left\langle\psi_m^{(-)\dagger}(y)\psi_\ell^{(-)}(x)\right\rangle_0.
\tag{6.2.29}
\label{eq:6.2.29}
\end{align}
