\subsection{Vacuum Polarization}

We now begin our first calculation of a radiative correction involving
loop graphs, the so-called vacuum polarization effect, consisting of the
corrections to the propagator associated with an internal photon line.
Vacuum polarization produces measurable shifts in the energy levels of
hydrogen, and makes an important correction to the energies of muons
bound in atomic orbits around heavy nuclei. Also, as we shall see in
Volume II, the calculation of the vacuum polarization provides a key
element in the calculation of the high energy behavior of electrodynamics
and other gauge theories.

As in Section 10.5, we define $\ii(2\pi)^4\Pi^{*\mu\nu}(q)$ as the sum of all
connected graphs with two external photon lines with polarization indices
$\mu$ and $\nu$ and carrying four-momentum $q$ into and out of the diagram,
not including photon propagators for the two external lines, and with the
asterisk indicating that we exclude diagrams that can be disconnected by
cutting through some internal photon line. The complete photon propagator
$\Delta'^{\mu\nu}(q)$ is given by Eq. (10.5.13):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta'=\Delta\bigl[\mathbf{1}-\Pi^*\Delta\bigr]^{-1},
  \tag{11.2.1}\label{eq:11.2.1}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta^{\mu\nu}(q)$ is the photon propagator without radiative
corrections. Our task here is to calculate the leading contributions to
$\Pi^{*\mu\nu}(q)$.

In lowest order there is a one-loop contribution to $\Pi^*$, corresponding
to the diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:11.1}:
\begin{align}
  \ii(2\pi)^4\Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  ={}&-\int \dd^4p\,\operatorname{Tr}\left\{
  \left[\frac{-\ii}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{-\ii\sl{p}+m}{p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]
  \left[(2\pi)^4e\gamma^\rho\right]
  \right.\notag\\[-2pt]
  &\left.\qquad\cdot
  \left[\frac{-\ii}{(2\pi)^4}
  \frac{-\ii(\sl{p}-\sl{q})+m}{(p-q)^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon}\right]
  \left[(2\pi)^4e\gamma^\sigma\right]\right\}.
  \tag{11.2.2}\label{eq:11.2.2}
\end{align}
with the first minus sign on the right required by the presence of a fermion
loop. More simply, this is
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  =\frac{-\ii e^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int \dd^4p\,
  \frac{\operatorname{Tr}\{[-\ii\sl{p}+m]\gamma^\rho
  [-\ii(\sl{p}-\sl{q})+m]\gamma^\sigma\}}
  {(p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)((p-q)^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)}.
  \tag{11.2.3}\label{eq:11.2.3}
\end{equation}
The first step in doing this integral is to use a trick introduced by
Feynman~\hyperref[ref:11.1]{[1]}. We use the elementary formula
\begin{equation}
  \frac1{AB}=\int_0^1\frac{\dd x}{[(1-x)A+xB]^2}
  \tag{11.2.4}\label{eq:11.2.4}
\end{equation}
to write the product of scalar propagators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.3} as
\begin{align*}
  &\frac1{(p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)((p-q)^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)}\\
  &\quad=\int_0^1\bigl[(p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)(1-x)
  +((p-q)^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)x\bigr]^{-2}\,\dd x\\
  &\quad=\int_0^1[p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon-2p\cdot qx+q^2x]^{-2}\,\dd x\\
  &\quad=\int_0^1[(p-qx)^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon+q^2x(1-x)]^{-2}\,\dd x.
\end{align*}
(This is a special case of a class of integrals given in the Appendix to
this chapter.) We can now shift the variable of integration in momentum
space\footnote{Strictly speaking, this step is only valid in convergent integrals.
In principle, in order to justify the shift of variables, we should introduce
some regulator scheme to make all integrals converge, such as the dimensional
regularization scheme discussed below.}
\[
  p\longrightarrow p+qx,
\]
so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.3} becomes
\begin{align}
  \Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  ={}&\frac{-\ii e^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1\dd x\int \dd^4p\,
  [p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon+q^2x(1-x)]^{-2}\notag\\
  &\cdot\operatorname{Tr}\{[-\ii(\sl{p}+\sl{q}x)+m]\gamma^\rho
  [-\ii(\sl{p}-\sl{q}(1-x))+m]\gamma^\sigma\}.
  \tag{11.2.5}\label{eq:11.2.5}
\end{align}
Using the results of the Appendix to Chapter 8, the trace here can easily
be calculated as
\begin{align}
  &\operatorname{Tr}\{[-\ii(\sl{p}+\sl{q}x)+m]\gamma^\rho
  [-\ii(\sl{p}-\sl{q}(1-x))+m]\gamma^\sigma\}\notag\\
  &\quad=4\bigl[-(p+qx)^\rho(p-q(1-x))^\sigma
  +(p+qx)\cdot(p-q(1-x))\eta^{\rho\sigma}\notag\\
  &\hspace{6.6em}-(p+qx)^\sigma(p-q(1-x))^\rho+m^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}\bigr].
  \tag{11.2.6}\label{eq:11.2.6}
\end{align}

Our next step is called a Wick rotation~\hyperref[ref:11.2]{[2]}. As long as $-q^2<4m^2$, the
quantity $m^2+q^2x(1-x)$ is positive for all $x$ between 0 and 1, so the
poles in the integrand of Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.5} are at
$p^0=\pm\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2+q^2x(1-x)-\ii\epsilon}$, i.e., just above the
negative real axis and just below the positive real axis. (See Figure~\ref{fig:11.2}.)
We can rotate the contour of integrations of $p^0$ counterclockwise without
crossing either of these poles, so that instead of integrating $p^0$ on the
real axis from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, we integrate it on the imaginary axis
from $-\ii\infty$ to $+\ii\infty$. That is, we can write $p^0=\ii p^4$, with $p^4$
integrated over real values from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. (If an $\ii\epsilon$
instead of $-\ii\epsilon$ had appeared in the denominator of the propagator,
then we would have been setting $p^0=-\ii p^4$, with $p^4$ again integrated
over real values from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. The effect would be a change
of sign of $\Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)$.)

Equation~\eqref{eq:11.2.5} now becomes
\begin{align}
  \Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  ={}&\frac{4e^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1\dd x\int(\dd^4p)_{\mathrm E}
  [p^2+m^2+q^2x(1-x)]^{-2}\notag\\
  &\cdot\bigl[-(p+qx)^\rho(p-q(1-x))^\sigma
  +(p+qx)\cdot(p-q(1-x))\eta^{\rho\sigma}\notag\\
  &\hspace{5em}-(p+qx)^\sigma(p-q(1-x))^\rho+m^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}\bigr],
  \tag{11.2.7}\label{eq:11.2.7}
\end{align}
where
\[
  (\dd^4p)_{\mathrm E}=\dd p^1\dd p^2\dd p^3\dd p^4
\]
and all scalar products are evaluated using the Euclidean norm
\[
  a\cdot b=a^1b^1+a^2b^2+a^3b^3+a^4b^4
\]
with the understanding that $q^4\equiv-\ii q^0$. Also, $\eta^{\rho\sigma}$ can
be taken as either the Kronecker delta, with the indices running over
$1,2,3,4$, or as the usual Minkowski tensor, with the indices running over
$0,1,2,3$.

The integral \eqref{eq:11.2.7} is badly divergent. Eventually all infinities will
cancel, but to see this it is necessary at intermediate stages of the
calculation to use some sort of regularization technique that makes the
integrals finite. It would not do simply to cut off the integrals at some
maximum momentum $\Lambda$, integrating only over $p^\mu$ with
$p^2<\Lambda^2$, because this would amount to introducing a step function
$\theta(\Lambda^2-p^2)$ into the electron propagator, and the Ward identity
(10.4.25) shows that in order to maintain gauge invariance, any modification
of the electron propagator must be accompanied with a modification of the
electron--photon vertex. In fact, with an ordinary cutoff $\Lambda$, radiative
corrections would induce a photon mass, a clear violation of the requirements
of gauge invariance.

Experience has shown that the most convenient method for regulating
divergent integrals without impairing gauge invariance is the dimensional
regularization technique introduced by 't Hooft and Veltman~\hyperref[ref:11.3]{[3]} in 1972,
based on a continuation from four to an arbitrary number $d$ of spacetime
dimensions. This amounts to carrying out angular averages in integrals
like \eqref{eq:11.2.7} by dropping all terms that are odd in $p$, and replacing the
terms that have even numbers of $p$-factors with\footnote{These expressions
may most easily be derived by noting that their form is dictated by Lorentz
invariance and the symmetry among the indices $\mu$, $\nu$, $\rho$, etc.,
while the factors may be found by requiring that both sides give the same
result when contracted with $\eta$'s.}
\begin{align}
  p^\mu p^\nu&\longrightarrow p^2\eta^{\mu\nu}/d,
  \tag{11.2.8}\label{eq:11.2.8}\\
  p^\mu p^\nu p^\rho p^\sigma&\longrightarrow
  (p^2)^2\bigl[\eta^{\mu\nu}\eta^{\rho\sigma}
  +\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}
  +\eta^{\mu\sigma}\eta^{\nu\rho}\bigr]/d(d+2).
  \tag{11.2.9}\label{eq:11.2.9}
\end{align}
Also, after writing the integrand in this way as a function only of $p^2$,
the volume element $\dd^4p_{\mathrm E}$ is to be replaced with
$\Omega_d\kappa^{d-1}\dd\kappa$, where $\kappa\equiv\sqrt{p^2}$, and
$\Omega_d$ is the area of a unit sphere in $d$ dimensions
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_d=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2).
  \tag{11.2.10}\label{eq:11.2.10}
\end{equation}

The integral \eqref{eq:11.2.7} now converges for complex spacetime dimensionality
$d$. We can continue the integral through complex $d$-values to $d=4$,
the infinities then reappearing as factors $(d-4)^{-1}$.

For the integral \eqref{eq:11.2.7}, dimensional regularization gives
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  ={}&\frac{4e^2\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1\dd x\int_0^\infty
  \kappa^{d-1}\dd\kappa\,[\kappa^2+m^2+q^2x(1-x)]^{-2}\\
  &\cdot\left[-\frac{2\kappa^2}{\dd}\eta^{\rho\sigma}
  +2q^\rho q^\sigma x(1-x)
  +(\kappa^2-q^2x(1-x))\eta^{\rho\sigma}
  +m^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}\right].
\end{align*}
The integrals over $\kappa$ can be carried out for any complex $d$ (or for
any real $d$, aside from the even integers). We use the well-known formulas
(given in greater generality in the Appendix to this chapter):
\begin{align}
  \int_0^\infty\kappa^{d-1}[\kappa^2+v^2]^{-2}\dd\kappa
  &=\frac12(v^2)^{d/2-2}\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2),
  \tag{11.2.11}\label{eq:11.2.11}\\
  \int_0^\infty\kappa^{d+1}[\kappa^2+v^2]^{-2}\dd\kappa
  &=\frac12(v^2)^{d/2-1}\Gamma(1+d/2)\Gamma(1-d/2).
  \tag{11.2.12}\label{eq:11.2.12}
\end{align}
and find
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  ={}&\frac{2e^2\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1\dd x
  \Bigl[\left(1-\frac2\dd\right)\eta^{\rho\sigma}
  (m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-1}
  \Gamma(1+d/2)\Gamma(1-d/2)\\
  &+\bigl(2q^\rho q^\sigma x(1-x)-q^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}x(1-x)
  +m^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}\bigr)
  (m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}\\
  &\hspace{13em}\cdot\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2)\Bigr].
\end{align*}
The two terms in the integrand can be combined, using
\[
  (1-2/d)\Gamma(1+d/2)\Gamma(1-d/2)
  =-\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2).
\]
We find
\begin{align}
  \Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  ={}&\frac{4e^2\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^4}
  \Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2)(q^\rho q^\sigma-q^2\eta^{\rho\sigma})
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\int_0^1\dd x\,x(1-x)(m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}.
  \tag{11.2.13}\label{eq:11.2.13}
\end{align}
We note the very important result that this contribution to
$\Pi^{*\rho\sigma}$ satisfies the relation
\begin{equation}
  q_\rho\Pi_{\text{1 loop}}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)=0
  \tag{11.2.14}\label{eq:11.2.14}
\end{equation}
that was derived in Section 10.5 on the basis of the conservation and
neutrality of the electric current. It was precisely to achieve this result
that we adopted the dimensional regularization scheme. The reason that
dimensional regularization gives this result is that the conservation of
current does not depend on the dimensionality of spacetime.

The gamma function $\Gamma(2-d/2)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.13} blows up for
$\dd\to4$. Fortunately, as we saw in Section 11.1, there is another term that
must be added to $\Pi^{*\rho\sigma}(q)$, arising from the term
$-\tfrac14(Z_3-1)F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ in the interaction Lagrangian.
This term has a structure like Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.13}
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_{\mathcal L_2}^{*\rho\sigma}(q)
  =-(Z_3-1)(q^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}-q^\rho q^\sigma),
  \tag{11.2.15}\label{eq:11.2.15}
\end{equation}
so to order $e^2$, the complete $\Pi^*$ has the form
\begin{equation}
  \Pi^{*\rho\sigma}(q)=(q^2\eta^{\rho\sigma}-q^\rho q^\sigma)\pi(q^2),
  \tag{11.2.16}\label{eq:11.2.16}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  \pi(q^2)={}&-\frac{4e^2\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^4}
  \Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2)\int_0^1\dd x\,x(1-x)
  (m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}
  -(Z_3-1).
  \tag{11.2.17}\label{eq:11.2.17}
\end{align}

As we saw in Section 10.5, the definition of the renormalized
electromagnetic field requires that $\pi(0)=0$ (in order that the residue
of the pole in the complete photon propagator at $q^2=0$ should be the
same as for the bare propagator, aside from gauge-dependent terms).
Therefore, to order $e^2$,
\begin{equation}
  Z_3=1-\frac{4e^2\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^4}\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2)
  (m^2)^{d/2-2}\int_0^1x(1-x)\,\dd x,
  \tag{11.2.18}\label{eq:11.2.18}
\end{equation}
so that, to order $e^2$,
\begin{align}
  \pi(q^2)={}&-\frac{4e^2\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^4}
  \Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(2-d/2)\int_0^1\dd x\,x(1-x)\notag\\
  &\cdot\left[(m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}-(m^2)^{d/2-2}\right].
  \tag{11.2.19}\label{eq:11.2.19}
\end{align}

Now we can remove the regularization, allowing $d$ to approach its
physical value $d=4$. As mentioned before, there is an infinity in the
one-loop contribution, arising from the limiting behavior of the Gamma
function
\[
  \Gamma(2-d/2)\longrightarrow\frac1{2-d/2}-\gamma,
\]
where $\gamma$ is the Euler constant, $\gamma=0.5772157$. The infinite part
of $Z_3-1$ is given by using $1/(2-d/2)$ for $\Gamma(2-d/2)$, and
replacing $d$ everywhere else by 4:
\begin{equation}
  (Z_3-1)_\infty
  =-\frac{4e^2\cdot2\pi^2}{6(2\pi)^4}\frac1{2-d/2}
  =\frac{e^2}{6\pi^2}\frac1{d-4}.
  \tag{11.2.20}\label{eq:11.2.20}
\end{equation}
We shall see in Volume II that this result may be used to derive the leading
term in the renormalization group equation for the electric charge.

The poles at $d=4$ obviously cancel in $\pi(q^2)$, because for $d=4$ both
$(m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}$ and $(m^2)^{d/2-2}$ have the same limit,
unity. For the same reason, the term $-\gamma$ in $\Gamma(2-d/2)$ cancels
in the total $\pi(q^2)$, though it does make a finite contribution to
$Z_3-1$. There are other finite contributions to $Z_3-1$, that arise from
the product of the pole in $\Gamma(2-d/2)$ with the linear terms in the
expansion of $\Omega_d\Gamma(d/2)$ around $d=4$, but these also cancel in
the total $\pi(q^2)$. Indeed, in carrying out our dimensional regularization,
we might have replaced $(2\pi)^{-4}$ with $(2\pi)^{-\dd}$, and the factor
$\operatorname{Tr}\mathbf{1}=4$ might have been replaced with the dimensionality
$2^{d/2}$ of gamma matrices in arbitrary even spacetime dimensionalities
$d$, and these too would have contributed to the finite part of $Z_3-1$,
but not of $\pi(q^2)$. Moreover, $e^2$ cannot be supposed to be
$d$-independent, because as shown by inspection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.13}, it has
the $d$-dependent dimensionality $[\text{mass}]^{4-\dd}$. If we take
$e^2\propto\mu^{4-\dd}$, where $\mu$ is some quantity with the units of mass,
then there are additional finite terms in $Z_3-1$, arising from the product
of the pole in $\Gamma(2-d/2)$ with the term $(4-d)\ln\mu$ in the
expansion of $\mu^{4-\dd}$ in powers of $4-d$, but again, these cancel between
$Z_3-1$ and the one-loop contributions to $\pi(q^2)$.

The only terms that do contribute to $\pi(q^2)$ in the limit $\dd\to4$ are
those arising from the product of the pole in $\Gamma(2-d/2)$ with the
linear terms in the expansion of $(m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}$ and
$(m^2)^{d/2-2}$ in powers of $d-4$:
\begin{equation}
  (m^2+q^2x(1-x))^{d/2-2}-(m^2)^{d/2-2}
  \longrightarrow\left(\frac d2-2\right)
  \ln\left(1+\frac{q^2x(1-x)}{m^2}\right).
  \tag{11.2.21}\label{eq:11.2.21}
\end{equation}
This gives at last
\begin{equation}
  \pi(q^2)=\frac{e^2}{2\pi^2}\int_0^1x(1-x)
  \ln\left(1+\frac{q^2x(1-x)}{m^2}\right)\dd x.
  \tag{11.2.22}\label{eq:11.2.22}
\end{equation}

The physical significance of the vacuum polarization can be explored by
considering its effect on the scattering of two charged particles of spin
$\tfrac12$. The Feynman diagrams of Figure~\ref{fig:11.3} make contributions to the
scattering $S$-matrix element of the form
\begin{align*}
  S_a(1,2\to1',2')={}&(2\pi)^{-12/2}\delta^4(p_{1'}+p_{2'}-p_1-p_2)
  \bigl[e_1(2\pi)^4\bar u_{1'}\gamma^\mu u_1\bigr]\\
  &\cdot\left[-\ii(2\pi)^{-4}\frac1{q^2}\right]
  \bigl[e_2(2\pi)^4\bar u_{2'}\gamma_\mu u_2\bigr],\\[4pt]
  S_b(1,2\to1',2')={}&(2\pi)^{-12/2}\delta^4(p_{1'}+p_{2'}-p_1-p_2)
  \bigl[e_1(2\pi)^4\bar u_{1'}\gamma^\mu u_1\bigr]\\
  &\cdot\left[-\ii(2\pi)^{-4}\frac1{q^2}\right]^2
  \bigl[\ii(2\pi)^4(q^2\eta_{\mu\nu}-q_\mu q_\nu)\pi(q^2)\bigr]
  \bigl[e_2(2\pi)^4\bar u_{2'}\gamma^\nu u_2\bigr],
\end{align*}
where $e_1$ and $e_2$ are the charges of the two particles being scattered;
$\pi(q^2)$ is calculated using for $e$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.22} the magnitude of
the charge of the particle circulating in the loop in Figure~\ref{fig:11.3}; and
$q^\mu$ is the momentum transfer $q=p_1-p_{1'}=p_{2'}-p_2$. Using the
conservation property $q_\mu\bar u_{1'}\gamma^\mu u_1=0$ the two diagrams
together yield an $S$-matrix element:
\begin{align}
  S_{a+b}(1,2\to1',2')={}&\frac{-\ii e_1e_2}{4\pi^2q^2}
  [1+\pi(q^2)]\delta^4(p_{1'}+p_{2'}-p_1-p_2)\notag\\
  &\cdot[\bar u_{1'}\gamma^\mu u_1][\bar u_{2'}\gamma_\mu u_2].
  \tag{11.2.23}\label{eq:11.2.23}
\end{align}
In the non-relativistic limit,
$\bar u_{1'}\gamma^0u_1\simeq-\ii\delta_{\sigma_{1'}\sigma_1}$ while
$\bar u_{1'}\gamma^iu_1\simeq0$, and likewise for particle 2. Also, in this
limit $q^0$ is negligible compared with $|\mathbf q|$. Equation~\eqref{eq:11.2.23}
in this limit becomes
\begin{equation}
  S_{a+b}(1,2\to1',2')=\frac{-\ii e_1e_2}{4\pi^2\mathbf q^2}
  [1+\pi(\mathbf q^2)]\delta^4(p_{1'}+p_{2'}-p_1-p_2)
  \delta_{\sigma_{1'}\sigma_1}\delta_{\sigma_{2'}\sigma_2}.
  \tag{11.2.24}\label{eq:11.2.24}
\end{equation}
This may be compared with the $S$-matrix in the Born approximation due
to a local spin-independent central potential $V(r)$:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{Born}}(1,2\to1',2')=-2\pi \ii\delta(E_{1'}+E_{2'}-E_1-E_2)
  T_{\mathrm{Born}}(1,2\to1',2').
  \tag{11.2.25}\label{eq:11.2.25}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
  T_{\mathrm{Born}}(1,2\to1',2')={}&
  \delta_{\sigma_{1'}\sigma_1}\delta_{\sigma_{2'}\sigma_2}
  \int \dd^3x_1\int \dd^3x_2\,V(|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2|)\notag\\
  &\cdot(2\pi)^{-12/2}e^{-\ii\mathbf p_{1'}\cdot\mathbf x_1}
  e^{-\ii\mathbf p_{2'}\cdot\mathbf x_2}
  e^{\ii\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf x_1}e^{\ii\mathbf p_2\cdot\mathbf x_2}.
  \tag{11.2.26}\label{eq:11.2.26}
\end{align}
Setting $\mathbf x_1=\mathbf x_2+\mathbf r$, this gives
\begin{align}
  S_{\mathrm{Born}}={}&\frac{-\ii}{4\pi^2}
  \delta^4(p_{1'}+p_{2'}-p_1-p_2)
  \delta_{\sigma_{1'}\sigma_1}\delta_{\sigma_{2'}\sigma_2}
  \int \dd^3r\,V(r)e^{-\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}.
  \tag{11.2.27}\label{eq:11.2.27}
\end{align}
Comparing this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.23} shows that in the non-relativistic limit
the diagrams of Figure~\ref{fig:11.3} yield the same $S$-matrix element as a potential
$V(r)$ such that
\[
  \int \dd^3r\,V(r)e^{-\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}
  =e_1e_2\frac{1+\pi(\mathbf q^2)}{\mathbf q^2}
\]
or, inverting the Fourier transform,
\begin{equation}
  V(r)=\frac{e_1e_2}{(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3q\,e^{\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}
  \left[\frac{1+\pi(\mathbf q^2)}{\mathbf q^2}\right].
  \tag{11.2.28}\label{eq:11.2.28}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:11.2.28} is to first order in the radiative correction the same
potential energy that would be produced by the electrostatic interaction of
two extended charge distributions $e_1\eta(\mathbf x)$ and
$e_2\eta(\mathbf y)$ at a distance $\mathbf r$:
\begin{equation}
  V(|\mathbf r|)=e_1e_2\int \dd^3x\int \dd^3y\,
  \frac{\eta(\mathbf x)\eta(\mathbf y)}{4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf y+\mathbf r|},
  \tag{11.2.29}\label{eq:11.2.29}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \eta(\mathbf r)=\delta^3(\mathbf r)+\frac1{2(2\pi)^3}
  \int \dd^3q\,\pi(\mathbf q^2)e^{\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}.
  \tag{11.2.30}\label{eq:11.2.30}
\end{equation}
Note that
\begin{equation}
  \int \dd^3r\,\eta(\mathbf r)=1+\frac12\pi(0)=1,
  \tag{11.2.31}\label{eq:11.2.31}
\end{equation}
so the total charges of particles 1 and 2, as determined from the long-range
part of the Coulomb potential, are the same constants $e_1$ and $e_2$ that
govern the interactions of the renormalized electromagnetic field.

For $|\mathbf r|\ne0$ the integral \eqref{eq:11.2.30} can be carried out by a
straightforward contour integration:
\[
  \eta(\mathbf r)=-\frac{e^2}{8\pi^3r^3}\int_0^1x(1-x)\,\dd x
  \left[1+\frac{mr}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right]
  \exp\left(\frac{-mr}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right).
\]
This expression is negative everywhere. However, we have seen that the
integral of $\eta(\mathbf r)$ over all $\mathbf r$ equals $+1$. Therefore,
$\eta(\mathbf r)$ must contain a term $(1+L)\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ that is
singular at $\mathbf r=0$, with $L$ chosen to satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.31}:
\begin{equation}
  L=\frac{e^2}{8\pi^3}\int\frac{\dd^3r}{r^3}\int_0^1x(1-x)\,\dd x
  \left[1+\frac{mr}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right]
  \exp\left(\frac{-mr}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right).
  \tag{11.2.32}\label{eq:11.2.32}
\end{equation}
The complete expression for the charge distribution function is then
\begin{align}
  \eta(\mathbf r)={}&(1+L)\delta^3(\mathbf r)
  -\frac{e^2}{8\pi^3r^3}\int_0^1x(1-x)\,\dd x\notag\\
  &\cdot\left[1+\frac{mr}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right]
  \exp\left(\frac{-mr}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right).
  \tag{11.2.33}\label{eq:11.2.33}
\end{align}
The physical interpretation of this result is that a bare point charge
attracts particles of charge of opposite sign out of the vacuum, repelling
their antiparticles to infinity, so that the bare charge is partially shielded,
yielding a renormalized charge smaller by a factor $1/(1+L)$. As a check,
we may note that if we cut off the divergent integral \eqref{eq:11.2.32} by taking the
integral to extend only over $r\ge a$, we find that the part that is divergent
for $a\to0$ is
\begin{equation}
  L_\infty=\frac{e^2}{12\pi^2}\ln a^{-1}.
  \tag{11.2.34}\label{eq:11.2.34}
\end{equation}
Hence if we identify the momentum space cutoff $\Lambda$ with $a^{-1}$,
the divergent part of $L$ is related to the divergent part of $Z_3-1$ by
\begin{equation}
  (Z_3-1)_\infty=-2L_\infty,
  \tag{11.2.35}\label{eq:11.2.35}
\end{equation}
because to order $e^2$ the renormalized charge (10.4.18) is given by
\begin{equation}
  e_r=Z_3^{1/2}e_{\mathrm B r}\simeq
  \left(1+\frac12(Z_3-1)\right)e_{\mathrm B r}\simeq
  (1+L)^{-1}e_{\mathrm B r}.
  \tag{11.2.36}\label{eq:11.2.36}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:11.2.35} is confirmed below.

Vacuum polarization has a measurable effect on muonic atomic energy
levels. As we shall see in Chapter 14, the effect of Feynman graph (b) in
Figure~\ref{fig:11.3} is to shift the energy of an atomic state with wave function
$\psi(\mathbf r)$ by
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E=\int \dd^3r\,\Delta V(\mathbf r)|\psi(\mathbf r)|^2,
  \tag{11.2.37}\label{eq:11.2.37}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta V(\mathbf r)$ is the perturbation in the potential \eqref{eq:11.2.28}:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta V(\mathbf r)=\frac{e_1e_2}{(2\pi)^3}\int \dd^3q\,
  e^{\ii\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf q^2)}{\mathbf q^2}\right].
  \tag{11.2.38}\label{eq:11.2.38}
\end{equation}
This perturbation falls off exponentially for $r\gg m^{-1}$. On the other
hand, the wave function of electrons in ordinary atoms will generally be
confined within a much larger radius $a\gg m^{-1}$; for instance, for
hydrogenic orbits of electrons around a nucleus of charge $Ze$ we have
$a=137/Zm$ (where here $m=m_e$). The energy shift will then depend only
on the behavior of the wave function for $r\ll a$. For orbital angular
momentum $\ell$, the wave function behaves like $r^\ell$ for $r\ll a$, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.37} gives $\Delta E$ proportional to a factor $(ma)^{-2\ell-3}$.
The effect of vacuum polarization is therefore very much larger for $\ell=0$
than for higher orbital angular momenta. For $\ell=0$ the wave function is
approximately equal to the constant $\psi(0)$ for $r$ less than or of the
order of $m^{-1}$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.37} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E=|\psi(0)|^2\int \dd^3r\,\Delta V(\mathbf r).
  \tag{11.2.39}\label{eq:11.2.39}
\end{equation}
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:11.2.38} and \eqref{eq:11.2.22}, the integral of the shift in the potential
(for $e_1e_2=-Ze^2$) is
\begin{equation}
  \int \dd^3r\,\Delta V(\mathbf r)=-Ze^2\pi'(0)
  =-\frac{4Z\alpha^2}{15m^2}.
  \tag{11.2.40}\label{eq:11.2.40}
\end{equation}
Also, in states of hydrogenic atoms with $\ell=0$ and principal quantum
number $n$ the wave function at the origin is
\begin{equation}
  \psi(0)=\frac2{\sqrt{4\pi}}\left(\frac{Z\alpha m}{n}\right)^{3/2},
  \tag{11.2.41}\label{eq:11.2.41}
\end{equation}
so the energy shift \eqref{eq:11.2.39} is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E=-\frac{4Z^4\alpha^5m}{15\pi n^3}.
  \tag{11.2.42}\label{eq:11.2.42}
\end{equation}
For instance, in the $2s$ state of hydrogen this energy shift is
$-1.122\cdot10^{-7}$ eV, corresponding to a frequency shift
$\Delta E/2\pi\hbar$ of $-37.13$ MHz. This is sometimes called the
Uehling effect~\hyperref[ref:11.4]{[4]}. As discussed in Chapter 1, such tiny energy shifts became
measurable because in the absence of various radiative corrections the pure
Dirac theory would predict exact degeneracy of the $2s$ and $2p$ states of
hydrogen. As we shall see in Chapter 14, most of the $+1058$ MHz ``Lamb
shift'' between the $2s$ and the $2p$ states comes from other radiative
corrections, but the agreement between theory and experiment is good enough
to verify the presence of the $-37.13$ MHz shift due to vacuum polarization.

Although vacuum polarization contributes only a small part of the radiative
corrections in ordinary atoms, it dominates the radiative corrections in
muonic atoms, in which a muon takes the place of the orbiting electron.
This is because most radiative corrections give energy shifts in muonic atoms
that on dimensional grounds are proportional to $m_\mu$, while the integrated
vacuum polarization energy $\int \dd^3r\,\Delta V$ due to an electron loop is
still proportional to $m_e^{-2}$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:11.2.40}, giving an energy shift
proportional to $m_\mu^3m_e^{-2}=(210)^2m_\mu$. However, in this case the
muonic atomic radius is not much larger than the electron Compton wavelength,
so the approximate result \eqref{eq:11.2.39} only gives the order of magnitude of the
energy shift due to vacuum polarization.
