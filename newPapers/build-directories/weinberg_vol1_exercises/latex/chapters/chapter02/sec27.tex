\subsection[Projective Representations]{Projective Representations\texorpdfstring{\protect\footnotemark}{}}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of
the book's main line of development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}

We now return to the possibility mentioned in Section 2.2, that a group of
symmetries may be represented projectively on physical states; that is, the
elements $T$, $\bar T$, etc. of the symmetry group may be represented on the
physical Hilbert space by unitary operators $U(T)$, $U(\bar T)$, etc., which
satisfy the composition rule
\begin{equation}
  U(T)U(\bar T)=\exp\bigl(\ii\phi(T,\bar T)\bigr)U(T\bar T)
  \tag{2.7.1}\label{eq:2.7.1}
\end{equation}
with $\phi$ a real phase. (A bar is used here just to distinguish one symmetry
operator from another.) The basic requirement that any phase $\phi$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.1} would have to satisfy is the associativity condition
\begin{equation*}
  U(T_3)\bigl(U(T_2)U(T_1)\bigr)
    =\bigl(U(T_3)U(T_2)\bigr)U(T_1),
\end{equation*}
which imposes on $\phi$ the corresponding condition
\begin{equation}
  \phi(T_2,T_1)+\phi(T_3,T_2T_1)
    =\phi(T_3,T_2)+\phi(T_3T_2,T_1).
  \tag{2.7.2}\label{eq:2.7.2}
\end{equation}
Of course, any phase of the form
\begin{equation}
  \phi(T,\bar T)=\alpha(T\bar T)-\alpha(T)-\alpha(\bar T)
  \tag{2.7.3}\label{eq:2.7.3}
\end{equation}
will automatically satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.2}, but a projective representation with
such a phase can be replaced with an ordinary representation by replacing
$U(T)$ with
\begin{equation*}
  \widetilde U(T)\equiv U(T)\exp\bigl(\ii\alpha(T)\bigr)
\end{equation*}
for which
\begin{equation*}
  \widetilde U(T)\widetilde U(\bar T)=\widetilde U(T\bar T).
\end{equation*}
Any set of functions $\phi(T,\bar T)$ that satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.2}, and that differ
only by functions $\Delta\phi(T,\bar T)$ of the form \eqref{eq:2.7.3}, is called a
`two-cocycle'. A trivial cocycle is one that contains the function $\phi=0$,
and hence consists of functions of the form \eqref{eq:2.7.3}, which can be eliminated by
a redefinition of $U(T)$. We are interested here in whether a symmetry group
allows any non-trivial two-cocyles; that is, whether it may have a
representation on the physical Hilbert space that is \emph{intrinsically}
projective, in the sense that the phase $\phi(T,\bar T)$ \emph{cannot} be
eliminated in this way.

In order to answer this question, it is useful first to consider the effect of
a phase $\phi$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.1} on the commutation relations of the generators of
infinitesimal transformations. When either $\bar T$ or $T$ is the identity, the
phase $\phi$ must clearly vanish
\begin{equation}
  \phi(T,\mathbf{1})=\phi(\mathbf{1},\bar T)=0.
  \tag{2.7.4}\label{eq:2.7.4}
\end{equation}
When both $T$ and $\bar T$ are \emph{near} the identity the phase must be small.
Using coordinates $\theta^a$ to parameterize group elements (as in Section
2.2), with $T(0)=\mathbf{1}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.4} tells us that the expansion of
$\phi[T(\theta),T(\bar\theta)]$ around $\theta=\bar\theta=0$ must start with
terms of order $\theta\bar\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  \phi\bigl(T(\theta),T(\bar\theta)\bigr)
    =f_{ab}\theta^a\bar\theta^b+\cdots,
  \tag{2.7.5}\label{eq:2.7.5}
\end{equation}
where $f_{ab}$ are real numerical constants. Inserting this expansion in the
power series expansion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.1}, and repeating the steps that led to
\eqref{eq:2.2.22}, we now have
\begin{equation}
  [t_b,t_c]=\ii C^a{}_{bc}t_a+\ii C_{bc}\mathbf{1},
  \tag{2.7.6}\label{eq:2.7.6}
\end{equation}
where $C_{bc}$ is the antisymmetric coefficient
\begin{equation}
  C_{bc}=-f_{bc}+f_{cb}.
  \tag{2.7.7}\label{eq:2.7.7}
\end{equation}
The appearance of terms on the right-hand side of the commutation relation
proportional to the unit element (so-called \emph{central charges}) is the
counterpart for the Lie algebra of the presence of phases in a projective
representation of a group.

The constants $C_{bc}$ as well as $C^a{}_{bc}$ are subject to an important
constraint, which follows from the Jacobi identity. Taking the commutator of
\eqref{eq:2.7.6} with $t_d$, and adding the same expressions with $b,c,d$ replaced with
$c,d,b$ and $d,b,c$, the sum of the three double-commutators on the left-hand
side vanishes identically, and so
\begin{equation}
  C^a{}_{bc}C^e{}_{ad}+C^a{}_{cd}C^e{}_{ab}
    +C^a{}_{\dd b}C^e{}_{ac}=0
  \tag{2.7.8}\label{eq:2.7.8}
\end{equation}
and also
\begin{equation}
  C^a{}_{bc}C_{ad}+C^a{}_{cd}C_{ab}+C^a{}_{\dd b}C_{ac}=0.
  \tag{2.7.9}\label{eq:2.7.9}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.9} always has one obvious class of non-zero solutions for $C_{ab}$:
\begin{equation}
  C_{ab}=C^e{}_{ab}\phi_e,
  \tag{2.7.10}\label{eq:2.7.10}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_e$ is an arbitrary set of real constants. For these solutions, we
can eliminate the central charges from Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.6} by a redefinition of the
generators
\begin{equation}
  t_a\longrightarrow\widetilde t_a\equiv t_a+\phi_a.
  \tag{2.7.11}\label{eq:2.7.11}
\end{equation}
The new generators then satisfy the commutation relations without central
charges
\begin{equation}
  [\widetilde t_b,\widetilde t_c]
    =\ii C^a{}_{bc}\widetilde t_a.
  \tag{2.7.12}\label{eq:2.7.12}
\end{equation}
A given Lie algebra may or may not allow solutions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.9} other than
Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.10}.

We can now state the key theorem that governs the occurrence of intrinsically
projective representations. The phase of any representation $U(T)$ of a given
group can be chosen so that $\phi=0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.1}, if two conditions are met:

\begin{list}{(\alph{enumi})}{\usecounter{enumi}\setlength{\leftmargin}{2.3em}}
\item The generators of the group in this representation can be redefined (as
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.11}), so as to eliminate all central charges from the Lie algebra.

\item The group is simply connected, i.e., any two group elements may be
connected by a path lying within the group, and any two such paths may be
continuously transformed into one another. (An equivalent statement is that
any loop that starts and ends at the same group element may be shrunk
continuously to a point.)
\end{list}

This theorem is proved in Appendix B of this chapter, which also offers comments
about the case of groups that are not simply connected. It shows that there are
just two (not exclusive) ways that intrinsically projective representations may
arise: either algebraically, because the group is represented projectively even
near the identity, or topologically, because the group is not simply connected,
and hence a path from $\mathbf{1}$ to $T$ and then from $T$ to $\bar T$ may not
be continuously deformable into some other path from $\mathbf{1}$ to
$T\bar T$. In the latter case, the phase $\phi$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.1} depends on the
particular choice of standard paths, leading from the origin to the various
group elements, that are used to define the corresponding $U$-operators.

Let's now consider each of these possibilities in turn for the special case of
the Poincar\'e group.

\begin{center}
\emph{(A)\quad Algebra}
\end{center}

With central charges, the commutation relations of the generators of the
Poincar\'e group would read
\begin{align}
  \ii[J^{\mu\nu},J^{\rho\sigma}]
  &={}
    \eta^{\nu\rho}J^{\mu\sigma}
    -\eta^{\mu\rho}J^{\nu\sigma}
    -\eta^{\sigma\mu}J^{\rho\nu}
    +\eta^{\sigma\nu}J^{\rho\mu}
    +C^{\rho\sigma,\mu\nu},
  \tag{2.7.13}\label{eq:2.7.13}\\
  \ii[P^\mu,J^{\rho\sigma}]
  &={}
    \eta^{\mu\rho}P^\sigma-\eta^{\mu\sigma}P^\rho
    +C^{\rho\sigma,\mu},
  \tag{2.7.14}\label{eq:2.7.14}\\
  \ii[J^{\mu\nu},P^\rho]
  &={}
    \eta^{\nu\rho}P^\mu-\eta^{\mu\rho}P^\nu
    +C^{\rho,\mu\nu},
  \tag{2.7.15}\label{eq:2.7.15}\\
  \ii[P^\mu,P^\rho]&=C^{\rho,\mu}
  \tag{2.7.16}\label{eq:2.7.16}
\end{align}
in place of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.4.12}--\eqref{eq:2.4.14}. We see that the $C$s also satisfy the
antisymmetry conditions
\begin{align}
  C^{\rho\sigma,\mu\nu}&=-C^{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma},
  \tag{2.7.17}\label{eq:2.7.17}\\
  C^{\rho\sigma,\mu}&=-C^{\mu,\rho\sigma},
  \tag{2.7.18}\label{eq:2.7.18}\\
  C^{\rho,\mu}&=-C^{\mu,\rho}.
  \tag{2.7.19}\label{eq:2.7.19}
\end{align}
We will now show that all these constants have additional algebraic properties
that allow them to be eliminated by shifting the definitions of $J^{\mu\nu}$
and $P^\mu$ by constant terms. (This corresponds to redefining the phase of the
operators $U(\Lambda,a)$.) To derive these properties, we apply the Jacobi
identities
\begin{align}
  [J^{\mu\nu},[P^\rho,P^\sigma]]
  +[P^\sigma,[J^{\mu\nu},P^\rho]]
  +[P^\rho,[P^\sigma,J^{\mu\nu}]]&=0,
  \tag{2.7.20}\label{eq:2.7.20}\\
  [J^{\lambda\eta},[J^{\mu\nu},P^\rho]]
  +[P^\rho,[J^{\lambda\eta},J^{\mu\nu}]]
  +[J^{\mu\nu},[P^\rho,J^{\lambda\eta}]]&=0,
  \tag{2.7.21}\label{eq:2.7.21}\\
  [J^{\lambda\eta},[J^{\mu\nu},J^{\rho\sigma}]]
  +[J^{\rho\sigma},[J^{\lambda\eta},J^{\mu\nu}]]
  +[J^{\mu\nu},[J^{\rho\sigma},J^{\lambda\eta}]]&=0.
  \tag{2.7.22}\label{eq:2.7.22}
\end{align}
(The Jacobi identity involving three $P$s is automatically satisfied, and hence
yields no further information.) Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.7.13}--\eqref{eq:2.7.16} in Eqs.
\eqref{eq:2.7.20}--\eqref{eq:2.7.22}, we obtain algebraic conditions on the $C$s
\begin{equation}
  0=\eta^{\nu\rho}C^{\mu,\sigma}
    -\eta^{\mu\rho}C^{\nu,\sigma}
    -\eta^{\nu\sigma}C^{\mu,\rho}
    +\eta^{\mu\sigma}C^{\nu,\rho},
  \tag{2.7.23}\label{eq:2.7.23}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
  0={}&\eta^{\nu\rho}C^{\mu,\lambda\eta}
    -\eta^{\mu\rho}C^{\nu,\lambda\eta}
    -\eta^{\mu\lambda}C^{\rho,\eta\nu}
    +\eta^{\mu\eta}C^{\rho,\lambda\nu}
  \notag\\
  &+\eta^{\lambda\nu}C^{\rho,\mu\eta}
    -\eta^{\eta\nu}C^{\rho,\mu\lambda}
    +\eta^{\rho\lambda}C^{\eta,\mu\nu}
    -\eta^{\rho\eta}C^{\lambda,\mu\nu},
  \tag{2.7.24}\label{eq:2.7.24}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  0={}&\eta^{\nu\rho}C^{\mu\sigma,\lambda\eta}
    -\eta^{\mu\rho}C^{\nu\sigma,\lambda\eta}
    -\eta^{\sigma\mu}C^{\rho\nu,\lambda\eta}
    +\eta^{\sigma\nu}C^{\rho\mu,\lambda\eta}
  \notag\\
  &+\eta^{\eta\mu}C^{\lambda\nu,\rho\sigma}
    -\eta^{\lambda\mu}C^{\eta\nu,\rho\sigma}
    -\eta^{\nu\lambda}C^{\mu\eta,\rho\sigma}
    +\eta^{\nu\eta}C^{\mu\lambda,\rho\sigma}
  \notag\\
  &+\eta^{\sigma\lambda}C^{\rho\eta,\mu\nu}
    -\eta^{\rho\lambda}C^{\sigma\eta,\mu\nu}
    -\eta^{\sigma\eta}C^{\rho\lambda,\mu\nu}
    +\eta^{\rho\eta}C^{\sigma\lambda,\mu\nu}.
  \tag{2.7.25}\label{eq:2.7.25}
\end{align}
Contracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.23} with $\eta_{\nu\rho}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mu,\sigma}=0.
  \tag{2.7.26}\label{eq:2.7.26}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, the constants $C^{\mu,\lambda\eta}$ and
$C^{\rho\sigma,\mu\nu}$ are not necessarily zero, but their algebraic
structure is simple enough so that they can be eliminated by shifting the
definitions of $P^\mu$ and $J^{\mu\nu}$, respectively. Contracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.24}
with $\eta_{\nu\rho}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mu,\lambda\eta}=\eta^{\eta\mu}C^\lambda
    -\eta^{\mu\lambda}C^\eta,
  \tag{2.7.27}\label{eq:2.7.27}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C^\lambda\equiv\frac{1}{3}\eta_{\rho\nu}C^{\rho,\lambda\nu}.
  \tag{2.7.28}\label{eq:2.7.28}
\end{equation}
Also, contracting \eqref{eq:2.7.25} with $\eta_{\nu\rho}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mu\sigma,\lambda\eta}
    =\eta^{\eta\mu}C^{\lambda\sigma}
      -\eta^{\lambda\mu}C^{\eta\sigma}
      +\eta^{\sigma\lambda}C^{\eta\mu}
      -\eta^{\eta\sigma}C^{\lambda\mu},
  \tag{2.7.29}\label{eq:2.7.29}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C^{\lambda\sigma}\equiv
    \frac{1}{2}\eta_{\nu\rho}C^{\lambda\nu,\sigma\rho}.
  \tag{2.7.30}\label{eq:2.7.30}
\end{equation}
(These expressions automatically satisfy Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.7.24} and \eqref{eq:2.7.25}, so there
is no further information to be gained from the Jacobi identities.) We now see
that if the $C$s are not zero, they can be eliminated by defining new
generators
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde P^\mu\equiv P^\mu+C^\mu,
  \tag{2.7.31}\label{eq:2.7.31}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde J^{\mu\sigma}\equiv J^{\mu\sigma}+C^{\mu\sigma},
  \tag{2.7.32}\label{eq:2.7.32}
\end{equation}
and the commutation relations are then what they would be for an ordinary
representation
\begin{align}
  \ii[\widetilde J^{\mu\nu},\widetilde J^{\rho\sigma}]
  &={}
    \eta^{\nu\rho}\widetilde J^{\mu\sigma}
    -\eta^{\mu\rho}\widetilde J^{\nu\sigma}
    -\eta^{\sigma\mu}\widetilde J^{\rho\nu}
    +\eta^{\sigma\nu}\widetilde J^{\rho\mu},
  \tag{2.7.33}\label{eq:2.7.33}\\
  \ii[\widetilde J^{\mu\nu},\widetilde P^\rho]
  &={}
    \eta^{\nu\rho}\widetilde P^\mu
    -\eta^{\mu\rho}\widetilde P^\nu,
  \tag{2.7.34}\label{eq:2.7.34}\\
  \ii[\widetilde P^\mu,\widetilde P^\rho]&=0.
  \tag{2.7.35}\label{eq:2.7.35}
\end{align}
The commutation relations will always be taken in the form Eqs.
\eqref{eq:2.7.33}--\eqref{eq:2.7.35}, but with tildas dropped.

Incidentally, the fact that there is no central charge in the algebra of the
$J^{\mu\nu}$ could have been immediately inferred from the fact that this
algebra is of the type known as `semi-simple'. (Semi-simple Lie algebras are
those that have no `invariant Abelian' subalgebra, consisting of generators that
commute with each other and whose commutators with any other generators also
belong to the subalgebra.) There is a general theorem~\hyperref[ref:2.11]{[11]} that
any central charges in semi-simple Lie algebras may always be removed by a
redefinition of generators, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.32}. On the other hand, the full
Poincar\'e algebra spanned by $J^{\mu\nu}$ and $P^\mu$ is not semi-simple (the
$P^\mu$ form an invariant Abelian subalgebra), and we needed a special argument
to show that its central charges can also be eliminated in this way. Indeed,
the non-semi-simple Galilean algebra discussed in Section 2.4 does allow a
central charge, the mass $M$.

We see that the Poincar\'e group satisfies the first of the two
conditions needed to rule out intrinsically projective representations. How
about the second?

\begin{center}
\emph{(B)\quad Topology}
\end{center}

To explore the topology of the Poincar\'e group, it is very
convenient to represent homogeneous Lorentz transformations by $2\times2$
complex matrices. Any real four-vector $V^\mu$ can be used to construct an
Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix
\begin{equation}
  v\equiv V^\mu\sigma_\mu
    =\begin{pmatrix}
      V^0+V^3 & V^1-\ii V^2\\
      V^1+\ii V^2 & V^0-V^3
    \end{pmatrix},
  \tag{2.7.36}\label{eq:2.7.36}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\mu$ are the usual Pauli matrices with
$\sigma_0=\mathbf{1}$. Conversely any $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix can be put in
this form, and therefore defines a real four-vector $V^\mu$.

The property of Hermiticity will be preserved under the transformation
\begin{equation}
  v\longrightarrow\lambda v\lambda^\dagger
  \tag{2.7.37}\label{eq:2.7.37}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda$ an arbitrary complex $2\times2$ matrix. Furthermore, the
covariant square of the four-vector is
\begin{equation}
  V_\mu V^\mu=(V^1)^2+(V^2)^2+(V^3)^2-(V^0)^2
    =-\operatorname{Det}v
  \tag{2.7.38}\label{eq:2.7.38}
\end{equation}
and this determinant is preserved by the transformation \eqref{eq:2.7.37} provided that
\begin{equation}
  |\operatorname{Det}\lambda|=1.
  \tag{2.7.39}\label{eq:2.7.39}
\end{equation}
Each complex $2\times2$ matrix $\lambda$ satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.39} thus defines a
real linear transformation of $V^\mu$ that leaves Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.38} invariant, i.e.,
a homogeneous Lorentz transformation $\Lambda(\lambda)$:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda V^\mu\sigma_\mu\lambda^\dagger
    =\bigl(\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}(\lambda)V^\nu\bigr)\sigma_\mu.
  \tag{2.7.40}\label{eq:2.7.40}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, for two such matrices $\lambda$ and $\bar\lambda$, we have
\begin{align*}
  (\lambda\bar\lambda)V^\mu\sigma_\mu
    (\lambda\bar\lambda)^\dagger
  &=\lambda\bigl(\bar\lambda V^\mu\sigma_\mu
      \bar\lambda^\dagger\bigr)\lambda^\dagger\\
  &=\lambda\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}(\bar\lambda)V^\nu
      \sigma_\mu\lambda^\dagger\\
  &=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}(\lambda)
      \Lambda^\rho{}_{\nu}(\bar\lambda)V^\nu\sigma_\mu
\end{align*}
and so
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda(\lambda\bar\lambda)=\Lambda(\lambda)\Lambda(\bar\lambda).
  \tag{2.7.41}\label{eq:2.7.41}
\end{equation}
However, two $\lambda$s that differ only by an overall phase have the same
effect on $v$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.37}, and so correspond to the same Lorentz
transformation. It is therefore convenient to adjust the phase of the
$\lambda$s so that
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Det}\lambda=1,
  \tag{2.7.42}\label{eq:2.7.42}
\end{equation}
which is consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.41}. The $2\times2$ complex matrices with
unit determinant form a group, known as $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$. (The $SL$ stands for
`special linear', with `special' denoting a unit determinant, while $C$ stands
for `complex'.) The group elements depend on $4-1=3$ complex parameters, or 6
real parameters, the same number as the Lorentz group. However, $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ is
not the same as the Lorentz group; if $\lambda$ is a matrix in $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, then
so is $-\lambda$, and both $\lambda$ and $-\lambda$ produce the same Lorentz
transformation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.37}. Indeed, it is easy to see that the matrix
\begin{equation*}
  \lambda(\theta)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    e^{\ii\theta/2} & 0\\
    0 & e^{-\ii\theta/2}
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
produces a Lorentz transformation $\Lambda(\lambda(\theta))$ which is just a
rotation by an angle $\theta$ around the three-axis, and hence
$\lambda=-\mathbf{1}$ produces a rotation by an angle $2\pi$. The Lorentz group
is not the same as $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, but rather\footnote{The group $\mathbb{Z}_2$ consists of just
elements $+\mathbf{1}$ and $-\mathbf{1}$. In general, when we write $G/H$, with
$H$ an invariant subgroup of $G$, we mean the group $G$ with elements $g$ and
$gh$ identified if $g\in G$ and $h\in H$. The subgroup $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is trivially
invariant because its elements commute with all elements of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$.}
$SL(2,\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{Z}_2$, which is the group of complex $2\times2$ matrices with unit
determinant, and with $\lambda$ identified with $-\lambda$.

Now, what is the topology of the Lorentz group? By the polar decomposition
theorem~\hyperref[ref:2.12]{[12]}, any complex non-singular matrix $\lambda$ may be
written in the form
\begin{equation*}
  \lambda=u e^h,
\end{equation*}
where $u$ is unitary and $h$ is Hermitian
\begin{equation*}
  u^\dagger u=\mathbf{1},
  \qquad
  h^\dagger=h.
\end{equation*}
Since $\operatorname{Det}u$ is a phase factor, and
$\operatorname{Det}\exp h=\exp\operatorname{Tr}h$ is real and positive, the
condition \eqref{eq:2.7.42} requires both
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{Det}u=1,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Tr}h=0.
\end{equation*}
(The factor $u$ simply provides the rotation subgroup of the Lorentz group; if
$u$ is unitary then $\operatorname{Tr}(uvu^\dagger)=\operatorname{Tr}v$, so
$V^0=\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}v$ is left invariant by $\Lambda(u)$.)
Furthermore, this decomposition is unique, so $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ is topologically just
the direct product (i.e., the set of pairs of points) of the space of all $u$s
and the space of all $h$s. Any Hermitian traceless $2\times2$ matrix $h$ can be
expressed as
\begin{equation*}
  h=\begin{pmatrix}
    c & a-ib\\
    a+ib & -c
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
with $a,b,c$ real but otherwise unconstrained, so the space of all $h$s is
topologically the same as ordinary three-dimensional flat space, $\mathbb{R}^3$. On the
other hand, any unitary $2\times2$ matrix with unit determinant can be expressed
as
\begin{equation*}
  u=\begin{pmatrix}
    d+ie & f+ig\\
    -f+ig & d-ie
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
with $d,e,f,g$ subject to the single non-linear constraint
\begin{equation*}
  \dd^2+e^2+f^2+g^2=1,
\end{equation*}
so the space $SU(2)$ of all $u$s is topologically the same as $S^3$, the
three-dimensional surface of a spherical ball in flat four-dimensional space.
Thus $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ is topologically the same as the direct product $\mathbb{R}^3\times S^3$.
This is simply connected: any curve connecting two points of $\mathbb{R}^3$ or $S^3$ can
be deformed into any other, and the same is true of the direct product. (All
spheres $S_n$ except the circle $S_1$ are simply connected.) However, we are
interested in $SL(2,\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{Z}_2$, not $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$. Identifying $\lambda$ with
$-\lambda$ is the same as identifying the unitary factors $u$ and $-u$ (because
$e^h$ is always positive), so the Lorentz group has the topology of
$\mathbb{R}^3\times S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$, where $S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$ is the three-dimensional spherical surface
with opposite points of the sphere identified. This is \emph{not} simply
connected; for instance, a path on $S^3$ from $u$ to $u'$ cannot be continuously
deformed into a path on $S^3$ from $u$ to $-u'$, even though these two paths
link the same points of $S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$. In fact, $S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$ is \emph{doubly}
connected; the paths between any two points fall into two classes, depending on
whether or not they involve an inversion $u\to-u$, and any path in one class
can be deformed into another path of that class. An equivalent statement is
that a double loop, that goes twice over the same path from any element back to
itself, may be continuously contracted to a point. (As discussed in Appendix B,
this is summarized mathematically in the statement that the fundamental group,
or first homotopy group, of $S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$ is $\mathbb{Z}_2$.) Similarly, the
Poincar\'e group has the same topology as $\mathbb{R}^4 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \times S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$, and is
therefore also doubly connected.

Because the Lorentz group (homogeneous or inhomogeneous) is not simply
connected, it does have intrinsically projective representations. However,
because the double loop that goes twice from $\mathbf{1}$ to $\Lambda$ to
$\Lambda\bar\Lambda$ and then back to $\mathbf{1}$ can be contracted to a
point, we must have
\begin{equation*}
  \left[U(\Lambda)U(\bar\Lambda)
    U^{-1}(\Lambda\bar\Lambda)\right]^2=\mathbf{1}
\end{equation*}
and hence the phase $e^{\ii\phi(\Lambda,\bar\Lambda)}$ is just a sign
\begin{equation}
  U(\Lambda)U(\bar\Lambda)=\mathord{\pm}U(\Lambda\bar\Lambda).
  \tag{2.7.43}\label{eq:2.7.43}
\end{equation}
Likewise, for the Poincar\'e group
\begin{equation}
  U(\Lambda,a)U(\bar\Lambda,\bar a)
    =\mathord{\pm}U(\Lambda\bar\Lambda,\Lambda\bar a+a).
  \tag{2.7.44}\label{eq:2.7.44}
\end{equation}
These `representations up to a sign' are familiar; they are just the states of
integer spin, for which the signs in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:2.7.43} and \eqref{eq:2.7.44} are always $+1$,
and the states of half-integer spin, for which these signs are $+1$ or $-1$
according to whether the path from $\mathbf{1}$ to $\Lambda$ to
$\Lambda\bar\Lambda$ and then back to $\mathbf{1}$ is or is not contractible to
a point. This difference arises because a rotation of $2\pi$ around the
three-axis acting on a state with angular-momentum three-component $\sigma$
produces a phase $e^{2\pi \ii\sigma}$, and thus has no effect on a state of
integer spin and produces a sign change when acting on a state of half-integer
spin. (These two cases correspond to the two irreducible representations of the
first homotopy group, $\mathbb{Z}_2$.) Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.43} or Eq.~\eqref{eq:2.7.44} imposes a
superselection rule: we must not mix states of integer and half-integer spin.

For finite mass, the limitation to integer or half-integer spin was previously
derived by purely algebraic means from the well-known representations of the
generators of the little group, which here are just the angular-momentum
matrices $J^{(j)}$ with $j$ integer or half-integer. On the other hand, for zero
mass the action of the little group on physical one-particle states is just a
rotation around the momentum, and here there is no \emph{algebraic} reason for
a limitation to integer or half-integer helicity. There is, however, a
\emph{topological} reason: a rotation by an angle $4\pi$ around the momentum can
be continuously deformed into no rotation at all, so the factor
$\exp(4\pi \ii\sigma)$ must be unity, and hence $\sigma$ must be an integer or
half-integer.

Instead of working with projective representations and imposing a
superselection rule, we can just as well expand the Lorentz group, taking it as
$SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ itself, instead of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{Z}_2$ as before. Ordinary rotation
invariance forbids transitions between states of integer and half-integer total
spin, so the only difference is that now the group is simply-connected, and it
therefore has only ordinary representations, not projective representations,
so that we cannot infer a superselection rule. This does not mean that we
actually can prepare physical systems in linear combinations of states of
integer and half-integer spin, but only that the observed Lorentz invariance of
nature cannot be used to show that such superpositions are impossible.

Similar remarks apply to any symmetry group. If its Lie algebra involves
central charges, then we can always expand the algebra to include generators
that commute with anything, and whose eigenvalues are the central charges, just
as we did when we added a mass operator to the Lie algebra of the Galilean group
at the end of Section 2.4. The expanded Lie algebra is then, of course, free of
central charges, so the part of the group near the identity has only ordinary
representations, and does not require any superselection rule. Likewise, even
though a Lie group $G$ may not be simply connected, it can always be expressed
as $C/H$, where $C$ is a simply connected group known as the `universal covering
group' of $G$, and $H$ is an invariant subgroup\footnote{The first homotopy
group of $C/H$ is $H$. We have seen that the covering group of the homogeneous
Lorentz group is $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, and the covering group of the three-dimensional
rotation group is $SU(2)$. This connection with $SL$ and $SU$ groups is special
to the case of three, four, or six dimensions; for general dimensions $d$ the
covering group of $SO(d)$ is given a special name, `Spin$(d)$'.}
of $C$. In general, we may just as well take the symmetry group as $C$ instead
of $G$, because there is no difference in their consequences, except that $G$
implies a superselection rule, while $C$ does not. In short, the issue of
superselection rules is a bit of a red herring; \emph{it may or it may not be
possible to prepare physical systems in arbitrary superpositions of states,
but one cannot settle the question by reference to symmetry principles,
because whatever one thinks the symmetry group of nature may be, there is
always another group whose consequences are identical except for the absence
of superselection rules.}
