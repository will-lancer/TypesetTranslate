\subsection{The Lagrangian Formalism}

Having seen that various realistic theories may be cast in the canonical
formalism, we must now face the question of how to choose the Hamiltonian.
As we will see in the next section, the easiest way to enforce Lorentz
invariance and other symmetries is to choose a suitable Lagrangian and use
it to derive the Hamiltonian. There is not much loss of generality in this;
given a realistic Hamiltonian, we can generally reconstruct a Lagrangian
from which it could be derived, by reversing the process that we are
going to describe here of deriving Hamiltonians from Lagrangians. (The
derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.1.26} gives one example of this reconstruction.) But
although we can go from Hamiltonians to Lagrangians or Lagrangians
to Hamiltonians, it is easier to explore physically satisfactory theories by
listing possible Lagrangians, rather than Hamiltonians.

The Lagrangian is, in general, a functional\footnote{Recall that in the notation
we use for functionals, a functional like $L$ in which we display the variable
$t$ is understood to depend on the fields $\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)$ and
$\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)$, with the undisplayed variables $\ell$ and $\mathbf{x}$
running over all their values at a fixed value of the displayed variable $t$.
We use upper case $\Psi$s and $\Pi$s to indicate that these are interacting
rather than free fields.} $L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]$ of a set of generic fields
$\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)$ and their time-derivatives $\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)$.
The conjugate fields $\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)$ are defined as the variational
derivatives\footnote{Because the $\Psi$s and $\dot\Psi$s do not in general satisfy
simple commutation or anticommutation relations, we cannot give a simple
definition of the functional derivatives occurring here as we did for functional
derivatives with respect to the $Q$s and $P$s in the previous section. Instead,
we will simply specify that the variational derivatives are what they would be
for c-number variables, with minus signs and equal-time commutators or
anticommutators supplied as needed to make the formulas correct
quantum-mechanically. As far as I know, no important issues hinge on the details
here.}
\begin{equation}
\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)\equiv
\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)},
\tag{7.2.1}\label{eq:7.2.1}
\end{equation}
The equations of motion are
\begin{equation}
\dot\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)=
\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}.
\tag{7.2.2}\label{eq:7.2.2}
\end{equation}
These field equations can be usefully reformulated as a variational principle.
We define a functional of $\Psi^\ell(x)$ over all spacetime, known as the
\textit{action}
\begin{equation}
I[\Psi]\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\,L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)].
\tag{7.2.3}\label{eq:7.2.3}
\end{equation}
Under an arbitrary variation of $\Psi(x)$, the change in $I[\Psi]$ is
\[
\delta I[\Psi]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\int \dd^3x
\left[\frac{\delta L}{\delta\Psi^\ell(x)}\delta\Psi^\ell(x)
+\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(x)}\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(x)\right].
\]
Assuming that $\delta\Psi^\ell(x)$ vanishes for $t\to\pm\infty$, we may integrate
by parts, and write
\begin{equation}
\delta I[\Psi]=\int \dd^4x\left[
\frac{\delta L}{\delta\Psi^\ell(x)}
-\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(x)}
\right]\delta\Psi^\ell(x).
\tag{7.2.4}\label{eq:7.2.4}
\end{equation}
We see that the action is stationary with respect to all variations $\delta\Psi^\ell$
that vanish at $t\to\pm\infty$ if and only if the fields satisfy the field
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.2}.

Because the field equations are determined by the functional $I[\Psi]$,
it is natural in trying to construct a Lorentz-invariant theory to make
$I[\Psi]$ a scalar functional. In particular, since $I[\Psi]$ is a time-integral
of $L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]$, we guess that $L$ should itself be a space-integral of an
ordinary scalar function of $\Psi(x)$ and $\partial\Psi(x)/\partial x^\mu$, known
as the \textit{Lagrangian density} $\mathcal{L}$:
\begin{equation}
L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]=\int \dd^3x\,
\mathcal{L}\bigl(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t),\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Psi(\mathbf{x},t),
\dot\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)\bigr),
\tag{7.2.5}\label{eq:7.2.5}
\end{equation}
so that the action is
\begin{equation}
I[\Psi]=\int \dd^4x\,
\mathcal{L}\bigl(\Psi(x),\partial\Psi(x)/\partial x^\mu\bigr).
\tag{7.2.6}\label{eq:7.2.6}
\end{equation}
All field theories used in current theories of elementary particles have
Lagrangians of this form.

Varying $\Psi^\ell(x)$ by an amount $\delta\Psi^\ell(x)$, and integrating by parts,
we find a variation in $L$:
\begin{align*}
\delta L
&=\int \dd^3x\left[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Psi^\ell}\delta\Psi^\ell
+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Psi^\ell)}
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta\Psi^\ell
+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\Psi^\ell}\delta\dot\Psi^\ell
\right]
\\
&=\int \dd^3x\left[
\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Psi^\ell}
-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Psi^\ell)}\right)
\delta\Psi^\ell
+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\Psi^\ell}\delta\dot\Psi^\ell
\right],
\end{align*}
so (with obvious arguments suppressed)
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta L}{\delta\Psi^\ell}
=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Psi^\ell}
-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Psi^\ell)},
\tag{7.2.7}\label{eq:7.2.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell}
=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\Psi^\ell}.
\tag{7.2.8}\label{eq:7.2.8}
\end{equation}
The field equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.1} then read
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial\Psi^\ell/\partial x^\mu)}
=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Psi^\ell}.
\tag{7.2.9}\label{eq:7.2.9}
\end{equation}
These are known as the \textit{Euler--Lagrange equations}. As expected, if
$\mathcal{L}$ is a scalar then these equations are Lorentz-invariant.

In addition to being Lorentz-invariant, the action $I$ is required to be
\textit{real}. This is because we want just as many field equations as there are
fields. By breaking up any complex fields into their real and imaginary
parts, we can always think of $I$ as being a functional only of a number
of \textit{real} fields, say $N$ of them. If $I$ were complex, with independent real
and imaginary parts, then the real and imaginary parts of the conditions
that $I$ be stationary (the Euler--Lagrange equations) would yield $2N$ field
equations for $N$ fields, too many to be satisfied except in special cases.
We will see in the next section that the reality of the action also ensures
that the generators of various symmetry transformations are Hermitian
operators.

Although the Lagrangian formalism makes it easy to construct theories
that will satisfy Lorentz invariance and other symmetries, to calculate the
$S$-matrix we need a formula for the interaction Hamiltonian. In general,
the Hamiltonian is given by the \textit{Legendre transformation}
\begin{equation}
H=\sum_\ell\int \dd^3x\,
\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)-L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)].
\tag{7.2.10}\label{eq:7.2.10}
\end{equation}
Although Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.1} does not in general allow $\dot\Psi^\ell$ to be expressed uniquely
in terms of $\Psi^\ell$ and $\Pi_\ell$, it is easy to see that Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.10} has vanishing
variational derivative with respect to $\dot\Psi^\ell$ for any $\dot\Psi^\ell$ satisfying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.1}, so in general it is a functional only of $\Psi^\ell$ and $\Pi_\ell$.
Its variational derivatives with respect to these variables are
\begin{align*}
\left.\frac{\delta H}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Pi}
={}&\int \dd^3y\sum_{\ell'}\Pi_{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)
\left.\frac{\delta\dot\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)}
{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Pi}
-\left.\frac{\delta L}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\dot\Psi}
\\
&-\int \dd^3y\sum_{\ell'}
\left.\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)}\right|_{\Psi}
\left.\frac{\delta\dot\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)}
{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Pi},
\\[3pt]
\left.\frac{\delta H}{\delta\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Psi}
={}&\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)
+\int \dd^3y\sum_{\ell'}\Pi_{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)
\left.\frac{\delta\dot\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)}
{\delta\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Psi}
\\
&-\int \dd^3y\sum_{\ell'}
\left.\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)}\right|_{\Psi}
\left.\frac{\delta\dot\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{y},t)}
{\delta\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Psi},
\end{align*}
where subscripts denote the quantities held fixed in these variational
derivatives. Using the defining equation~\eqref{eq:7.2.1} for $\Pi_\ell$, this simplifies to
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\delta H}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Pi}
=-\left.\frac{\delta L}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\dot\Psi},
\tag{7.2.11}\label{eq:7.2.11}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\delta H}{\delta\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Psi}
=\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.2.12}\label{eq:7.2.12}
\end{equation}
The equations of motion \eqref{eq:7.2.2} are then equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\delta H}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}\right|_{\Pi}
=-\dot\Pi_\ell(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.2.13}\label{eq:7.2.13}
\end{equation}

It is tempting now to identify the generic field variables $\Psi^\ell$ and their
conjugates $\Pi_\ell$ with the canonical variables $Q^n$ and $P_n$ of the previous
section, and impose on them the same canonical commutation relations
\eqref{eq:7.1.30}--\eqref{eq:7.1.32}, so that Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.2.12} and \eqref{eq:7.2.13} are the same as the
Hamiltonian equations of motion \eqref{eq:7.1.33} and \eqref{eq:7.1.34}. This is indeed the
case for the simple example of the real scalar field $\Phi$ with non-derivative
coupling. Consider the Lagrangian density\footnote{We do not include a free
constant factor in the term $-\tfrac12\partial_\mu\Phi\partial^\mu\Phi$, because
any such constant if positive can be absorbed into the normalization of $\Phi$.
As we shall see, a negative constant here would lead to a Hamiltonian that is
not bounded below. The constant $m$ is known as the bare mass. The most
general Lagrangian that satisfies the principle of renormalizability (discussed
in Chapter 12) is of this form, with $\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi)$ a quartic polynomial
in $\Phi$.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=-\frac12\partial_\mu\Phi\partial^\mu\Phi
-\frac{m^2}{2}\Phi^2-\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi),
\tag{7.2.14}\label{eq:7.2.14}
\end{equation}
which can be obtained by adding a real function $-\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi)$ of $\Phi$
to the free-field Lagrangian density found in the previous section. The
Euler--Lagrange equations here are
\begin{equation}
(\Box-m^2)\Phi=\mathcal{H}_I'(\Phi).
\tag{7.2.15}\label{eq:7.2.15}
\end{equation}
From this Lagrangian density, we calculate a canonical conjugate to $\Phi$:
\begin{equation}
\Pi=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\Phi}=\dot\Phi,
\tag{7.2.16}\label{eq:7.2.16}
\end{equation}
which is the same as Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.1.36} if we identify $\Phi$ and $\Pi$ with the canonical
variables $Q$ and $P$. The Hamiltonian is now given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.10} as\footnote{In
order for $H$ to be interpreted as an energy, it should be bounded below. The
positivity of the first two terms shows that we guessed correctly as to the sign
in the first term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.14}. The remaining condition is that
$\tfrac12m^2\Phi^2+\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi)$ must be bounded below as a function of
$\Phi$.}
\begin{align}
H&=\int \dd^3x\,(\Pi\dot\Phi-\mathcal{L})
\notag\\
&=\int \dd^3x\left[\frac12\Pi^2+\frac12(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi)^2
+\frac12m^2\Phi^2+\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi)\right],
\tag{7.2.17}\label{eq:7.2.17}
\end{align}
which we recognize as the Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:7.1.35}. This little exercise should
not be regarded as another derivation of this Hamiltonian, but rather as a
validation of the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:7.2.14} as a possible theory of scalar fields.

Matters are not always so simple. We have already seen in the previous
section that there are field variables, such as the time component of a
vector field or the Hermitian conjugate of a Dirac field, that are not
canonical field variables $Q^n$ and do not have canonical conjugates; yet
Lorentz invariance dictates that these must appear in the Lagrangians for
the vector and Dirac fields.

From the point of view of the Lagrangian formalism, the special character
of field variables like the time component of a vector field or the
Hermitian conjugate of a Dirac field arises from the fact that although
they appear in the Lagrangian, their time-derivatives do not. We shall
denote the field variables $\Psi^\ell$ whose time-derivatives do not appear in
the Lagrangian as $C^r$; the remaining independent field variables are the
canonical variables $Q^n$. The $Q^n$ have canonical conjugates
\begin{equation}
P_n(\mathbf{x},t)=
\frac{\delta L[Q(t),\dot Q(t),C(t)]}{\delta\dot Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)},
\tag{7.2.18}\label{eq:7.2.18}
\end{equation}
and satisfy the commutation relations~\eqref{eq:7.1.30}--\eqref{eq:7.1.32}, but there are no
canonical conjugates for the $C^r$. Because $\delta L/\delta\dot C^r=0$, the
Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:7.2.10} is in general
\begin{equation}
H=\sum_n\int \dd^3x\,P_n\dot Q^n-L[Q(t),\dot Q(t),C(t)],
\tag{7.2.19}\label{eq:7.2.19}
\end{equation}
but this is not yet useful until we express the $C^r$ and $\dot Q^\ell$ in terms of the
$Q$s and $P$s. The equations of motion of the $C^r$ involve only fields and
their first time-derivatives
\begin{equation}
0=\frac{\delta L[Q(t),\dot Q(t),C(t)]}{\delta C^r(\mathbf{x},t)}.
\tag{7.2.20}\label{eq:7.2.20}
\end{equation}
In the simple cases to be discussed in this chapter, these equations together
with Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.18} can be solved to give the $C^r$ and $\dot Q^\ell$ in terms of the
$Q$s and $P$s. Section 7.6 shows how in such cases one can avoid the task of
actually solving for the $C^r$ and $\dot Q^\ell$. In gauge theories like electrodynamics
other methods must be used: either choosing a particular gauge, as in
Chapter 8, or the more modern covariant methods to be discussed in
Volume II.

Once we have derived a Hamiltonian as a functional of the Heisenberg
picture $Q$s and $P$s, to use perturbation theory we must make a transition
to the interaction picture. The Hamiltonian is time-independent, so it can
be written in terms of the $P_n$ and $Q^n$ at $t=0$, which are equal to the
corresponding operators $p_n$ and $q^n$ in the interaction picture at $t=0$.
The Hamiltonian derived in this way may then be expressed in terms of the
$q$s and $p$s of the interaction picture, and split into two parts, a suitable
free-particle term $H_0$ and an interaction $H_{\mathrm{int}}$. Finally, the
time-dependence equations~\eqref{eq:7.1.21} and \eqref{eq:7.1.22} and the commutation or
anticommutation relations~\eqref{eq:7.1.1}--\eqref{eq:7.1.2} are used to express the $q$s and $p$s
in $H_I(t)$ as linear combinations of annihilation and creation operators.

We shall present a number of examples of this procedure in Section
7.5; for the moment we will give only one example of the simplest type,
the scalar field with Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:7.2.17}. We split $H$ into a free-particle
term and an interaction
\begin{equation}
H=H_0+H_{\mathrm{int}},
\tag{7.2.21}\label{eq:7.2.21}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H_0=\int \dd^3x\left[\frac12\Pi^2+\frac12(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi)^2
+\frac12m^2\Phi^2\right],
\tag{7.2.22}\label{eq:7.2.22}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{int}}=\int \dd^3x\,\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi).
\tag{7.2.23}\label{eq:7.2.23}
\end{equation}
Here $\Phi$ and $\Pi$ are taken at the same time $t$, and $H$ is independent of
$t$, though $H_0$ and $H_{\mathrm{int}}$ usually are not.

We now pass to the interaction representation. Taking $t=0$ in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.2.22} and \eqref{eq:7.2.23}, we can simply replace $\Phi,\Pi$ with the
interaction picture variables $\phi,\pi$, since they are defined by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.1.28} and
\eqref{eq:7.1.29} to be equal at that time. To calculate the interaction $H_I(t)$ in the
interaction picture, we apply the similarity transformation \eqref{eq:3.5.5}
\begin{equation}
H_I(t)=\exp(\ii H_0t)H_{\mathrm{int}}\exp(-\ii H_0t)
=\int \dd^3x\,\mathcal{H}_I\bigl(\phi(\mathbf{x},t)\bigr).
\tag{7.2.24}\label{eq:7.2.24}
\end{equation}
The same transformation applied to $H_0$ leaves it constant:
\begin{align}
H_0&=\exp(\ii H_0t)H_0\exp(-\ii H_0t)
\notag\\
&=\int \dd^3x\left[\frac12\pi^2(\mathbf{x},t)
+\frac12\bigl(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi(\mathbf{x},t)\bigr)^2\right].
\tag{7.2.25}\label{eq:7.2.25}
\end{align}
The relation between $\pi$ and $\dot\phi$ is dictated by Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.1.21}
\begin{equation}
\dot\phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\delta H_0}{\delta\pi(\mathbf{x},t)}
=\pi(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.2.26}\label{eq:7.2.26}
\end{equation}
(This happens to be the same relation as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.16}, but as we shall
see this is not to be expected in general.) Also, the equation of motion for
$\phi$ is dictated by Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.1.22}:
\begin{equation}
\dot\pi(\mathbf{x},t)=-\frac{\delta H_0}{\delta\phi(\mathbf{x},t)}
=+\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\phi(\mathbf{x},t)-m^2\phi(\mathbf{x},t),
\tag{7.2.27}\label{eq:7.2.27}
\end{equation}
which together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.26} yields the field equations
\begin{equation}
(\Box-m^2)\phi(x)=0.
\tag{7.2.28}\label{eq:7.2.28}
\end{equation}
The general real solution may be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\phi(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int \dd^3p\,(2p^0)^{-1/2}
\left[e^{\ii p\cdot x}a_{\mathbf{p}}+e^{-\ii p\cdot x}a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}}\right]
\tag{7.2.29}\label{eq:7.2.29}
\end{equation}
with $p^0=\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}$ understood, and $a_{\mathbf{p}}$ some
as-yet-unknown operator function of $\mathbf{p}$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.26} then gives the
canonical conjugate as
\begin{equation}
\pi(x)=-\ii(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int \dd^3p\,(p^0/2)^{1/2}
\left[e^{\ii p\cdot x}a_{\mathbf{p}}-e^{-\ii p\cdot x}a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}}\right].
\tag{7.2.30}\label{eq:7.2.30}
\end{equation}
In order to get the desired commutation relations,
\begin{equation}
[\phi(\mathbf{x},t),\pi(\mathbf{y},t)]=\ii\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}),
\tag{7.2.31}\label{eq:7.2.31}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[\phi(\mathbf{x},t),\phi(\mathbf{y},t)]=0,
\tag{7.2.32}\label{eq:7.2.32}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[\pi(\mathbf{x},t),\pi(\mathbf{y},t)]=0,
\tag{7.2.33}\label{eq:7.2.33}
\end{equation}
we must take the $a$s to satisfy the familiar commutation relations
\begin{equation}
[a_{\mathbf{p}},a^\dagger_{\mathbf{p}'}]=\delta^3(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}'),
\tag{7.2.34}\label{eq:7.2.34}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[a_{\mathbf{p}},a_{\mathbf{p}'}]=0.
\tag{7.2.35}\label{eq:7.2.35}
\end{equation}
Also, we have already shown in the previous section that using these
expansions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.25} gives the usual formula~\eqref{eq:4.2.11} for the
free-particle Hamiltonian, up to an inconsequential additive constant. As
remarked before, these results should not be regarded so much as an
alternative derivation of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.2.29}, \eqref{eq:7.2.34}, and \eqref{eq:7.2.35} (which were
obtained in Chapter 5 on quite other grounds) but rather as a validation
of the first two terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.14} as the correct free-particle Lagrangian
for a real scalar field. We can now proceed to use perturbation theory to
calculate the $S$-matrix, taking \eqref{eq:7.2.24} as $H_I(t)$, with the field $\phi(x)$ given
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.29}.

The procedures illustrated here will be carried out for examples that
are more complicated and more interesting in Section 7.5.

In considering the various possible Lagrangian densities for physical
theories it is common to apply integration by parts, treating as equivalent
any Lagrangian densities that differ only by total derivatives
$\partial_\mu\mathcal{F}^\mu$. It is obvious that such total derivative terms do
not contribute to the action and hence do not affect the field equations. It
is also obvious that a space-derivative term $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}$
in the Lagrangian density does not contribute to the Lagrangian and hence
does not affect the quantum theory defined by the Lagrangian.\footnote{This
is under the usual assumption, that the fields vanish at infinity. These
results do not necessary apply when we allow fields of different topology,
as discussed in Volume II.} What is less obvious and worth noting here is that
a time-derivative $\partial_0\mathcal{F}^0$ in the Lagrangian density also does
not affect the quantum structure of the theory. To see this, let's first consider
the effect of adding a term to the Lagrangian of the more general form
\begin{equation}
\Delta L(t)=\int \dd^3x\,D_{n,\mathbf{x}}[Q(t)]\dot Q^n(\mathbf{x},t),
\tag{7.2.36}\label{eq:7.2.36}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is an arbitrary $n$- and $\mathbf{x}$-dependent functional of the values
of $Q$ at a given time. This changes the formula for the conjugate variables
$P(t)$ as functionals of $Q(t)$ and $\dot Q(t)$ by the amount
\begin{equation}
\Delta P_n(\mathbf{x},t)=
\frac{\delta\Delta L(t)}{\delta\dot Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)}
=D_{n,\mathbf{x}}[Q(t)].
\tag{7.2.37}\label{eq:7.2.37}
\end{equation}
It follows that there is no change in the Hamiltonian as expressed as a
functional of the $Q(t)$ and $\dot Q(t)$:
\begin{equation}
\int \dd^3x\,\Delta P_n(\mathbf{x},t)\dot Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)-\Delta L(t)=0.
\tag{7.2.38}\label{eq:7.2.38}
\end{equation}
Hence also there is no change in the Hamiltonian as expressed as a
functional of the old canonical variables $Q^n$ and $P_n$. However, the
Hamiltonian is \textit{not} the same functional of the \textit{new} canonical variables
$Q^n$ and $P_n+\Delta P_n$ as it was of the $Q^n$ and $P_n$, and in a theory
described by the new Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}+\Delta\mathcal{L}$ it is the new
canonical variables $Q^n$ and $P_n+\Delta P_n$ rather than the $Q^n$ and $P_n$
that would satisfy the canonical commutation relations. The commutators
of the $Q^n$ with each other and of the $Q^n$ with the $P_m$ are given by the
usual canonical relations, but the commutators of the $P_n$ with each other
are now
\begin{align}
[P_n(\mathbf{x},t),P_m(\mathbf{y},t)]
={}&[P_n(\mathbf{x},t)+\Delta P_n(\mathbf{x},t),
P_m(\mathbf{y},t)+\Delta P_m(\mathbf{y},t)]
\notag\\
&-[\Delta P_n(\mathbf{x},t),P_m(\mathbf{y},t)+\Delta P_m(\mathbf{y},t)]
\notag\\
&-[P_n(\mathbf{x},t)+\Delta P_n(\mathbf{x},t),\Delta P_m(\mathbf{y},t)]
\notag\\
&+[\Delta P_n(\mathbf{x},t),\Delta P_m(\mathbf{y},t)]
\notag\\
={}&-\ii\frac{\delta D_{n,\mathbf{x}}[Q(t)]}{\delta Q^m(\mathbf{y},t)}
+\ii\frac{\delta D_{m,\mathbf{y}}[Q(t)]}{\delta Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)}.
\tag{7.2.39}\label{eq:7.2.39}
\end{align}
In general this doesn't vanish, but if the added term in the Lagrangian is
a total time-derivative
\begin{equation}
\Delta L=\frac{\dd G}{\dd t}=\int \dd^3x\,
\frac{\delta G[Q(t)]}{\delta Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)}\dot Q^n(\mathbf{x},t),
\tag{7.2.40}\label{eq:7.2.40}
\end{equation}
then $D$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.36} is of the special form
\begin{equation}
D_{n,\mathbf{x}}[Q]=\frac{\delta G[Q(t)]}{\delta Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)}.
\tag{7.2.41}\label{eq:7.2.41}
\end{equation}
In this case the commutator \eqref{eq:7.2.39} vanishes, so the variables $Q^n$ and
$P_n$ satisfy the usual commutation relations. We have seen that a change
of the form \eqref{eq:7.2.36} in the Lagrangian does not change the form of the
Hamiltonian as a functional of the $Q^n$ and $P_n$, and since, as we have now
shown, the commutation relations of these variables are also unchanged,
the addition to the Lagrangian of the term \eqref{eq:7.2.36} has no effect on the
quantum structure of the theory. Different Lagrangian densities obtained
from each other by partial integration may therefore be regarded as
equivalent in quantum as well as classical field theory.
