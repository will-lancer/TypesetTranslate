\subsection{Global Symmetries}

We now come to the real point of the Lagrangian formalism, that it provides a
natural framework for the quantum mechanical implementation of symmetry
principles. This is because the dynamical equations in the Lagrangian formalism
take the form of a variational principle, the principle of stationary action.
Consider any infinitesimal transformation of the fields
\begin{equation}
\Psi^\ell(x)\longrightarrow\Psi^\ell(x)+\ii\epsilon\mathcal{F}^\ell(x)
\tag{7.3.1}\label{eq:7.3.1}
\end{equation}
that leaves the action \eqref{eq:7.2.3} invariant:
\begin{equation}
0=\delta I=\ii\epsilon\int \dd^4x\,
\frac{\delta I[\Psi]}{\delta\Psi^\ell(x)}\mathcal{F}^\ell(x).
\tag{7.3.2}\label{eq:7.3.2}
\end{equation}
(With $\epsilon$ a constant, such symmetries are known as \emph{global
symmetries}. In general, $\mathcal{F}^\ell$ depends on the fields and their
derivatives at $x$.) Of course Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.2} is automatically satisfied for
\emph{all} infinitesimal variations of the fields if the fields satisfy the
dynamical equations; by an infinitesimal symmetry transformation we mean one
that leaves the action invariant even when the dynamical equations are
\emph{not} satisfied. If we now consider the same transformation with
$\epsilon$ an arbitrary function of position in spacetime:
\begin{equation}
\Psi^\ell(x)\longrightarrow\Psi^\ell(x)
+\ii\epsilon(x)\mathcal{F}^\ell(x),
\tag{7.3.3}\label{eq:7.3.3}
\end{equation}
then, in general, the variation of the action will not vanish, but it will
have to be of the form
\begin{equation}
\delta I=-\int \dd^4x\,J^\mu(x)\frac{\partial\epsilon(x)}{\partial x^\mu}
\tag{7.3.4}\label{eq:7.3.4}
\end{equation}
in order that it should vanish when $\epsilon(x)$ is constant. If we \emph{now}
take the fields in $I[\Psi]$ to satisfy the field equations then $I$ is
stationary with respect to arbitrary field variations that vanish at large
spacetime distances, including variations of the form \eqref{eq:7.3.3}, so in this case
\eqref{eq:7.3.4} should vanish. Integrating by parts, we see that $J^\mu(x)$ must satisfy
a conservation law:
\begin{equation}
0=\frac{\partial J^\mu(x)}{\partial x^\mu}.
\tag{7.3.5}\label{eq:7.3.5}
\end{equation}
It follows immediately that
\begin{equation}
0=\frac{\dd F}{\dd t},
\tag{7.3.6}\label{eq:7.3.6}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
F\equiv\int \dd^3x\,J^0.
\tag{7.3.7}\label{eq:7.3.7}
\end{equation}
There is one such conserved current $J^\mu$ and one constant of the motion $F$
for each independent infinitesimal symmetry transformation. This represents a
general feature of the canonical formalism, often referred to as Noether's
theorem: \emph{symmetries imply conservation laws}.

Many symmetry transformations leave the Lagrangian and not just the action
invariant. This is the case, for instance, for translations and rotations in
space and also isospin transformations and other internal symmetry
transformations, though not for general Lorentz transformations. When the
Lagrangian is invariant we can go further, and write an explicit formula for
the conserved quantities $F$. Consider a field variation \eqref{eq:7.3.3} in which
$\epsilon(x)$ depends on $t$ but not $\mathbf{x}$. In this case the variation in
the action is
\begin{align}
\delta I=\ii\int \dd t\int \dd^3x\Bigg[&
\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}
\epsilon(t)\mathcal{F}^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)\notag\\
&+\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(\epsilon(t)\mathcal{F}^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)\bigr)\Bigg].
\tag{7.3.8}\label{eq:7.3.8}
\end{align}
The requirement that the Lagrangian be invariant under this transformation
when $\epsilon$ is a constant yields
\begin{equation}
0=\int \dd^3x\left[
\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}
\mathcal{F}^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)
+\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\mathcal{F}^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)\right],
\tag{7.3.9}\label{eq:7.3.9}
\end{equation}
so for general fields (whether or not the field equations are satisfied) the
variation in the action is
\begin{equation}
\delta I=\ii\int \dd t\int \dd^3x\,
\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}
\dot\epsilon(t)\mathcal{F}^\ell(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.10}\label{eq:7.3.10}
\end{equation}
Comparing this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.4} gives
\begin{equation}
F=-\ii\int \dd^3x\,
\frac{\delta L[\Psi(t),\dot\Psi(t)]}{\delta\dot\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{x},t)}
\mathcal{F}^\ell(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.11}\label{eq:7.3.11}
\end{equation}
Using the symmetry condition \eqref{eq:7.3.9}, the reader can easily check that this
$F$ is indeed time-independent for any fields that satisfy the dynamical
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.2}.

Other symmetry transformations such as isospin rotations leave not only the
action and the Lagrangian invariant but also the Lagrangian density. In such
cases we can go even further, and write an explicit formula for the current
$J^\mu(x)$. Writing the action as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.2.6} as the integral of the
Lagrangian density, its variation under the transformation \eqref{eq:7.3.3} with a
general infinitesimal parameter $\epsilon(x)$ is
\begin{align}
\delta I[\Psi]=\ii\int \dd^4x\Bigg[&
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\Psi(x),\partial_\mu\Psi(x))}
{\partial\Psi^\ell(x)}\mathcal{F}^\ell(x)\epsilon(x)\notag\\
&+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\Psi(x),\partial_\mu\Psi(x))}
{\partial(\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x))}
\partial_\mu\bigl(\mathcal{F}^\ell(x)\epsilon(x)\bigr)\Bigg].
\tag{7.3.12}\label{eq:7.3.12}
\end{align}
The invariance of the Lagrangian density when $\epsilon$ is a constant requires
that
\begin{equation}
0=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\Psi(x),\partial_\mu\Psi(x))}
{\partial\Psi^\ell(x)}\mathcal{F}^\ell(x)
+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\Psi(x),\partial_\mu\Psi(x))}
{\partial(\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x))}\partial_\mu\mathcal{F}^\ell(x),
\tag{7.3.13}\label{eq:7.3.13}
\end{equation}
so for arbitrary fields the variation of the action is
\begin{equation}
\delta I[\Psi]=\ii\int \dd^4x\,
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\Psi(x),\partial_\mu\Psi(x))}
{\partial(\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x))}
\partial_\mu\epsilon(x).
\tag{7.3.14}\label{eq:7.3.14}
\end{equation}
Comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.4} shows that
\begin{equation}
J^\mu=-\ii\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}
{\partial(\partial\Psi^\ell/\partial x^\mu)}\mathcal{F}^\ell.
\tag{7.3.15}\label{eq:7.3.15}
\end{equation}
Using the symmetry condition \eqref{eq:7.3.13}, it is easy to see directly that
$\partial_\mu J^\mu$ vanishes when the fields satisfy the Euler--Lagrange
equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.9}. Note also that the integral of the time component of the
current \eqref{eq:7.3.15} has the previously derived value \eqref{eq:7.3.11}.

So far everything we have said would apply to classical as well as quantum
mechanical field theories. The quantum properties of the conserved quantities
$F$ are most easily seen for symmetries of the Lagrangian (not necessarily the
Lagrangian density) that transform the canonical fields $Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)$
(that is, those of the $\Psi^\ell$ whose time derivatives appear in the
Lagrangian) into $\mathbf{x}$-dependent functionals of themselves at the same
time. For such transformations, we have
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}^n(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathcal{F}^n[Q(t);\mathbf{x}].
\tag{7.3.16}\label{eq:7.3.16}
\end{equation}
As we shall see, infinitesimal spatial translations and rotations as well as
all infinitesimal internal symmetry transformations are of the form \eqref{eq:7.3.1},
\eqref{eq:7.3.16}, with $\mathcal{F}^n$ a linear functional of the $Q^m$, but we will not
need to assume here that the symmetry is linear. For all such symmetries the
operator $F$ is not only conserved; it also acts in quantum mechanics as a
\emph{generator} of this symmetry.

To see this, note first that when $\Psi^\ell$ is a canonical field $Q^n$, the
functional derivative $\delta L/\delta\dot\Psi^\ell$ is equal to the canonical
conjugate $P_n$, while when $\Psi^\ell$ is an auxiliary field $C^r$, this
functional derivative vanishes; hence we may rewrite Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.11} in the form
\begin{equation}
F=-\ii\int \dd^3x\,P_n(\mathbf{x},t)\mathcal{F}^n(\mathbf{x},t)
=-\ii\int \dd^3x\int \dd^3y\,P_n(\mathbf{x},t)
\mathcal{F}^n[Q(t);\mathbf{x}].
\tag{7.3.17}\label{eq:7.3.17}
\end{equation}
To calculate the commutator (not anticommutator) of $F$ with a canonical field
$Q^m(\mathbf{x},t)$ at an arbitrary time $t$, we can invoke Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.6} to
evaluate $F$ as a functional of the $Q$s and $P$s at the time $t$, and then use
the equal-time canonical commutation relations~\eqref{eq:7.1.30}--\eqref{eq:7.1.32} to
obtain\footnote{We are here assuming that for $Q^n$ bosonic or fermionic the
variation $\mathcal{F}^n$ is also respectively bosonic or fermionic, so that
$F$ is bosonic. The only exceptions are certain symmetries known as
supersymmetries, for which $F$ is fermionic and \eqref{eq:7.3.18} is an anticommutator if
$Q^n$ is also fermionic.}
\begin{equation}
[F,Q^m(\mathbf{x},t)]=-\mathcal{F}^m(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.18}\label{eq:7.3.18}
\end{equation}
It is in this sense that $F$ is the generator of the transformation with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.16}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.17} and the canonical commutation rules give also
\begin{equation}
[F,P_n(\mathbf{x},t)]
=\int \dd^3y\,P_m(\mathbf{y},t)
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}^m[Q(t);\mathbf{y}]}
{\delta Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)}.
\tag{7.3.19}\label{eq:7.3.19}
\end{equation}
Where $\mathcal{F}^m$ is linear, Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.19} tells us that $P_n$ transforms
contragrediently to $Q^n$.

As a first example, consider the symmetry transformation of spacetime
translation:
\begin{equation}
\Psi^\ell(x)\longrightarrow\Psi^\ell(x+\epsilon)
=\Psi^\ell(x)+\epsilon^\mu\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x).
\tag{7.3.20}\label{eq:7.3.20}
\end{equation}
This is of the form \eqref{eq:7.3.1}, with four independent parameters $\epsilon^\mu$
and four corresponding transformation functions
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}^\ell_\mu=-\ii\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell.
\tag{7.3.21}\label{eq:7.3.21}
\end{equation}
In consequence we have four independent conserved currents, conventionally
grouped together in the \emph{energy--momentum tensor} $T^\mu{}_\nu$:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\mu T^\mu{}_\nu=0
\tag{7.3.22}\label{eq:7.3.22}
\end{equation}
from which we can derive time-independent quantities as the spatial integrals
of the time components of the translation `currents' (not to be confused with
the canonical conjugate field variables $P_n(\mathbf{x},t)$):
\begin{equation}
P_\nu=\int \dd^3x\,T^0{}_\nu,
\tag{7.3.23}\label{eq:7.3.23}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}P_\nu=0.
\tag{7.3.24}\label{eq:7.3.24}
\end{equation}
The Lagrangian is invariant under spatial translations, so in accordance with
the above general results we can conclude that the spatial components of
$P_\nu$ take the form
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}=-\int \dd^3x\,P_n(\mathbf{x},t)\boldsymbol{\nabla}Q^n(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.25}\label{eq:7.3.25}
\end{equation}
Using the equal-time commutation relations~\eqref{eq:7.1.30}--\eqref{eq:7.1.32}, we also find the
commutator of this operator with the canonical fields and conjugates:
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{P},Q^n(\mathbf{x},t)]=\ii\boldsymbol{\nabla}Q^n(\mathbf{x},t),
\tag{7.3.26}\label{eq:7.3.26}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{P},P_n(\mathbf{x},t)]=\ii\boldsymbol{\nabla}P_n(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.27}\label{eq:7.3.27}
\end{equation}
It follows that for any function $\mathcal{G}$ of $Q$s and $P$s that does not
also depend explicitly on $\mathbf{x}$, we have
\begin{equation}
[\mathbf{P},\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x})]=\ii\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}).
\tag{7.3.28}\label{eq:7.3.28}
\end{equation}
These results show that the operator $\mathbf{P}$ is indeed the generator of
space translations.

In contrast, time-translations do not leave the Lagrangian $L(t)$ invariant.
However, we already know the generator of time-translations; it is the
Hamiltonian $P^0\equiv H$, which as we know satisfies the commutation relation
\begin{equation}
[H,\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x},t)]=-\ii\dot{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x},t)
\tag{7.3.29}\label{eq:7.3.29}
\end{equation}
for any function $\mathcal{G}$ of Heisenberg picture operators.

If we further assume that the Lagrangian is the integral of a Lagrangian
density, then we may also obtain an explicit formula for the energy--momentum
tensor $T^\mu{}_\nu$. However, the Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L}(x)$ is not
invariant under spacetime translations, so we cannot use Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.15} here.
Instead, note that the change in the action under a spacetime-dependent
translation
\begin{equation}
\Psi^\ell(x)\longrightarrow\Psi^\ell(x+\epsilon(x))
=\Psi^\ell(x)+\epsilon^\mu(x)\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell(x)
\tag{7.3.30}\label{eq:7.3.30}
\end{equation}
is
\begin{equation}
\delta I[\Psi]=\int \dd^4x\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Psi^\ell}\epsilon^\mu\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell
+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\nu\Psi^\ell)}
\partial_\nu[\epsilon^\mu\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell]\right).
\tag{7.3.31}\label{eq:7.3.31}
\end{equation}
The Euler--Lagrange equations~\eqref{eq:7.2.9} show that the terms proportional to
$\epsilon$ add up to $\epsilon^\mu\partial_\mu\mathcal{L}$, so
\begin{equation}
\delta I[\Psi]=\int \dd^4x\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x^\mu}\epsilon^\mu
+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\nu\Psi^\ell)}
\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell\,\partial_\nu\epsilon^\mu\right).
\tag{7.3.32}\label{eq:7.3.32}
\end{equation}
Integrating by parts, we see that this takes the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.4}
\begin{equation}
\delta I=-\int \dd^4x\,T^\nu{}_\mu\partial_\nu\epsilon^\mu
\tag{7.3.33}\label{eq:7.3.33}
\end{equation}
with `currents'
\begin{equation}
T^\nu{}_\mu=\delta^\nu{}_\mu\mathcal{L}
-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\nu\Psi^\ell)}
\partial_\mu\Psi^\ell.
\tag{7.3.34}\label{eq:7.3.34}
\end{equation}
As a check, we may note that the spatial components of Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.23} are the
same as our previous formula~\eqref{eq:7.3.25} for $\mathbf{P}$, while for $\mu=0$
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.23} gives the usual formula for the Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}
H\equiv-P_0=\int \dd^3x\left[\sum_nP_n\dot Q^n-\mathcal{L}\right].
\tag{7.3.35}\label{eq:7.3.35}
\end{equation}
(A warning: the tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$ obtained by raising the second index in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.34} is not in general symmetric, and therefore cannot be used as the
right-hand side of the field equations of general relativity. The correct
energy--momentum tensor to use as the source of the gravitational field is the
symmetric tensor $\Theta^{\mu\nu}$ introduced in the next section.)

In many theories there are also one or more symmetry principles that state the
invariance of the action, under a set of linear coordinate-independent
transformations of the canonical fields
\begin{equation}
Q^n(x)\longrightarrow Q^n(x)+\ii\epsilon^a(t_a)^n{}_mQ^m(x)
\tag{7.3.36}\label{eq:7.3.36}
\end{equation}
together with a set of suitable transformations on any auxiliary fields $C^r$:
\begin{equation}
C^r(x)\longrightarrow C^r(x)+\ii\epsilon^a(\tau_a)^r{}_sC^s(x).
\tag{7.3.37}\label{eq:7.3.37}
\end{equation}
Here $t_a$ and $\tau_a$ are sets of Hermitian matrices furnishing some
representations of the Lie algebra of the symmetry group, and we sum over
repeated group indices $a$, $b$, etc. (For instance, in electrodynamics there
is such a symmetry, for which the one matrix $t^n{}_m$ is diagonal, with the
charges carried by each field on the main diagonal.) From any such symmetry,
we can infer the existence of another set of conserved currents $J_a^\mu$:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\mu J_a^\mu=0,
\tag{7.3.38}\label{eq:7.3.38}
\end{equation}
whose time components are the densities of a set of time-independent operators
\begin{equation}
T_a\equiv\int \dd^3x\,J_a^0.
\tag{7.3.39}\label{eq:7.3.39}
\end{equation}
When the Lagrangian as well as the action is invariant under the transformation
\eqref{eq:7.3.36}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.11} provides an explicit formula for the $T_a$:
\begin{equation}
T_a=-\ii\int \dd^3x\,P_n(\mathbf{x},t)(t_a)^n{}_mQ^m(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.40}\label{eq:7.3.40}
\end{equation}
The equal-time commutation relations here give
\begin{equation}
[T_a,Q^n(x)]=-(t_a)^n{}_mQ^m(x),
\tag{7.3.41}\label{eq:7.3.41}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[T_a,P_n(x)]=+(t_a)^m{}_nP_m(x).
\tag{7.3.42}\label{eq:7.3.42}
\end{equation}
(Where $t_a$ is diagonal, this tells us that $Q^n$ and $P_n$ respectively
lower and raise the value of $T_a$ by an amount equal to the $n$th diagonal
element of $t_a$.) Using these results, we can calculate the commutator of
$T_a$ with another generator $T_b$:
\begin{equation}
[T_a,T_b] =\ii\int \dd^3x\left[
-P_m(t_a)^m{}_n(t_b)^n{}_kQ^k
+P_n(t_b)^n{}_k(t_a)^k{}_mQ^m\right].
\tag{7.3.43}\label{eq:7.3.43}
\end{equation}
Thus, if the matrices $t_a$ form a Lie algebra with structure constants
$f_{ab}{}^c$,
\begin{equation}
[t_a,t_b] =if_{ab}{}^ct_c,
\tag{7.3.44}\label{eq:7.3.44}
\end{equation}
then so do the quantum operators $T_a$:
\begin{equation}
[T_a,T_b] =if_{ab}{}^cT_c.
\tag{7.3.45}\label{eq:7.3.45}
\end{equation}
This confirms that the quantities \eqref{eq:7.3.40} are correctly normalized to qualify
as generators of the symmetry group.

Where the Lagrangian is the integral of a Lagrangian density which is invariant
under \eqref{eq:7.3.36} and \eqref{eq:7.3.37} we can go further, and use Eq.~\eqref{eq:7.3.15} to provide
an explicit formula for the currents associated with these global symmetries:
\begin{equation}
J_a^\mu=-\ii\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}
{\partial(\partial Q^n/\partial x^\mu)}(t_a)^n{}_mQ^m
-\ii\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}
{\partial(\partial C^r/\partial x^\mu)}(\tau_a)^r{}_sC^s.
\tag{7.3.46}\label{eq:7.3.46}
\end{equation}
As an illustration, suppose we have two real scalar fields of equal mass, with
Lagrangian density
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=-\frac12\partial_\mu\Phi_1\partial^\mu\Phi_1
-\frac12m^2\Phi_1^2
-\frac12\partial_\mu\Phi_2\partial^\mu\Phi_2
-\frac12m^2\Phi_2^2-\mathcal{H}_I(\Phi_1^2+\Phi_2^2).
\tag{7.3.47}\label{eq:7.3.47}
\end{equation}
This is invariant under a linear transformation like \eqref{eq:7.3.36}:
\[
\delta\Phi_1=-\epsilon\Phi_2,
\qquad
\delta\Phi_2=+\epsilon\Phi_1,
\]
so there is a conserved current \eqref{eq:7.3.46}:
\[
J^\mu=\Phi_2\partial^\mu\Phi_1-\Phi_1\partial^\mu\Phi_2.
\]
The explicit formula~\eqref{eq:7.3.46} for the current can be used to derive other
useful commutation relations. In particular, since the Lagrangian density does
not involve time-derivatives of the auxiliary fields, we have
\begin{equation}
J_a^0=-\ii P_n(t_a)^n{}_mQ^m.
\tag{7.3.48}\label{eq:7.3.48}
\end{equation}
We can then derive the equal-time commutators of general fields not only with
the symmetry generators $T_a$, but also with the densities $J_a^0$:
\begin{equation}
[J_a^0(\mathbf{x},t),Q^n(\mathbf{y},t)]
=-\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})(t_a)^n{}_mQ^m(\mathbf{x},t),
\tag{7.3.49}\label{eq:7.3.49}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[J_a^0(\mathbf{x},t),P_m(\mathbf{y},t)]
=\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})(t_a)^n{}_mP_n(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.50}\label{eq:7.3.50}
\end{equation}
If the auxiliary fields are constructed as local functions of the $P$s and
$Q$s in such a way that they transform according to a representation of the
symmetry algebra with generators $\tau_a$, then also
\begin{equation}
[J_a^0(\mathbf{x},t),C^r(\mathbf{y},t)]
=-\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})(\tau_a)^r{}_sC^s(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.51}\label{eq:7.3.51}
\end{equation}
We often summarize Eqs.~\eqref{eq:7.3.49} and \eqref{eq:7.3.51} in the single commutation
relation
\begin{equation}
[J_a^0(\mathbf{x},t),\Psi^\ell(\mathbf{y},t)]
=-\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})(t_a)^\ell{}_{\ell'}
\Psi^{\ell'}(\mathbf{x},t).
\tag{7.3.52}\label{eq:7.3.52}
\end{equation}
Commutation relations like \eqref{eq:7.3.49}--\eqref{eq:7.3.51} will be used in Chapter 10 to
derive relations called Ward identities for matrix elements involving the
current $J^\mu$.
