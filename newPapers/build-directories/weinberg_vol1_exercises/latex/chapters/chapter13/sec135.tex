\subsection[Soft Photon Scattering]{Soft Photon Scattering\texorpdfstring{\protect\footnotemark}{}}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of
development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}

In our treatment in this chapter of soft photon interactions, we have up
to now considered only processes in which the soft photons are emitted
or absorbed in a process $\alpha\to\beta$ which was going on anyway. It is also
possible to make useful general statements about processes in which the
process $\alpha\to\beta$ is trivial, and the soft photons play an essential part in
producing an interesting reaction. We will consider here the simplest
and most important example of this sort, the scattering of a soft photon
from a massive particle of arbitrary type and spin, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are
single-particle states. The complication here is that the leading term in the
soft photon scattering amplitude does not come from the pole terms, but
from non-pole terms that are related to the pole terms by the condition
of current conservation.

The $S$-matrix for photon scattering may be put in the form
\begin{align}
S(q,\lambda;p,\sigma\to q',\lambda';p',\sigma')={}&\ii(2\pi)^4
\delta^4(q+p-q'-p')\notag\\
&\cdot\frac{\epsilon^*_{\nu}(q',\lambda')\epsilon_{\mu}(q,\lambda)
M^{\nu\mu}_{\sigma',\sigma}(q;p',p)}
{(2\pi)^6\sqrt{4q^0q'^0}}\,.
\tag{13.5.1}\label{eq:13.5.1}
\end{align}
where $q$ and $q'$ are the initial and final photon four-momenta, $p$ and $p'$
are the initial and final target four-momenta, $\lambda$ and $\lambda'$ are the initial and
final photon helicities, $\epsilon_\nu(q',\lambda')$ and $\epsilon_\mu(q,\lambda)$ are the corresponding photon
polarization vectors, and $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ are the initial and final target spin
$z$-components. According to the theorem of Section 6.4, the amplitude
$M^{\nu\mu}$ may be expressed as
\begin{equation}
(2\pi)^{-3}M^{\nu\mu}_{\sigma',\sigma}(q;p',p)
=\int \dd^4x\,e^{\ii q\cdot x}
\bra{p',\sigma'}T\{J^\nu(0),J^\mu(x)\}\ket{p,\sigma}+\cdots.
\tag{13.5.2}\label{eq:13.5.2}
\end{equation}
where $J^\mu(x)$ is the electromagnetic current, and the dots indicate possible
`seagull' terms such as those in the theory of charged scalar fields in
which the two photons interact at a single vertex instead of with separate
currents. We now repeat the standard polology arguments described
in Chapter 10 and already used in Section 13.1. Inserting a complete
set of intermediate states between the current operators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.2},
integrating over $x$ and isolating the one-particle intermediate states gives
\begin{align}
M^{\nu\mu}(q;p',p)={}&
\frac{G^\nu(p',p+q)G^\mu(p+q,p)}
{E(p+q)-E(p)-q^0-\ii\epsilon}\notag\\
&+\frac{G^\mu(p',p'-q)G^\nu(p'-q,p)}
{E(p'-q)-E(p')+q^0-\ii\epsilon}
+N^{\nu\mu}(q;p',p),
\tag{13.5.3}\label{eq:13.5.3}
\end{align}
where $G^\mu$ is the one-particle matrix element of the current
\begin{equation}
(2\pi)^{-3}G^\mu_{\sigma',\sigma}(p',p)
\equiv\bra{p',\sigma'}J^\mu(0)\ket{p,\sigma}
\tag{13.5.4}\label{eq:13.5.4}
\end{equation}
and $N^{\nu\mu}$ represents the contribution of states other than the one-particle
state itself, plus any seagull terms. (Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.3} is to be understood in
the sense of matrix multiplication, with spin indices not shown explicitly.)
About $N^{\nu\mu}$ we know very little, except that it does not have the singularity
at $q^\mu\to0$ exhibited by the first two terms, and therefore may be expanded
in powers of $q^\mu$.

We now use the current conservation (or gauge invariance) conditions:
\begin{equation}
q_\mu M^{\nu\mu}(q;p',p)=0,
\tag{13.5.5}\label{eq:13.5.5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf G(p+q,p)
=\bigl[E(p+q)-E(p)\bigr]G^0(p+q,p),
\tag{13.5.6}\label{eq:13.5.6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf G(p',p'-q)
=\bigl[E(p')-E(p'-q)\bigr]G^0(p',p'-q).
\tag{13.5.7}\label{eq:13.5.7}
\end{equation}
Applied to Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.3}, these conditions yield the condition we need on
$N^{\nu\mu}$:
\begin{align}
q_\mu N^{\nu\mu}(q;p',p)={}&-G^\nu(p',p+q)G^0(p+q,p)
\notag\\
&+G^0(p',p'-q)G^\nu(p'-q,p).
\tag{13.5.8}\label{eq:13.5.8}
\end{align}
We also note that $M^{\nu\mu}$ satisfies the `crossing symmetry' condition
\begin{equation}
M^{\nu\mu}(q;p',p)=M^{\mu\nu}(p'-p-q;p',p)
\tag{13.5.9}\label{eq:13.5.9}
\end{equation}
and since the pole terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.3} evidently satisfy this condition, so
also does $N^{\nu\mu}$:
\begin{equation}
N^{\nu\mu}(q;p',p)=N^{\mu\nu}(p'-p-q;p',p).
\tag{13.5.10}\label{eq:13.5.10}
\end{equation}
We will use these conditions to determine the first terms in the expansion
of $N^{\nu\mu}$ in powers of momenta.

First we need to say something about the expansion of the one-particle
current matrix elements $G^\mu(p',p)$ in powers of the momenta $p'$ and $p$.
Space inversion invariance (to the extent that it is applicable) tells us that
the expansion of $G^0$ and $G^i$ (with $i=1,2,3$) contain terms respectively of
only even and odd order in the momenta. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.6.3}, the
term in $G^0_{\sigma',\sigma}$ of zeroth order in momenta is $e\delta_{\sigma',\sigma}$, where $e$ is the particle
charge. The current conservation condition then tells us that to second
order in momenta,
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf G_{\sigma',\sigma}(\mathbf p',\mathbf p)
=\left(\frac{\mathbf p'^2}{2m}-\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}\right)e\delta_{\sigma',\sigma}.
\end{equation*}
The terms in $\mathbf G$ of first order in momenta are thus given by
$e(\mathbf p'+\mathbf p)\delta_{\sigma',\sigma}/2m$, plus a possible first-order term orthogonal to
$\mathbf p'-\mathbf p$, which rotational invariance tells us must be proportional to
$(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)\times\mathbf J_{\sigma',\sigma}$, where $\mathbf J$ is the familiar spin matrix of the charged particle. Summarizing these results,
we have the expansions
\begin{equation}
G^0(\mathbf p',\mathbf p)=e\mathbf{1}+\text{quadratic},
\tag{13.5.11}\label{eq:13.5.11}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf G(\mathbf p',\mathbf p)
=\frac{e\mathbf{1}}{2m}(\mathbf p'+\mathbf p)
+\frac{\ii\mu}{j}\,\mathbf J\times(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p)+\text{cubic},
\tag{13.5.12}\label{eq:13.5.12}
\end{equation}
where `$\mathbf{1}$' is the unit spin matrix, and `quadratic' and `cubic' refer to the
order of the neglected terms in powers of the small momenta $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf p'$. The
coefficient $\mu/j$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.12} is real because the current is Hermitian.
With the coefficient written in this way (with $j$ the spin of the charged
particle), $\mu$ is the quantity known as the magnetic moment of the particle.

Now let us return to $N^{\nu\mu}$, and consider the expansion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.8} in
powers of the small momenta $q^\mu$, $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf p'$. Taking $\nu=0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.8}
shows that $q_\mu N^{0\mu}$ is at least quadratic in these small quantities. There is
no constant vector orthogonal to $q^\mu$, so $N^{0\mu}$ must be at least of first order
in small momenta. The crossing symmetry condition \eqref{eq:13.5.10} then tells
us that $N^{\mu0}$ must also be at least of first order in small momenta. Taking
$\mu=\ii$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.8} and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.3.12} then tells us that
\begin{equation*}
q_kN^{ik}=-\frac{e^2q^i}{m}+\text{quadratic}
\end{equation*}
and hence
\begin{equation}
N^{ik}=-\frac{e^2}{m}\delta_{ik}+\text{linear}.
\tag{13.5.13}\label{eq:13.5.13}
\end{equation}
Since $G^i$ is at least of first order in the small momenta, so are the pole
terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:13.5.3} for $M^{ik}$, leaving us in zero order with only the \emph{non-pole}
term $N^{ik}$:
\begin{equation}
M^{ik}(0;0,0)=N^{ik}(0;0,0)=-\frac{e^2}{m}\delta_{ik}.
\tag{13.5.14}\label{eq:13.5.14}
\end{equation}

From this we can calculate the soft photon scattering cross-section. But
there is no need for this calculation; now that we know that the photon
scattering amplitude in the limit of zero momentum depends only on the
target particle mass and charge, and is of second order in the charge,
we can immediately use the results of any second-order calculation of the
photon scattering cross-section for target particles of any given spin, such
as our result \eqref{eq:8.7.42} for the differential photon scattering cross-section in
quantum electrodynamics:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}=\frac{e^4}{32\pi^2m^2}(1+\cos^2\theta).
\tag{13.5.15}\label{eq:13.5.15}
\end{equation}
We now see that this is a universal formula, valid in the low-energy limit
for target particles of mass $m$ and charge $e$ and of arbitrary type and
spin, even if these particles are composite and strongly interacting, like
atomic nuclei. Gell-Mann and Goldberger and Low~\hyperref[ref:13.8]{[8]} have shown that
these results may be extended to give the next-to-leading term in the soft
photon scattering amplitude in terms of the target particle's mass, charge,
and magnetic moment.
