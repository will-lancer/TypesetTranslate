\subsection{Radiative Corrections in External Fields}

We now consider radiative corrections to the results of the previous section,
due to the interaction of electrons with the quantum electromagnetic field
as well as the external field of the heavy charged particles. These radiative
corrections can be calculated using Feynman diagrams of the usual sort,
with the whole effect of the external field being to modify the propagator
of the electron field in the presence of the external electromagnetic field
(and to supply the external-field-dependent renormalization counterterms
shown in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.1.1}). To be specific, the effect of inserting any number
of vertices corresponding to the first term of the interaction \eqref{eq:14.1.1} in an
internal electron line of any graph is to replace the bare coordinate space
propagator $-iS(x-y)$ with a corrected propagator
\begin{align}
 -iS_{\mathcal A}(x,y)\equiv{}&-iS(x-y)
 +(-i)^2\int d^4z_1\,S(x-z_1)e\gamma^\mu\mathcal A_\mu(z_1)S(z_1-y)
 \notag\\
 &+(-i)^3\int d^4z_1\int d^4z_2\,
 S(x-z_1)e\gamma^\mu\mathcal A_\mu(z_1)S(z_1-z_2)
 \notag\\
 &\quad\cdot e\gamma^\nu\mathcal A_\nu(z_2)S(z_2-y)+\cdots ,
 \tag{14.2.1}\label{eq:14.2.1}
\end{align}
where as usual
\[
 S(x-y)\equiv\frac{1}{(2\pi)^4}\int d^4p\,
 \frac{-i\sl{p}+m}{p^2+m^2-i\epsilon}\,e^{ip\cdot(x-y)}.
\]
(We must write $S_{\mathcal A}$ as a function of $x$ and $y$ rather than of
$x-y$, because the external field invalidates translation invariance.) The
theorem proved in Section 6.4 tells us that Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.1} is the same as
\begin{equation}
 -iS_{\mathcal A}(x,y)
 ={}_{\mathcal A}\!\bra{\Omega}T\{\psi(x),\bar\psi(y)\}\ket{\Omega}_{\mathcal A}.
 \tag{14.2.2}\label{eq:14.2.2}
\end{equation}
with the subscript $\mathcal A$ on the right indicating that the vacuum state
$\ket{\Omega}_{\mathcal A}$ and electron field $\psi(x)$ are to be defined in a
Heisenberg picture in which the only interaction taken into account is the
interaction \eqref{eq:14.1.1} with the external field. Inserting a complete set of
intermediate states $\ket{N}_{\mathcal A}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.2} yields an
expression for the propagator in terms of the Dirac wave functions $u_N$ and
$v_N$ introduced in the previous section
\begin{equation}
 -iS_{\mathcal A}(x,y)=\theta(x^0-y^0)\sum_Nu_N(x)\bar u_N(y)
 -\theta(y^0-x^0)\sum_Mv_M(x)\bar v_M(y).
 \tag{14.2.3}\label{eq:14.2.3}
\end{equation}
It is also possible to obtain the propagator \eqref{eq:14.2.2} as the solution of
the \emph{inhomogeneous Dirac equation}:
\begin{equation}
 \left[\gamma^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}+m
 +ie\gamma^\mu\mathcal A_\mu(x)\right]S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)=\delta^4(x-y),
 \tag{14.2.4}\label{eq:14.2.4}
\end{equation}
which follows from the field equations \eqref{eq:14.1.3} and anticommutation
relations \eqref{eq:14.1.7}, or formally from the perturbation series
\eqref{eq:14.2.1}. Also Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.3} tells us that the propagator satisfies
boundary conditions; its Fourier decomposition contains only `positive
frequency terms' proportional to $\exp\{-iE(x^0-y^0)\}$ with $E>0$ for
$x^0-y^0\to\infty$, and only `negative frequency terms' proportional to
$\exp\{+iE(x^0-y^0)\}$ with $E>0$ for $x^0-y^0\to-\infty$. The inhomogeneous
Dirac equation with these boundary conditions may be used to obtain a
numerical solution~\hyperlink{ref:14.4}{[4]} for this propagator even in cases
where the external field is too strong to allow the use of the perturbation
series \eqref{eq:14.2.1}. Once the propagator $S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$ has been
calculated, the amplitudes for scattering in an external field can be
calculated using ordinary Feynman diagrams, but with $S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$ in
place of $S(x-y)$ (and with $\mathcal A$-dependent renormalization counterterms
inserted where appropriate).

Now let us see how to use the perturbation series with this corrected
propagator to calculate the shifts of bound-state energy levels. Consider the
full electron propagator $S'_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$, involving interactions of the
electron with the quantum electromagnetic field as well as the external field:
\begin{equation}
 -iS'_{\mathcal A}(x,y)
 ={}_{\mathcal A}\!\bra{\Omega}T\{\Psi(x),\bar\Psi(y)\}\ket{\Omega}_{\mathcal A}
 \tag{14.2.5}\label{eq:14.2.5}
\end{equation}
with $\Psi(x)$ the electron field in a Heisenberg picture including all
interactions, and $\ket{\Omega}$ the vacuum eigenstate of the full Hamiltonian.
For a time-independent external potential we can find a complete orthonormal
set $\ket{N}$ of eigenstates of the full Hamiltonian with energies $E'_N$.
Inserting a sum over these states in the operator product in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.5}, we find
\begin{align}
 -iS'_{\mathcal A}(x,y)={}&\theta(x^0-y^0)e^{-iE'_N(x^0-y^0)}
 \sum_NU_N(\mathbf x)\bar U_N(\mathbf y)
 \notag\\
 &-\theta(y^0-x^0)e^{-iE'_N(y^0-x^0)}
 \sum_NV_N(\mathbf x)\bar V_N(\mathbf y),
 \tag{14.2.6}\label{eq:14.2.6}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}\Psi(\mathbf x,t)\ket{N}\equiv e^{-iE'_Nt}U_N(\mathbf x),
 \tag{14.2.7}\label{eq:14.2.7}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \bra{N}\bar\Psi(\mathbf x,t)\ket{\Omega}\equiv e^{+iE'_Nt}\bar V_N(\mathbf x).
 \tag{14.2.8}\label{eq:14.2.8}
\end{equation}
(The sum includes an integral over continuum states as well as a sum over
discrete bound states. As before, $U_N$ and $V_N$ are non-zero only if the state
$\ket{N}$ has charge $-e$ or $+e$, respectively.) We can redefine the
propagator as a function of energy rather than time
\begin{equation}
 S'_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)
 \equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^0\,e^{iE(x^0-y^0)}S'_{\mathcal A}(x,y).
 \tag{14.2.9}\label{eq:14.2.9}
\end{equation}
(Time-translation invariance dictates that $S'_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$ is a function
of $x^0-y^0$ but not of $x^0$ and $y^0$ separately.) From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.6} we see that
\begin{equation}
 S'_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)
 =\sum_N\frac{U_N(\mathbf x)\bar U_N(\mathbf y)}{E'_N-E-i\epsilon}
 -\sum_N\frac{V_N(\mathbf x)\bar V_N(\mathbf y)}{E'_N+E-i\epsilon}.
 \tag{14.2.10}\label{eq:14.2.10}
\end{equation}
In particular, $S'_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)$ has a pole at any
electron bound-state energy, and also at the negative of any positron
bound-state energy. (Of course, positrons do not have bound states in the
Coulomb field of an ordinary positively charged nucleus.)

Let us now consider the lowest-order radiative corrections to the complete
propagator. The Feynman rules here give the complete propagator to this order as
$S'_{\mathcal A}=S_{\mathcal A}+\delta S_{\mathcal A}$, with a correction term
\begin{equation}
 \delta S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)=\int d^4z\int d^4w\,
 S_{\mathcal A}(x,z)\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(z,w)S_{\mathcal A}(w,y),
 \tag{14.2.11}\label{eq:14.2.11}
\end{equation}
where $i\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}$ is the sum of all one-loop diagrams with one
incoming and one outgoing electron line (excluding final electron propagators)
calculated using $S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$ in place of $S(x-y)$ for internal
electron lines, plus second-order renormalization counterterms. Using energy
variables in place of time variables, this is
\begin{equation}
 \delta S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)=\int d^3z\int d^3w\,
 S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf z;E)\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf z,\mathbf w;E)
 S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf w,\mathbf y;E),
 \tag{14.2.12}\label{eq:14.2.12}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf z,\mathbf w;E)
 \equiv\int dz^0\,e^{iE(z^0-w^0)}\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(z,w).
 \tag{14.2.13}\label{eq:14.2.13}
\end{equation}

The effect of these radiative corrections is to change the wave functions to
$U_N=u_N+\delta u_N$ and $V_N=v_N+\delta v_N$ and the bound-state energies to
$E'_N=E_N+\delta E_N$, so that the complete propagator is
\begin{align}
 S'_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)\simeq{}&S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)
 \notag\\
 &+\sum_N\frac{\delta u_N(\mathbf x)\bar u_N(\mathbf y)
 +u_N(\mathbf x)\delta\bar u_N(\mathbf y)}{E_N-E}
 \notag\\
 &-\sum_N\frac{\delta v_N(\mathbf x)\bar v_N(\mathbf y)
 +v_N(\mathbf x)\delta\bar v_N(\mathbf y)}{E_N+E}
 \notag\\
 &-\sum_N\frac{u_N(\mathbf x)\bar u_N(\mathbf y)\delta E_N}{(E_N-E)^2}
 +\sum_N\frac{v_N(\mathbf x)\bar v_N(\mathbf y)\delta E_N}{(E_N+E)^2}.
 \tag{14.2.14}\label{eq:14.2.14}
\end{align}
(We are dropping the $i\epsilon$ terms because we are now not taking $E$ to lie
in the continuum of scattering states.) We see that the shift $\delta E_N$ of
an electron bound-state energy is given by the coefficient of
$-u_N(\mathbf x)\bar u_N(\mathbf y)/(E_N-E)^2$ in the complete propagator. To
calculate this, we note that Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.3} gives
\begin{equation}
 S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)
 =\sum_N\frac{u_N(\mathbf x)\bar u_N(\mathbf y)}{E_N-E-i\epsilon}
 -\sum_N\frac{v_N(\mathbf x)\bar v_N(\mathbf y)}{E_N+E-i\epsilon}.
 \tag{14.2.15}\label{eq:14.2.15}
\end{equation}
Inserting this in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.12} gives
\begin{align}
 \delta S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E)={}&
 \sum_{N,M}\frac{u_N(\mathbf x)\bar u_M(\mathbf y)}{(E_N-E)(E_M-E)}
 \notag\\[-2pt]
 &\quad\cdot\int d^3z\int d^3w\,
 \bar u_N(\mathbf z)\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf z,\mathbf w;E)
 u_M(\mathbf w)+\cdots .
 \tag{14.2.16}\label{eq:14.2.16}
\end{align}

where the dots denote additional terms involving at least one negative-energy
pole. Comparing the coefficients of $(E_N-E)^{-2}$ here and in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.14}, we find
\begin{equation}
 \delta E_N=-\int d^3x\int d^3y\,
 \bar u_N(\mathbf x)\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;E_N)u_N(\mathbf y).
 \tag{14.2.17}\label{eq:14.2.17}
\end{equation}
The $u_N$ are solutions of the homogeneous Dirac equation satisfying the
normalization condition \eqref{eq:14.1.8}, so this is very much like ordinary
first-order perturbation theory, but with $-\Sigma^*$ in place of a perturbation
to the Hamiltonian.

Generally $\delta E_N$ turns out to be complex. This is simply a consequence
of the instability of atomic energy levels to radiative decay to lower levels;
we saw in Chapter 3 that an unstable state of energy $E$ and decay rate $\Gamma$
produces poles in various amplitudes at the complex energy $E-i\Gamma/2$. The
imaginary part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.17} therefore equals $-\Gamma/2$, while its
real part gives the energy shift.

The Feynman diagrams for $\Sigma^*$ are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:14.1}. (Note
that there are new photon tadpole diagrams here because the external field
breaks the Lorentz invariance and charge-conjugation invariance that forbids
such diagrams in the ordinary Feynman rules.) Application of the position
space Feynman rules to these diagrams gives
\begin{align}
 i\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(x,y)={}&
 [e\gamma^\mu][-iS_{\mathcal A}(x,y)][e\gamma_\mu][-iD(x-y)]
 \notag\\
 &-[e\gamma^\mu\delta^4(x-y)]\int d^4z\,[-iD(x-z)]
 \operatorname{Tr}\{[-iS_{\mathcal A}(z,z)][e\gamma_\mu]\}
 \notag\\
 &-i(Z_2-1)(\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu+m)\delta^4(x-y)
 +i\delta m\,\delta^4(x-y)
 \notag\\
 &+e\gamma^\mu(Z_2-1)\delta^4(x-y)\mathcal A_\mu(x)
 \notag\\
 &+i(Z_3-1)[e\gamma_\mu]\delta^4(x-y)\int d^4z\,[-iD(x-z)]
 \partial_\nu\bigl(\partial^\nu\mathcal A^\mu(z)-\partial^\mu\mathcal A^\nu(z)\bigr).
 \tag{14.2.18}\label{eq:14.2.18}
\end{align}
with the renormalization constants $(Z_2-1)$, $(Z_3-1)$, and $\delta m$
calculated to second order in $e$. (The minus sign in the second term is the
usual one that accompanies closed fermion loops.)

For strong external fields with $Z\alpha$ of order unity it is necessary to
calculate the configuration space electron propagator $S_{\mathcal A}$ and the
integrals in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.2.17} and \eqref{eq:14.2.18}
numerically~\hyperlink{ref:14.4}{[4]}. However, for weak fields we can use the
first few terms of the series \eqref{eq:14.2.1} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:14.2.18} and
calculate these integrals in closed form. For this purpose, it is more
convenient to work in momentum space, defining:
\begin{equation}
 S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4p'\,d^4p\,
 e^{ip'\cdot x}e^{-ip\cdot y}S_{\mathcal A}(p',p),
 \tag{14.2.19}\label{eq:14.2.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int d^4p'\,d^4p\,
 e^{ip'\cdot x}e^{-ip\cdot y}\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(p',p),
 \tag{14.2.20}\label{eq:14.2.20}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 u_N(\mathbf x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int d^3p\,e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}u_N(\mathbf p),
 \tag{14.2.21}\label{eq:14.2.21}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal A^\mu(x)=\int d^4q\,e^{iq\cdot x}\mathcal A^\mu(q).
 \tag{14.2.22}\label{eq:14.2.22}
\end{equation}
(We are here committing the impropriety of using the same symbol for a function
and its Fourier transform, leaving it to the displayed arguments of the
functions to indicate which is which.) Then Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.2.1} and
\eqref{eq:14.2.18} become
\begin{align}
 S_{\mathcal A}(p',p)={}&\frac{-i\sl{p}'+m}{p'^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
 -ie\frac{-i\sl{p}'+m}{p'^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
 \sl{\mathcal A}(p'-p)\frac{-i\sl{p}+m}{p^2+m^2-i\epsilon}
 +\cdots .
 \tag{14.2.23}\label{eq:14.2.23}
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
 \Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(p',p)={}&
 \frac{ie^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int\frac{d^4k}{k^2-i\epsilon}\,
 \gamma^\mu S_{\mathcal A}(p'-k,p-k)\gamma_\mu
 \notag\\
 &+\bigl[-(Z_2-1)(i\sl{p}+m)+Z_2\delta m\bigr]\delta^4(p'-p)
 -ie(Z_2-1)\sl{\mathcal A}(p'-p)
 \notag\\
 &-\frac{ie^2\gamma^\mu}{(2\pi)^4[(p-p')^2-i\epsilon]}
 \int d^4q\,\operatorname{Tr}\{S_{\mathcal A}(q,q+p'-p)\gamma_\mu\}
 \notag\\
 &+\frac{ie(Z_3-1)}{(p-p')^2-i\epsilon}
 \left[(p-p')^2\sl{\mathcal A}(p'-p)
 -(\sl{p}-\sl{p}')(p-p')\cdot\mathcal A(p'-p)\right].
 \tag{14.2.24}\label{eq:14.2.24}
\end{align}
Because the external field is time-independent, $S_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$ and
$\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(x,y)$ can depend on $x^0$ and $y^0$ only through the
difference $x^0-y^0$, so $S_{\mathcal A}(p',p)$ and
$\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(p',p)$ as well as $\mathcal A^\mu(p'-p)$ must be
proportional to $\delta(p'^0-p^0)$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal A^\mu(p'-p)=\delta(p'^0-p^0)\mathcal A^\mu(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p),
 \tag{14.2.25}\label{eq:14.2.25}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 S_{\mathcal A}(p',p)=\delta(p'^0-p^0)S_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf p',\mathbf p;p^0),
 \tag{14.2.26}\label{eq:14.2.26}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(p',p)=\delta(p'^0-p^0)
 \Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf p',\mathbf p;p^0).
 \tag{14.2.27}\label{eq:14.2.27}
\end{equation}
The energy shift is then given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.2.17} and
\eqref{eq:14.2.13} as
\begin{equation}
 \delta E_N=-\int d^3p'\int d^3p\,
 \bar u_N(\mathbf p')\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf p',\mathbf p;E_N)u_N(\mathbf p),
 \tag{14.2.28}\label{eq:14.2.28}
\end{equation}
with $\Sigma^*_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf p',\mathbf p;E_N)$ given by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:14.2.23}, \eqref{eq:14.2.24}, and \eqref{eq:14.2.27}. This is the
master formula we shall use in the next section to calculate energy shifts in
weak external fields.
