\subsection{Cluster Decomposition and Connected Amplitudes}

It is one of the fundamental principles of physics (indeed, of all science)
that experiments that are sufficiently separated in space have unrelated
results. The probabilities for various collisions measured at Fermilab
should not depend on what sort of experiments are being done at CERN
at the same time. If this principle were not valid, then we could never
make any predictions about any experiment without knowing everything
about the universe.

In $S$-matrix theory, the cluster decomposition principle states that if
multi-particle processes $\alpha_1\to\beta_1$, $\alpha_2\to\beta_2$, $\ldots$,
$\alpha_{\mathcal N}\to\beta_{\mathcal N}$ are studied in $\mathcal N$
very distant laboratories, then the $S$-matrix element for the overall process
factorizes. That is,\footnote{We are here returning to the notation used in
Chapter 3; Greek letters $\alpha$ or $\beta$ stand for a collection of particles,
including for each particle a specification of its momentum, spin, and species.
Also, $\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\cdots+\alpha_{\mathcal N}$ is the state formed by
combining all the particles in the states $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots$, and
$\alpha_{\mathcal N}$, and likewise for
$\beta_1+\beta_2+\cdots+\beta_{\mathcal N}$.}
\begin{equation}
S_{\beta_1+\beta_2+\cdots+\beta_{\mathcal N},\,
   \alpha_1+\alpha_2+\cdots+\alpha_{\mathcal N}}
\longrightarrow
S_{\beta_1\alpha_1}S_{\beta_2\alpha_2}\cdots
S_{\beta_{\mathcal N}\alpha_{\mathcal N}}
\tag{4.3.1}
\label{eq:4.3.1}
\end{equation}
if for all $i\ne j$, \emph{all} of the particles in states $\alpha_i$ and
$\beta_i$ are at a great spatial distance from \emph{all} of the particles in
states $\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$. This factorization of $S$-matrix elements will
ensure a factorization of the corresponding transition probabilities,
corresponding to uncorrelated experimental results.

There is a combinatoric trick that allows us to rewrite Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.1} in
a more transparent way. Suppose we define the \emph{connected} part of the
$S$-matrix, $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$, by the formula\footnote{This decomposition has
been used in classical statistical mechanics by Ursell, Mayer, and others, and
in quantum statistical mechanics by Lee and Yang and others~\hyperref[ref:4.3]{[3]}. It has also
been used to calculate many-body ground state energies by Goldstone~\hyperref[ref:4.4]{[4]} and
Hugenholtz~\hyperref[ref:4.5]{[5]}. In all of these applications the purpose of isolating the
connected parts of Green's functions, partition functions, resolvents, etc., is
to deal with objects with a simple volume dependence. This is essentially our
purpose too, because as we shall see, the crucial property of the connected
parts of the $S$-matrix is that they are proportional to a single
momentum-conservation delta function, and in a box the delta function becomes
a Kronecker delta times the volume. The cluster decomposition is also the same
formal device as that used in the theory of noise~\hyperref[ref:4.6]{[6]} to decompose the
correlation function of several random variables into its `cumulants'; if the
random variable receives contributions from a large number $N$ of independent
fluctuations, then each cumulant is proportional to $N$.}
\begin{equation}
S_{\beta\alpha}
=\sum_{\mathrm{PART}}(\pm)
 S^C_{\beta_1\alpha_1}S^C_{\beta_2\alpha_2}\cdots\,.
\tag{4.3.2}
\label{eq:4.3.2}
\end{equation}
Here the sum is over all different ways of partitioning the particles in
the state $\alpha$ into clusters $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots$, and likewise a sum
over all ways of partitioning the particles in the state $\beta$ into clusters
$\beta_1,\beta_2,\ldots$; not counting as different those that merely arrange
particles within a given cluster or permute whole clusters. The sign is $+$ or
$-$ according to whether the rearrangements
$\alpha\to\alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots$ and
$\beta\to\beta_1\beta_2\cdots$ involve altogether an even or an odd number of
fermion interchanges, respectively. The term `connected' is used because of
the interpretation of $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ in terms of diagrams representing
different contributions in perturbation theory, to be discussed in the next
section.

This is a recursive definition. For each $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the sum on the
right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} consists of a term $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$, plus a
sum $\sum'$ over products of two or more $S^C$-matrix elements, with a total
number of particles in each of the states $\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$ that is
\emph{less} than the number of particles in the states $\alpha$ and $\beta$
\[
S_{\beta\alpha}=S^C_{\beta\alpha}
+\sum_{\mathrm{PART}}'(\pm)
 S^C_{\beta_1\alpha_1}S^C_{\beta_2\alpha_2}\cdots\,.
\]
Suppose that the $S^C$-matrix elements in this sum have already been chosen
in such a way that Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} is satisfied for states $\beta,\alpha$ containing
together fewer than, say, $N$ particles. Then no matter what values are found
in this way for the $S^C$-matrix elements appearing in the sum $\sum'$, we can
always choose the remaining term $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} is
also satisfied for states $\alpha,\beta$ containing a total of $N$
particles.\footnote{A technicality should be mentioned here. This argument
works only if we neglect the possibility that for one or more of the connected
$S$-matrix elements in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2}, the states $\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$ both
contain no particles at all. We must therefore define the connected
vacuum-vacuum element $S^C_{0,0}$ to be zero. We do not use Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} for
the vacuum-vacuum $S$-matrix $S_{0,0}$, which in the absence of time-varying
external fields is simply defined to be unity, $S_{0,0}=1$. We will have more
to say about the vacuum-vacuum amplitude in the presence of external fields in
Volume II.} Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} contains no information in itself; it is merely a
definition of $S^C$.

If the states $\alpha$ and $\beta$ each consist of just a single particle, say
with quantum numbers $q$ and $q'$ respectively, then the only term on the
right-hand-side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} is just $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ itself, so for
one-particle states
\begin{equation}
S^C_{q'q}=S_{q'q}=\delta(q'-q)\,.
\tag{4.3.3}
\label{eq:4.3.3}
\end{equation}
(Apart from possible degeneracies, the fact that $S_{q'q}$ is proportional to
$\delta(q'-q)$ follows from conservation laws. The absence of any
proportionality factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.3} is based on a suitable choice of the
relative phase of `in' and `out' states.) We are here assuming that
single-particle states are stable, so that there are no transitions between
single-particle states and any others, such as the vacuum.

For transitions between two-particle states, Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} reads
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{q'_1q'_2,\,q_1q_2}
={}&S^C_{q'_1q'_2,\,q_1q_2}
+\delta(q'_1-q_1)\delta(q'_2-q_2) \\
&\mathrel{\phantom{S^C_{q'_1q'_2,\,q_1q_2}}}
\mathord{\pm}\delta(q'_1-q_2)\delta(q'_2-q_1)\,.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3.4}
\label{eq:4.3.4}
\end{equation}
(We are here using Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.3}.) The sign $\pm$ is $-$ if both particles are
fermions, and otherwise $+$. We recognize that the two delta function terms
just add up to the norm~\eqref{eq:4.1.6}, so here $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ is just
$(S-\mathbf{1})_{\beta\alpha}$. But the general case is more complicated.

For transitions between three-particle or four-particle states, Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2}
reads
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{q'_1q'_2q'_3,\,q_1q_2q_3}
={}&S^C_{q'_1q'_2q'_3,\,q_1q_2q_3} \\
&+\delta(q'_1-q_1)S^C_{q'_2q'_3,\,q_2q_3}
  \mathbin{\pm}\text{ permutations} \\
&+\delta(q'_1-q_1)\delta(q'_2-q_2)\delta(q'_3-q_3)
  \mathbin{\pm}\text{ permutations}
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3.5}
\label{eq:4.3.5}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{q'_1q'_2q'_3q'_4,\,q_1q_2q_3q_4}
={}&S^C_{q'_1q'_2q'_3q'_4,\,q_1q_2q_3q_4} \\
&+S^C_{q'_1q'_2,\,q_1q_2}S^C_{q'_3q'_4,\,q_3q_4}
  \mathbin{\pm}\text{ permutations} \\
&+\delta(q'_1-q_1)S^C_{q'_2q'_3q'_4,\,q_2q_3q_4}
  \mathbin{\pm}\text{ permutations} \\
&+\delta(q'_1-q_1)\delta(q'_2-q_2)S^C_{q'_3q'_4,\,q_3q_4}
  \mathbin{\pm}\text{ permutations} \\
&+\delta(q'_1-q_1)\delta(q'_2-q_2)\delta(q'_3-q_3)\delta(q'_4-q_4)
  \mathbin{\pm}\text{ permutations}\,.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3.6}
\label{eq:4.3.6}
\end{equation}
(Taking account of all permutations, there are a total of $1+9+6=16$
terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.5} and $1+18+16+72+24=131$ terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.6}.
If we had not assumed that one-particle states are stable, there would be
even more terms.) As explained previously, the definition of
$S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ is recursive: we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.4} to define
$S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ for two-particle states, then use this definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.5} when we define $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ for three-particle states,
then use both of these definitions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.6} to obtain the definition of
$S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ for four-particle states, and so on.

The point of this definition of the connected part of the $S$-matrix is
that the cluster decomposition principle is equivalent to the requirement
that $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ must vanish when any one or more of the particles in
the states $\beta$ and/or $\alpha$ are far away in space from the
others.\footnote{In order to give a meaning to `far,' we will have to Fourier
transform $S^C$, so that each three-momentum label $\mathbf p$ is replaced with
a spatial coordinate three-vector $\mathbf x$.} To see this, suppose that the
particles in the states $\beta$ and $\alpha$ are grouped into clusters
$\beta_1,\beta_2,\ldots$ and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots$, and that all particles
in the set $\alpha_i+\beta_i$ are far from all particles in the set
$\alpha_j+\beta_j$ for any $j\ne i$. Then if $S^C_{\beta'\alpha'}$ vanishes if
any particles in $\beta'$ or $\alpha'$ are far from the others, it vanishes if
any particles in these states are in different clusters, so the definition
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2} yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{\beta\alpha}\longrightarrow{}&
\sum_{\mathrm{PART}}^{(1)}(\pm)
S^C_{\beta_{11}\alpha_{11}}S^C_{\beta_{12}\alpha_{12}}\cdots \\
&\quad\cdot
\sum_{\mathrm{PART}}^{(2)}(\pm)
S^C_{\beta_{21}\alpha_{21}}S^C_{\beta_{22}\alpha_{22}}\cdots
\cdot\cdots\,,
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3.7}
\label{eq:4.3.7}
\end{equation}
where $\sum^{(j)}$ is a sum over all different ways of partitioning the
clusters $\beta_j$ and $\alpha_j$ into subclusters
$\beta_{j1},\beta_{j2},\ldots$ and $\alpha_{j1},\alpha_{j2},\ldots$. But
referring back to Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.2}, this is just the desired factorization property
\eqref{eq:4.3.1}.

For instance, suppose that in the four-particle reaction
$1234\to1'2'3'4'$, we let particles $1,2,1'$, and $2'$ be very far from
$3,4,3'$, and $4'$. Then if $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ vanishes when any particles
in $\beta$ and/or $\alpha$ are far from the others, the only terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.6} that survive (in an even more abbreviated notation) are
\[
\begin{aligned}
S_{1'2'3'4',\,1234}\longrightarrow{}&
S^C_{1'2',\,12}S^C_{3'4',\,34} \\
&+(\delta_{1'1}\delta_{2'2}\mathbin{\pm}
    \delta_{1'2}\delta_{2'1})S^C_{3'4',\,34} \\
&+(\delta_{3'3}\delta_{4'4}\mathbin{\pm}
    \delta_{3'4}\delta_{4'3})S^C_{1'2',\,12} \\
&+(\delta_{1'1}\delta_{2'2}\mathbin{\pm}
    \delta_{1'2}\delta_{2'1})
  (\delta_{3'3}\delta_{4'4}\mathbin{\pm}
    \delta_{3'4}\delta_{4'3})\,.
\end{aligned}
\]
Comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.4} shows that this is just the required
factorization condition \eqref{eq:4.3.1}
\[
S_{1'2'3'4',\,1234}\longrightarrow
S_{1'2',\,12}S_{3'4',\,34}\,.
\]

We have formulated the cluster decomposition principle in coordinate
space, as the condition that $S^C_{\beta\alpha}$ vanishes if any particles in
the states $\beta$ or $\alpha$ are far from any others. It is convenient for us
to reexpress this in momentum space. The coordinate space matrix elements are
defined as a Fourier transform
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S^C_{\mathbf x'_1\mathbf x'_2\cdots,\,
     \mathbf x_1\mathbf x_2\cdots}
\equiv{}&\int d^3p'_1\,d^3p'_2\cdots d^3p_1\,d^3p_2\cdots\,
S^C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
     \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots} \\
&\cdot e^{i\mathbf p'_1\cdot\mathbf x'_1}
e^{i\mathbf p'_2\cdot\mathbf x'_2}\cdots
e^{-i\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf x_1}
e^{-i\mathbf p_2\cdot\mathbf x_2}\cdots\,.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3.8}
\label{eq:4.3.8}
\end{equation}
(We are here temporarily dropping spin and species labels, which just go along
with the momentum or coordinate labels.) If
$\lvert S^C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
                 \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots}\rvert$
were sufficiently well behaved (to be specific, if it were Lebesgue
integrable) then according to the Riemann--Lebesgue theorem~\hyperref[ref:4.7]{[7]} the integral
\eqref{eq:4.3.8} would vanish when any combination of spatial coordinates goes to
infinity. Now, this is certainly too strong a requirement. Translational
invariance tells us that the connected part of the $S$-matrix, like the
$S$-matrix itself, can only depend on differences of coordinate vectors, and
therefore does not change at all if all of the $x_i$ and $x'_j$ vary together,
with their differences held constant. This requires that the elements of
$S^C$ in a momentum basis must, like those of $S$, be proportional to a
three-dimensional delta function that ensures momentum conservation (and
makes
$\lvert S^C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
                 \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots}\rvert$
\emph{not} Lebesgue integrable), as well as the energy-conservation delta
function required by scattering theory. That is, we can write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S^C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
     \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots}
={}&\delta^3(\mathbf p'_1+\mathbf p'_2+\cdots
             -\mathbf p_1-\mathbf p_2-\cdots) \\
&\cdot\delta(E'_1+E'_2+\cdots-E_1-E_2-\cdots) \\
&\cdot C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
  \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots}\,.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3.9}
\label{eq:4.3.9}
\end{equation}
This is no problem: the cluster decomposition principle only requires that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.8} vanish when the \emph{differences} among some of the $x_i$ and/or
$x'_i$ become large. However, if $C$ itself in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.9} contained additional
delta functions of linear combinations of the three-momenta, then this
principle would not be satisfied. For instance, suppose that there were a delta
function in $C$ that required that the sum of the $\mathbf p'_i$ and
$-\mathbf p_i$ for some subset of the particles vanished. Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.8}
would not vary if all of the $\mathbf x'_i$ and $\mathbf x_i$ for the particles
in that subset moved together (with constant differences) away from all the
other $\mathbf x'_k$ and $\mathbf x_k$, in contradiction to the cluster
decomposition principle. Loosely speaking then, the cluster decomposition
principle simply says that \emph{the connected part of the $S$-matrix, unlike
the $S$-matrix itself, contains just a single momentum-conservation delta
function}.

In order to put this a bit more precisely, we can say that the coefficient
function
$C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
    \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots}$
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:4.3.9} is a smooth function of its momentum labels. But how smooth?
It would be most straightforward if we could simply require that
$C_{\mathbf p'_1\mathbf p'_2\cdots,\,
    \mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots}$
be analytic in all of the momenta at
$\mathbf p'_1=\mathbf p'_2=\cdots=\mathbf p_1=\mathbf p_2=\cdots=0$.
This requirement would indeed guarantee that
$S^C_{\mathbf x'_1\mathbf x'_2\cdots,\,
     \mathbf x_1\mathbf x_2\cdots}$
vanishes exponentially fast when any of the $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x'$ is
very distant from any of the other $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x'$. However, an
exponential fall-off of $S^C$ is not an essential part of the cluster
decomposition principle, and, in fact, the requirement of analyticity is not
met in all theories. Most notably, in theories with massless particles, $S^C$
can have poles at certain values of the $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf p'$. For
instance, as we will see in Chapter 10, if a massless particle can be emitted in
the transition $1\to3$ and absorbed in the transition $2\to4$, then
$S^C_{34,12}$ will have a term proportional to
$1/(\mathbf p_1-\mathbf p_3)^2$. After Fourier transforming, such poles yield
terms in
$S^C_{\mathbf x'_1\mathbf x'_2\cdots,\,
     \mathbf x_1\mathbf x_2\cdots}$
that fall off only as negative powers of coordinate differences~\hyperref[ref:4.1]{[1]}. There is
no need to formulate the cluster decomposition principle so stringently that
such behavior is ruled out. Thus the `smoothness' condition on $S^C$ should be
understood to allow various poles and branch-cuts at certain values of the
$\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf p'$, but not singularities as severe as delta
functions.
