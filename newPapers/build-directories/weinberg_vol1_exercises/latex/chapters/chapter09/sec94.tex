\subsection{Path-Integral Derivation of Feynman Rules}

We are now ready to use the path-integral formalism to derive the Feynman
rules in a wide class of theories. We will concentrate here on the vacuum
expectation values of time-ordered products of field operators (and their
adjoints),
\begin{equation}
M_{\ell_A\ell_B\cdots}(x_Ax_B\cdots)
=\frac{\OutBra{\Omega}T\{\Psi_{\ell_A}(x_A),
 \Psi_{\ell_B}(x_B),\ldots\}\InKet{\Omega}}
 {\OutBra{\Omega}\InKet{\Omega}},
\tag{9.4.1}\label{eq:9.4.1}
\end{equation}
from which $S$-matrix elements may be obtained (as shown in Section 6.4) by
stripping off the final propagators associated with each field, replacing them
with the coefficient functions that multiply creation or annihilation
operators in the corresponding free fields, and summing over the indices on
these coefficient functions.

For the simpler theories whose Hamiltonian is quadratic in the $\Pi$s,
Eq. \eqref{eq:9.3.11} gives
\begin{equation}
M_{\ell_A\ell_B\cdots}(x_Ax_B\cdots)
=\frac{\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{x,\ell}\dd\psi_\ell(x)\right]
 \psi_{\ell_A}(x_A)\psi_{\ell_B}(x_B)\cdots e^{\ii I[\psi]}}
 {\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{x,\ell}\dd\psi_\ell(x)\right]e^{\ii I[\psi]}},
\tag{9.4.2}\label{eq:9.4.2}
\end{equation}
where $I[\psi]$ is the action
\begin{equation}
I[\psi]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd\tau
 \left\{L[\psi(\tau),\dot\psi(\tau)]+\ii\epsilon\text{ terms}\right\}
\tag{9.4.3}\label{eq:9.4.3}
\end{equation}
with $L$ now including any terms that may arise from a field-dependent
determinant in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.3.10}.

Let us now suppose that the Lagrangian is the integral of a Lagrangian density,
consisting of a quadratic term $\mathcal L_0$ which would be present in the
absence of interactions, plus a Lagrangian interaction density $\mathcal L_1$:
\begin{align}
L[\psi(t),\dot\psi(t)]=\int \dd^3x\bigl[&
 \mathcal L_0(\psi(\mathbf{x},t),\partial_\mu\psi(\mathbf{x},t))
\notag\\
&+\mathcal L_1(\psi(\mathbf{x},t),\partial_\mu\psi(\mathbf{x},t))\bigr].
\tag{9.4.4}\label{eq:9.4.4}
\end{align}
That is, the action \eqref{eq:9.4.3} is
\begin{align}
I[\psi]&=I_0[\psi]+I_1[\psi],
\tag{9.4.5}\label{eq:9.4.5}\\
I_0[\psi]&=\int \dd^4x\,
 \mathcal L_0(\psi(x),\partial_\mu\psi(x))+\ii\epsilon\text{ terms},
\tag{9.4.6}\label{eq:9.4.6}\\
I_1[\psi]&=\int \dd^4x\,
 \mathcal L_1(\psi(x),\partial_\mu\psi(x)).
\tag{9.4.7}\label{eq:9.4.7}
\end{align}
Since $\mathcal L_0$ and the `$\ii\epsilon$ terms' are quadratic in the fields,
we may always write $I_0$ in the generalized quadratic form
\begin{equation}
I_0[\psi]=-\frac12\int \dd^4x\,\dd^4x'\sum_{\ell,\ell'}
 \mathcal D_{\ell x,\ell'x'}\psi_\ell(x)\psi_{\ell'}(x').
\tag{9.4.8}\label{eq:9.4.8}
\end{equation}
For instance, for a real scalar field of mass $m$, the unperturbed Lagrangian
is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_0=-\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi
 -\frac12m^2\phi^2
\tag{9.4.9}\label{eq:9.4.9}
\end{equation}
and the $\ii\epsilon$ terms in $I_0$ are given by Eq. \eqref{eq:9.2.16}, as
\begin{equation}
\frac12\ii\epsilon\int \dd t\int \dd^3x\,\dd^3x'\,
 \mathcal E(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')\phi(\mathbf{x},t)\phi(\mathbf{x}',t)
\tag{9.4.10}\label{eq:9.4.10}
\end{equation}
so here
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_{x,x'}=
 \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x'_\mu}
 \delta^4(x-x')+m^2\delta^4(x-x')
 -\ii\epsilon\mathcal E(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')\delta(t-t').
\tag{9.4.11}\label{eq:9.4.11}
\end{equation}
(We are now dropping the factor $e^{-\epsilon|t|}$ in the $\ii\epsilon$ term,
since it produces a correction of higher order in $\epsilon$.) To deal with
interactions, we will expand the exponential in powers of $I_1$,
\begin{equation}
\exp(\ii I[\psi])=\exp(\ii I_0[\psi])
 \sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^N}{N!}\bigl(I_1[\psi]\bigr)^N
\tag{9.4.12}\label{eq:9.4.12}
\end{equation}
and then expand $I_1$ in powers of the fields. The general integrals that we
encounter in the numerator and the denominator of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.4.2} are of the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I_{\ell_1\ell_2\cdots}(x_1x_2\cdots)
\equiv\int\left(\prod_{\ell,x}\dd\psi_\ell(x)\right)e^{\ii I_0[\psi]}
 \psi_{\ell_1}(x_1)\psi_{\ell_2}(x_2)\cdots,
\tag{9.4.13}\label{eq:9.4.13}
\end{equation}
where the field factors $\psi_{\ell_1}(x_1),\psi_{\ell_2}(x_2)$, etc. arise
from $I_1[\psi]$ and/or from the field factors $\psi_{\ell_A}(x_A)$ etc.
originally present in the numerator of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.4.2}. With $I_0[\psi]$ of the
form \eqref{eq:9.4.8}, the integral \eqref{eq:9.4.13} is of the same form as the integral
evaluated in the Appendix to this chapter, with the discrete index $s$
replaced with the pair of labels $\ell,x$. We can therefore use Eqs. \eqref{eq:9.A.12}
and \eqref{eq:9.A.15}, which give here
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I_{\ell_1\ell_2\cdots}(x_1x_2\cdots)
=\left[\operatorname{Det}\left(\frac{\ii\mathcal D}{2\pi}\right)\right]^{-1/2}
 \sum_{\substack{\text{pairings of}\\\text{fields}}}
 \prod_{\substack{\text{pairs of}\\\text{fields}}}
 [-\ii\mathcal D^{-1}]_{\text{paired fields}}.
\tag{9.4.14}\label{eq:9.4.14}
\end{equation}
This just amounts to the coordinate-space Feynman rules for calculating the
numerator of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.4.2} in their covariant form: we expand in the interaction
$I_1$, and then sum over the ways of pairing the fields in the $I_1$s with each
other and with the fields $\psi_{\ell_A}(x_A)$, etc., with the contribution of
each pairing being given by the spacetime integral of the product of the
coefficients of the fields in $I_1[\psi]$ and the product of the `propagators'
$-\ii\Delta$, where
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\ell_1\ell_2}(x_1,x_2)
=(\mathcal D^{-1})_{\ell_1x_1,\ell_2x_2}.
\tag{9.4.15}\label{eq:9.4.15}
\end{equation}
(The factor $[\operatorname{Det}(\ii\mathcal D/2\pi)]^{-1/2}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.4.14}
actually represents the contribution of graphs with unlimited numbers of
single loops unattached to any other lines, but in any case this factor
cancels in the ratio \eqref{eq:9.4.2}.)

It remains to calculate the propagators \eqref{eq:9.4.15}. We interpret Eq. \eqref{eq:9.4.15} as
an integral equation
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\ell_2}\int \dd^4x_2\,
 \mathcal D_{\ell_1x_1,\ell_2x_2}
 \Delta_{\ell_2\ell_3}(x_2,x_3)
=\delta^4(x_1-x_3)\delta_{\ell_1\ell_3}.
\tag{9.4.16}\label{eq:9.4.16}
\end{equation}
In the absence of external fields, translation invariance will make
$\mathcal D$ necessarily a function only of $x_1-x_2$, which can be written as
a Fourier integral
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_{\ell_1x_1,\ell_2x_2}
=(2\pi)^{-4}\int \dd^4p\,e^{\ii p\cdot(x_1-x_2)}
 \mathcal D_{\ell_1\ell_2}(p).
\tag{9.4.17}\label{eq:9.4.17}
\end{equation}
The solution of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.4.16} is then
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\ell_1\ell_2}(x_1,x_2)
=(2\pi)^{-4}\int \dd^4p\,e^{\ii p\cdot(x_1-x_2)}
 \mathcal D^{-1}_{\ell_1\ell_2}(p),
\tag{9.4.18}\label{eq:9.4.18}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D^{-1}$ is the ordinary inverse of the matrix $\mathcal D$.
As we will see, the $\ii\epsilon$ terms have the effect of making the inverse
well-defined for all real values of $p$. We have thus reduced the problem of
calculating the propagator to that of taking the inverse of a finite matrix.

First consider a massive scalar field, for which the kernel $\mathcal D$ takes
the form \eqref{eq:9.4.11}. We can write this as a Fourier integral
\[
\mathcal D_{x,y}=(2\pi)^{-4}\int \dd^4p\,e^{\ii p\cdot(x-y)}
 \bigl(p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon E(p)\bigr),
\]
so the propagator is
\[
\Delta(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int \dd^4p\,e^{\ii p\cdot(x-y)}
 \bigl(p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon E(p)\bigr)^{-1}.
\]
We recognize this as the same scalar propagator previously obtained by
operator methods. (The difference between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon E(p)$ is
immaterial, since both are just positive infinitesimals.)

For a second example, consider a real massive vector field. The unperturbed
Lagrangian is
\[
\mathcal L_0=-\frac14(\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu)
 (\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu)-\frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu.
\]
We can again write $I_0[\psi]$ in the form \eqref{eq:9.4.8}, with kernel
\begin{align*}
\mathcal D_{\rho x,\sigma y}
={}&\left[\eta_{\rho\sigma}
 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^\mu\partial y_\mu}
 -\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^\sigma\partial y^\rho}
 +m^2\eta_{\rho\sigma}\right]\delta^4(x-y)+\ii\epsilon\text{ terms}
\\
={}&(2\pi)^{-4}\int \dd^4p\,e^{\ii p\cdot(x-y)}
 \left[\eta_{\rho\sigma}p^2-p_\rho p_\sigma
 +m^2\eta_{\rho\sigma}+\ii\epsilon\text{ terms}\right].
\end{align*}
We will not bother to show it here, but the `$+\ii\epsilon$ terms' here take the
simple form $-\ii\epsilon E(p)\eta_{\rho\sigma}$. The vector field propagator is
then given by simply inverting the $4\times4$ matrix in the integrand
\[
\Delta_{\rho\sigma}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-4}\int
 \frac{\dd^4p\,e^{\ii p\cdot(x-y)}}{p^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon E(p)}
 \left[\eta_{\rho\sigma}+\frac{p_\rho p_\sigma}{m^2}\right].
\]
(Terms proportional to $\epsilon$ are dropped in the numerator. They are
important in the denominator in defining how the integrand is to be treated
near the mass shell, $p^2=-m^2$.) This is the same as the propagator derived by
operator methods, except that the non-covariant terms proportional to
$\delta(x^0-y^0)$ are now absent. These non-covariant terms were previously
needed to cancel non-covariant terms in the interaction Hamiltonian, but the
vertex contributions in the Feynman rules are now obtained directly by
inspection of the covariant Lagrangian, and no such cancellation is needed.

Theories with derivative coupling are equally simple. The factor arising from
the pairing of a field derivative $\partial_\mu\psi_\ell(x)$ with any other
field $\psi_m(y)$ (perhaps itself a field derivative) is
\begin{align}
\overline{\bigl(\partial_\mu\psi_\ell(x)\,\psi_m(y)\bigr)}
&=\frac{\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{x,\ell}\dd\psi_\ell(x)\right]
 \partial_\mu\psi_\ell(x)\psi_m(y)e^{\ii I[\psi]}}
 {\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{x,\ell}\dd\psi_\ell(x)\right]e^{\ii I[\psi]}}
\notag\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
 \overline{\bigl(\psi_\ell(x)\,\psi_m(y)\bigr)}.
\tag{9.4.19}\label{eq:9.4.19}
\end{align}
Such propagators have no non-covariant pieces. For instance, for a real scalar
field, the pairing of $\partial_\mu\phi$ with $\partial_\nu\phi$ gives a
momentum space propagator $k_\mu k_\nu/(k^2+m^2-\ii\epsilon)$. Also, as we saw in
the previous section, vertices in the theory of a scalar field with derivative
couplings to other fields may be read off from the Lagrangian, and are
separately covariant.
