In Chapters 7 and 8 we applied the canonical quantization operator formalism
to derive the Feynman rules for a variety of theories. In many cases, such
as the scalar field with derivative coupling or the vector field with zero or
non-zero mass, the procedure though straightforward was rather awkward.
The interaction Hamiltonian turned out to contain a covariant term, equal
to the negative of the interaction term in the Lagrangian, plus a non-covariant
term, which served to cancel non-covariant terms in the propagator. In the
case of electrodynamics this non-covariant term (the Coulomb energy) turned
out to be not even spatially local, though it is local in time. Yet the final
results are quite simple: the Feynman rules are just those we should obtain
with covariant propagators, and using the negative of the interaction term
in the Lagrangian to calculate vertex contributions. The awkwardness in
obtaining these simple results, which was bad enough for the theories
considered in Chapters 7 and 8, becomes unbearable for more complicated
theories, like the non-Abelian gauge theories to be discussed in Volume II,
and also general relativity. One would very much prefer a method of
calculation that goes directly from the Lagrangian to the Feynman rules in
their final, Lorentz-covariant form.

Fortunately, such a method does exist. It is provided by the path-integral
approach to quantum mechanics. This was first presented in the context of
non-relativistic quantum mechanics in Feynman's Princeton Ph.\ D. thesis~\hyperref[ref:9.1]{[1]},
as a means of working directly with a Lagrangian rather than a Hamiltonian.
In this respect, it was inspired by earlier work of Dirac~\hyperref[ref:9.2]{[2]}. The path-integral
approach played a part (along with inspired guesswork) in Feynman's later
derivation of his diagrammatic rules~\hyperref[ref:9.3]{[3]}. However, although Feynman diagrams
became widely used in the 1950s, most physicists (including myself) tended
to derive them using the operator methods of Schwinger and Tomonaga, which
were shown by Dyson in 1949 to lead to the same diagrammatic rules that had
been obtained by Feynman by his own methods.

The path-integral approach was revived in the late 1960s, when Faddeev and
Popov~\hyperref[ref:9.4]{[4]} and De Witt~\hyperref[ref:9.5]{[5]} showed how to apply it to non-Abelian gauge
theories and general relativity. For most theorists, the turning point came in
1971, when 't Hooft~\hyperref[ref:9.6]{[6]} used path-integral methods to derive the Feynman
rules for spontaneously broken gauge theories (discussed in Volume II),
including in particular the theory of weak and electromagnetic interactions,
in a gauge that made the high energy behavior of these theories transparent.
Soon after, as also discussed in Volume II, it was discovered that the
path-integral method allows us to take account of contributions to the
$S$-matrix that have an essential singularity at zero coupling constant and
therefore cannot be discovered in any finite order of perturbation theory.
Since then, the path-integral methods described here have become an
indispensable part of the equipment of all physicists who make use of
quantum field theory.

At this point the reader may be wondering why if the path-integral method
is so convenient we bothered in Chapter 7 to introduce the canonical
formalism. Indeed, Feynman seems at first to have thought of his path-integral
approach as a substitute for the ordinary canonical formulation of quantum
mechanics. There are two reasons for starting with the canonical formalism.
The first is a point of principle: although the path-integral formalism provides
us with manifestly Lorentz-invariant diagrammatic rules, it does not make
clear why the $S$-matrix calculated in this way is unitary. As far as I know,
the only way to show that the path-integral formalism yields a unitary
$S$-matrix is to use it to reconstruct the canonical formalism, in which
unitarity is obvious. There is a kind of conservation of trouble here; we can
use the canonical approach, in which unitarity is obvious and Lorentz
invariance obscure, or the path-integral approach, which is manifestly
Lorentz-invariant but far from manifestly unitary. Since the path-integral
approach is here derived from the canonical approach, we know that the two
approaches yield the same $S$-matrix, so that the $S$-matrix must indeed be
both Lorentz-invariant and unitary.

The second reason for introducing the canonical formalism first is more
practical: there are important theories in which the simplest version of the
Feynman path-integral method, in which propagators and interaction vertices
are taken directly from the Lagrangian, is simply wrong. One example is the
non-linear $\sigma$-model, with Lagrangian density
$\mathcal{L}=-\tfrac12 g_{k\ell}(\phi)\partial_\mu\phi^k\partial^\mu\phi^\ell$.
In such theories, using the naive Feynman rules derived directly from the
Lagrangian density would yield an $S$-matrix that is not only wrong but even
non-unitary, and that also depends on the way in which we define the scalar
field~\hyperref[ref:9.7]{[7]}. In this chapter we shall derive the path-integral formalism from the
canonical formalism, and in this way we will see what additional sorts of
vertices are needed to supplement the simplest version of the Feynman
path-integral method.

\subsection{The General Path-Integral Formula}

We start with a general quantum mechanical system, with Hermitian operator
`coordinates' $Q_a$ and conjugate `momenta' $P_b$, satisfying the canonical
commutation relations\footnote{We are tacitly assuming here that any first
class constraints are eliminated by choosing a gauge, and any remaining
second class constraints are `solved' by writing the constrained degrees of
freedom in terms of the unconstrained $Q_a$ and $P_a$, as in Section 7.6.
The direct application of path-integral methods to constrained systems is
described by Faddeev~\hyperref[ref:9.9]{[9]}.}:
\begin{equation}
  [Q_a,P_b]=i\delta_{ab},
  \tag{9.1.1}\label{eq:9.1.1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  [Q_a,Q_b]=[P_a,P_b]=0.
  \tag{9.1.2}\label{eq:9.1.2}
\end{equation}
(We shall restrict ourselves in this and the next three sections to bosonic
operators, which satisfy commutation rather than anticommutation relations.
Our results will be generalized to include fermionic operators in Section 9.5.)
In a field theory, the index $a$ consists of a position $\mathbf{x}$ and a
discrete Lorentz and species index $m$, and we conventionally write
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\mathbf{x},m}\equiv Q_m(\mathbf{x}),
  \tag{9.1.3}\label{eq:9.1.3}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathbf{x},m}\equiv P_m(\mathbf{x}).
  \tag{9.1.4}\label{eq:9.1.4}
\end{equation}
Also, the Kronecker delta in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.1} is interpreted in a field theory as
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\mathbf{x},m;\mathbf{y},n}\equiv
  \delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\delta_{mn}.
  \tag{9.1.5}\label{eq:9.1.5}
\end{equation}
However, for the present it will be convenient to use the more compact
notation of Eqs. \eqref{eq:9.1.1} and \eqref{eq:9.1.2}. These are `Schr\"odinger-picture'
operators, taken at a fixed time (say, $t=0$). The time-dependent operators
in the Heisenberg picture will be considered a little later.

Since the $Q_a$ all commute, we can find a simultaneous eigenstate
$\ket{q}$, with eigenvalues $q_a$:
\begin{equation}
  Q_a\ket{q}=q_a\ket{q}.
  \tag{9.1.6}\label{eq:9.1.6}
\end{equation}
(We are using lower case $q$s and $p$s here to denote eigenvalues rather
than to denote operators in the interaction picture as in Chapter 7, but
since we will not be using the interaction picture in this chapter no
confusion should arise.) The eigenvectors can be taken to be orthonormal,
\begin{equation}
  \braket{q'}{q}=\prod_a\delta(q'_a-q_a)\equiv\delta(q'-q),
  \tag{9.1.7}\label{eq:9.1.7}
\end{equation}
so that the completeness relation reads
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{1}=\int\prod_a dq_a\,\ket{q}\bra{q}.
  \tag{9.1.8}\label{eq:9.1.8}
\end{equation}

Similarly, we can find a complete orthonormal set of eigenstates of the
$P_a$:
\begin{equation}
  P_a\ket{p}=p_a\ket{p},
  \tag{9.1.9}\label{eq:9.1.9}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \braket{p'}{p}=\prod_a\delta(p'_a-p_a)\equiv\delta(p'-p),
  \tag{9.1.10}\label{eq:9.1.10}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{1}=\int\prod_a dp_a\,\ket{p}\bra{p}.
  \tag{9.1.11}\label{eq:9.1.11}
\end{equation}
As usual, it follows from Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.1} that these two complete sets of
eigenstates have the scalar product\footnote{The proof follows the same lines
as in the quantum mechanics of point particles. From Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.1}, we see
that $P_a$ acts as $-i\partial/\partial q_a$ on wave functions in a $q$-basis.
The right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.12} is then seen to be the wave function in
this basis of an eigenstate of $P$. The factor $\prod_a1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is fixed
by the normalization requirement, Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.10}.}
\begin{equation}
  \braket{q}{p}=\prod_a\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(iq_ap_a).
  \tag{9.1.12}\label{eq:9.1.12}
\end{equation}

In the Heisenberg picture, the $Q$ and $P$ operators are given a
time-dependence
\begin{equation}
  Q_a(t)=\exp(iHt)Q_a\exp(-iHt),
  \tag{9.1.13}\label{eq:9.1.13}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  P_a(t)=\exp(iHt)P_a\exp(-iHt),
  \tag{9.1.14}\label{eq:9.1.14}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the total Hamiltonian. These have eigenstates $\ket{q;t}$ and
$\ket{p;t}$
\begin{equation}
  Q_a(t)\ket{q;t}=q_a\ket{q;t},
  \tag{9.1.15}\label{eq:9.1.15}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  P_a(t)\ket{p;t}=p_a\ket{p;t},
  \tag{9.1.16}\label{eq:9.1.16}
\end{equation}
given by
\begin{equation}
  \ket{q;t}=\exp(iHt)\ket{q},
  \tag{9.1.17}\label{eq:9.1.17}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \ket{p;t}=\exp(iHt)\ket{p}.
  \tag{9.1.18}\label{eq:9.1.18}
\end{equation}
(Note that $\ket{q;t}$ is the eigenstate of $Q_a(t)$ with eigenvalue $q_a$,
and \emph{not} the result of letting the state $\ket{q}$ evolve for a time
$t$. This is why its time-dependence is given by a factor $\exp(iHt)$ rather
than $\exp(-iHt)$.) These states obviously satisfy the completeness and
orthonormality conditions
\begin{equation}
  \braket{q';t}{q;t}=\delta(q'-q),
  \tag{9.1.19}\label{eq:9.1.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \braket{p';t}{p;t}=\delta(p'-p),
  \tag{9.1.20}\label{eq:9.1.20}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \int\prod_a dq_a\,\ket{q;t}\bra{q;t}=\mathbf{1},
  \tag{9.1.21}\label{eq:9.1.21}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \int\prod_a dp_a\,\ket{p;t}\bra{p;t}=\mathbf{1},
  \tag{9.1.22}\label{eq:9.1.22}
\end{equation}
and also
\begin{equation}
  \braket{q;t}{p;t}=\prod_a\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(iq_ap_a).
  \tag{9.1.23}\label{eq:9.1.23}
\end{equation}

If, by measurements at time $t$, we find that our system is in a definite
state $\ket{q;t}$, then the probability amplitude for measurements at time
$t'$ to give a state $\ket{q';t'}$ is the scalar product
$\braket{q';t'}{q;t}$. Our central dynamical problem is to calculate this
scalar product.

This is easy when $t'$ and $t$ are infinitesimally close, say
$t'=\tau+d\tau$ and $t=\tau$. Using Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.17}, we have
\begin{equation}
  \braket{q';\tau+d\tau}{q;\tau}
  =\bra{q';\tau}\exp(-iH\,d\tau)\ket{q;\tau}.
  \tag{9.1.24}\label{eq:9.1.24}
\end{equation}
The Hamiltonian $H$ is given as a function $H(Q,P)$, but since \eqref{eq:9.1.13}
and \eqref{eq:9.1.14} are similarity transformations, and $H$ commutes with itself,
it can equally well be written as the \emph{same} function of $Q(t)$ and
$P(t)$
\begin{equation}
  H\equiv H(Q,P)=e^{iHt}H(Q,P)e^{-iHt}=H\bigl(Q(t),P(t)\bigr).
  \tag{9.1.25}\label{eq:9.1.25}
\end{equation}
This function can be written in various different forms, with different
constant coefficients, by using the commutation relations \eqref{eq:9.1.1} and
\eqref{eq:9.1.2} to move the $Q$s and $P$s past each other. It will be convenient to
adopt a standard form, in which all $Q$s appear to the \emph{left} of all
$P$s. For instance, given a term in the Hamiltonian of form $P_aQ_bP_c$,
we would rewrite it as $P_aQ_bP_c=Q_bP_aP_c-i\delta_{ab}P_c$. With this
convention, the $Q_a(t)$s in the Hamiltonian in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.24} may be
replaced\footnote{This is only possible because with $d\tau$ infinitesimal,
$\exp(-iH\,d\tau)$ is linear in $H$.} with their eigenvalues $q'_a$. To deal
with the $P(t)$, we use Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.23} to expand $\ket{q;\tau}$ in
$P$-eigenstates $\ket{p;\tau}$, and find
\begin{align}
  \braket{q';\tau+d\tau}{q;\tau}
  &={}
  \int\prod_a dp_a\,
  \bra{q';\tau}\exp\bigl(-iH(Q(\tau),P(\tau))d\tau\bigr)\ket{p;\tau}
  \braket{p;\tau}{q;\tau}
  \notag\\
  &=\int\prod_a\frac{dp_a}{2\pi}
  \exp\left[-iH(q',p)d\tau+i\sum_a(q'_a-q_a)p_a\right],
  \tag{9.1.26}\label{eq:9.1.26}
\end{align}
with each $p_a$ integrated from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.

Now let's return to the more general case of a finite time-interval. To
calculate $\braket{q';t'}{q;t}$, with $t<t'$, we break up the time-interval
from $t$ to $t'$ into steps $t,\tau_1,\tau_2,\ldots,\tau_N,t'$, with
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{k+1}-\tau_k=d\tau=(t'-t)/(N+1)
  \tag{9.1.27}\label{eq:9.1.27}
\end{equation}
and sum over a complete set of states $\ket{q;\tau_k}$ at each time
$\tau_k$:
\begin{align}
  \braket{q';t'}{q;t}
  ={}&\int dq_1\cdots dq_N\,\braket{q';t'}{q_N;\tau_N}
  \braket{q_N;\tau_N}{q_{N-1};\tau_{N-1}}\cdots
  \braket{q_1;\tau_1}{q;t}.
  \tag{9.1.28}\label{eq:9.1.28}
\end{align}
Inserting Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.26}, this becomes
\begin{align}
  \braket{q';t'}{q;t}
  ={}&\int
  \left[\prod_{k=1}^{N}\prod_a dq_{k,a}\right]
  \left[\prod_{k=0}^{N}\prod_a\frac{dp_{k,a}}{2\pi}\right]\notag \\
  &\cdot \exp\left[i\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}
  \left\{\sum_a(q_{k,a}-q_{k-1,a})p_{k-1,a}
  -H(q_k,p_{k-1})d\tau\right\}\right],
  \tag{9.1.29}\label{eq:9.1.29}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
  q_0\equiv q,
  \qquad
  q_{N+1}\equiv q'.
  \tag{9.1.30}\label{eq:9.1.30}
\end{equation}
Our result, Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.29}, can be put in a much more elegant form. Define
smooth interpolating functions, $q(\tau)$ and $p(\tau)$, such that
\begin{equation}
  q_a(\tau_k)=q_{k,a},
  \qquad
  p_a(\tau_k)=p_{k,a}.
  \tag{9.1.31}\label{eq:9.1.31}
\end{equation}
In the limit $d\tau\to0$ (i.e., $N\to\infty$), the argument of the
exponential in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.29} becomes just an integral over $\tau$
\begin{align}
  &\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\left\{\sum_a(q_{k,a}-q_{k-1,a})p_{k-1,a}
  -H(q_k,p_{k-1})d\tau\right\}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\left\{\sum_a\dot q_a(\tau_k)p_a(\tau_k)
  -H\bigl(q(\tau_k),p(\tau_k)\bigr)\right\}d\tau+O(d\tau^2)
  \notag\\
  &\quad\longrightarrow
  \int_t^{t'}d\tau\left\{\sum_a\dot q_a(\tau)p_a(\tau)
  -H\bigl(q(\tau),p(\tau)\bigr)\right\}.
  \tag{9.1.32}\label{eq:9.1.32}
\end{align}
Further, we may define integrals \emph{over the functions} $q(\tau),p(\tau)$
by
\begin{equation}
  \int\prod_{\tau,a}dq_a(\tau)\prod_{\tau,b}\frac{dp_b(\tau)}{2\pi}\cdots
  \equiv\lim_{d\tau\to0}\int\prod_{k,a}dq_{k,a}
  \prod_{k,b}\frac{dp_{k,b}}{2\pi}\cdots.
  \tag{9.1.33}\label{eq:9.1.33}
\end{equation}
Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.29} then becomes a constrained path integral
\begin{align}
  \braket{q';t'}{q;t}
  ={}&\int_{\substack{q_a(t)=q_a\\q_a(t')=q'_a}}
  \prod_{\tau,a}dq_a(\tau)\prod_{\tau,b}\frac{dp_b(\tau)}{2\pi}
  \notag\\
  &\cdot\exp\left[i\int_t^{t'}d\tau
  \left\{\sum_a\dot q_a(\tau)p_a(\tau)
  -H\bigl(q(\tau),p(\tau)\bigr)\right\}\right].
  \tag{9.1.34}\label{eq:9.1.34}
\end{align}
This is called a \emph{path integral}, because we integrate over all paths
that take $q(\tau)$ from $q$ at $\tau=t$ to $q'$ at $\tau=t'$, as well as
over all $p(\tau)$. The great advantage of writing matrix elements in this
way is that, as shown in Section 9.3, the path integrals are easy to calculate
when expanded in powers of the coupling constants in $H$.

The path-integral formalism allows us to calculate not only transition
probability amplitudes like $\braket{q';t'}{q;t}$, but also the matrix
elements between states $\bra{q';t'}$ and $\ket{q;t}$ of time-ordered
products of general operators $\mathcal{O}(P(t),Q(t))$. It will be convenient
to define these operators with (unlike $H$) all $P$s moved to the \emph{left}
and all $Q$s to the \emph{right}. Then by inserting any such operator
$\mathcal{O}[P(t),Q(t)]$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.26}, we have
\begin{align}
  &\bra{q';\tau+d\tau}\mathcal{O}\bigl(P(t),Q(t)\bigr)\ket{q;\tau}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \int\prod_a dp_a\,
  \bra{q';\tau}\exp\bigl(-iH(Q(\tau),P(\tau))d\tau\bigr)\ket{p;\tau}
  \bra{p;\tau}\mathcal{O}\bigl(P(t),Q(t)\bigr)\ket{q;\tau}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \int\prod_a\frac{dp_a}{2\pi}
  \exp\left[-iH(q',p)d\tau+i\sum_a(q'_a-q_a)p_a\right]
  \mathcal{O}(p,q).
  \tag{9.1.35}\label{eq:9.1.35}
\end{align}
In order to calculate the matrix element of the operator product
\[
 \mathcal{O}_A(P(t_A),Q(t_A))
 \mathcal{O}_B(P(t_B),Q(t_B))\cdots
\]
with $t_A>t_B>\cdots$, we can insert the $\mathcal{O}$-operators
between the appropriate states in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.28}, and use Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.35}. For
instance, if the time $t_A$ falls between $\tau_k$ and $\tau_{k+1}$, then
insert $\mathcal{O}_A(P(t_A),Q(t_A))$ between $\bra{q_{k+1};\tau_{k+1}}$
and $\ket{q_k;\tau_k}$. Note that in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.28} each successive sum over
states is at a later time, so this is only possible because of our assumption
that $t_A>t_B>\cdots$. Following the same steps as before, we now find the
general path-integral formula
\begin{align}
  &\bra{q';t'}\mathcal{O}_A\bigl(P(t_A),Q(t_A)\bigr)
  \mathcal{O}_B\bigl(P(t_B),Q(t_B)\bigr)\cdots\ket{q;t}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \int_{\substack{q_a(t)=q_a\\q_a(t')=q'_a}}
  \prod_{\tau,a}dq_a(\tau)\prod_{\tau,b}\frac{dp_b(\tau)}{2\pi}
  \mathcal{O}_A\bigl(p(t_A),q(t_A)\bigr)
  \mathcal{O}_B\bigl(p(t_B),q(t_B)\bigr)\cdots
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\left[i\int_t^{t'}d\tau
  \left\{\sum_a\dot q_a(\tau)p_a(\tau)
  -H\bigl(q(\tau),p(\tau)\bigr)\right\}\right].
  \tag{9.1.36}\label{eq:9.1.36}
\end{align}
This result is only valid if the times are ordered, with
\begin{equation}
  t'>t_A>t_B>\cdots>t.
  \tag{9.1.37}\label{eq:9.1.37}
\end{equation}
However, nothing on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.36} refers to the order
of time-arguments. Hence if we are presented with a path integral like the
right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.36}, with $t_A,t_B,\ldots$ in arbitrary order
(all between $t$ and $t'$, with $t<t'$), then this path integral will equal
a matrix element like the left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.36}, but with the
operators arranged in order (from left to right) of decreasing time. That is,
for $t_A,t_B,\ldots$ in arbitrary order, we have
\begin{align}
  &\bra{q';t'}T\left\{\mathcal{O}_A\bigl(P(t_A),Q(t_A)\bigr),
  \mathcal{O}_B\bigl(P(t_B),Q(t_B)\bigr),\ldots\right\}\ket{q;t}
  \notag\\
  &\quad={}
  \int_{\substack{q_a(t)=q_a\\q_a(t')=q'_a}}
  \prod_{\tau,a}dq_a(\tau)\prod_{\tau,b}\frac{dp_b(\tau)}{2\pi}
  \mathcal{O}_A\bigl(p(t_A),q(t_A)\bigr)
  \mathcal{O}_B\bigl(p(t_B),q(t_B)\bigr)\cdots
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\cdot\exp\left[i\int_t^{t'}d\tau
  \left\{\sum_a\dot q_a(\tau)p_a(\tau)
  -H\bigl(q(\tau),p(\tau)\bigr)\right\}\right],
  \tag{9.1.38}\label{eq:9.1.38}
\end{align}
where $T$ denotes the usual time-ordered product.

It should perhaps be stressed that the c-number functions $q_a(\tau)$,
$p_a(\tau)$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38} are mere variables of integration, and in
particular are \emph{not} constrained to obey the equations of motion of
classical Hamiltonian dynamics
\begin{equation}
  \dot q_a(\tau)-
  \frac{\partial H(q(\tau),p(\tau))}{\partial p_a(\tau)}=0,
  \tag{9.1.39}\label{eq:9.1.39}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \dot p_a(\tau)+
  \frac{\partial H(q(\tau),p(\tau))}{\partial q_a(\tau)}=0.
  \tag{9.1.40}\label{eq:9.1.40}
\end{equation}
(For this reason, the Hamiltonian $H(q(\tau),p(\tau))$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38} is
\emph{not} constant in $\tau$.) Nevertheless, there is a limited sense in
which path integrals do respect these equations of motion. Suppose that one
of the functions in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38}, say
$\mathcal{O}_A(p(t_A),q(t_A))$, happens to be the left-hand side of either
Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.39} or Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.40}. We note that (for $t<t_A<t'$)
\begin{align*}
  \left(\dot q_a(t_A)-
  \frac{\partial H(q(t_A),p(t_A))}{\partial p_a(t_A)}\right)
  \exp(iI[q,p])
  &=-i\frac{\delta}{\delta p_a(t_A)}\exp(iI[q,p]),\\
  \left(\dot p_a(t_A)+
  \frac{\partial H(q(t_A),p(t_A))}{\partial q_a(t_A)}\right)
  \exp(iI[q,p])
  &=i\frac{\delta}{\delta q_a(t_A)}\exp(iI[q,p]),
\end{align*}
where $I$ is the argument of the exponential in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38}:
\[
  I[q,p]\equiv\int_t^{t'}d\tau
  \left\{\sum_a\dot q_a(\tau)p_a(\tau)
  -H\bigl(q(\tau),p(\tau)\bigr)\right\}.
\]
As long as $t_A$ does not approach $t$ or $t'$, the integrations over
$q_a(t_A)$ and $p_a(t_A)$ are unconstrained, and so with reasonable
assumptions about the convergence of these integrals, the integral of such
variational derivatives must vanish. Hence the path integral \eqref{eq:9.1.38}
vanishes if $\mathcal{O}_A(p,q)$ is taken to be the left-hand side of either
of the equations of motion \eqref{eq:9.1.39} or \eqref{eq:9.1.40}.

This simple rule applies only if the integration variables $q_a(t_A)$,
$p_a(t_A)$ are independent of any of the variables $q_a(t_B)$, $p_a(t_B)$,
etc. appearing in any of the other functions $\mathcal{O}_B$,
$\mathcal{O}_C$, etc. in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38}, and hence only if we prohibit $t_A$
from approaching $t_B,t_C$, etc. as well as $t$ or $t'$. When $t_A$
approaches, say, $t_B$, the path integral will be found to involve a non-zero
term proportional to $\delta(t_A-t_B)$ or its derivatives. These delta
functions are the same as would be found in the operator formalism from
time derivatives of the step functions implicit in the definition of the
time-ordered product.

In evaluating the path integrals \eqref{eq:9.1.34} and \eqref{eq:9.1.38}, we only need to know
the classical Hamiltonian, the c-number function $H(q,p)$. If we were to
define a theory by the path integrals, the question would naturally arise,
which of many possible quantum mechanical Hamiltonians $H(Q,P)$
(differing is the order of $Q$s and $P$s) governed the quantum theory that
corresponds to these path integrals. Our derivation has provided an answer:
the quantum Hamiltonian is to be taken with all $Q$s on the left, all $P$s
on the right. But it would be a mistake to give this prescription too much
significance. There are a great many ways of interpreting the measure
$\prod dq_a(\tau)\prod dp_b(\tau)$ appearing in path integrals like
\eqref{eq:9.1.34} or \eqref{eq:9.1.38}. Our prescription, of putting all $Q$s to the left of all
$P$s, is appropriate only if the measure is interpreted according to
Eqs. \eqref{eq:9.1.31}--\eqref{eq:9.1.33}. Other measures would lead to other prescriptions
for operator ordering. The question is not an urgent one, because different
prescriptions for ordering operators in the Hamiltonian just correspond to
different choices of the constants that appear as coefficients of the various
terms in the Hamiltonian, and we generally formulate theories with these
constants left as arbitrary parameters anyway.

It is difficult to use the general path integral in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38} for numerical
calculations or as a source of rigorous theorems. For these purposes it is
better to use the path-integral method to calculate amplitudes in Euclidean
space, where $t$ is replaced with an imaginary quantity $-ix_4$, and the
argument of the exponential in Eq. \eqref{eq:9.1.38} is a negative real quantity. In
this way, instead of jagged paths producing rapid oscillations of the
integrand from one path to another, all jagged paths are exponentially
suppressed. Though we shall not go into it here, quantum field theory may
be formulated from the beginning in terms of Feynman amplitudes in
Euclidean spacetime~\hyperref[ref:9.8a]{[8a]}. Under certain plausible assumptions, it is possible
to reconstruct the Feynman amplitudes in Minkowskian spacetime from their
Euclidean counterparts~\hyperref[ref:9.8b]{[8b]}. But we may as well stick to the Minkowski
space formulation of the path integral if we are only going to use it to
calculate Feynman amplitudes in perturbation theory.
