\subsection{Tubes and Extended Tubes}\label{sec:2.4}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=075 print=63 kind=prose
We have seen in the preceding section that the Laplace transform of a tempered
distribution vanishing outside a cone is the boundary value of a function
holomorphic in a certain tube.  In the present section we consider a function or
a set of functions holomorphic in that tube and with a definite transformation
law under $SL(2,\mathbb C)$ or, what turns out to be the same thing, the
restricted Lorentz group $L^{\uparrow}_{+}$.  We show that these functions are
necessarily holomorphic in a larger domain, the so-called extended tube, and
satisfy a transformation law under the proper complex Lorentz group,
$L_{+}(\mathbb C)$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=075 print=63 kind=prose
As we have defined it, the tube $\mathcal{T}_{n}$ is the open set of complex
$4n$ space, $\mathbb C^{4n}$, given by $\eta_j\in V_{+}$, $j=1,\ldots,n$,
where $\zeta_j=\xi_j-i\eta_j$, $j=1,\ldots,n$, is the splitting of $\zeta_j$
into real and imaginary parts.  The extended tube $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ is the
union of the open sets obtained from $\mathcal{T}_{n}$ by applying all proper
complex Lorentz transformations.  In other words,
$\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ if and only if there exists a
$\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$ and a point
$w_1,\ldots,w_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ such that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=075 print=63 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n=\Lambda w_1,\ldots,\Lambda w_n .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=075 print=63 kind=prose
The transformation laws of sets of holomorphic functions with which we are
concerned are of the form

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=075 print=63 kind=equation id=2-84
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\beta}S(A)_{\alpha\beta}
  f_{\beta}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)
  =f_{\alpha}\bigl(\Lambda(A)\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda(A)\zeta_n\bigr) .
  \tag{2-84}\label{eq:ch2-tube-transform}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=076 print=64 kind=prose
where $A\mapsto S(A)$ is a matrix representation of $SL(2,\mathbb C)$.  In
\eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform}, the point $(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)$ lies in $\mathcal{T}_{n}$.  We
noted in Section \ref{sec:1.3}, after \eqref{eq:ch1-sl2-representation-block}, that by choosing suitable linear
combinations of the $f_{\beta}$ we can obtain equivalent equations for the
linear combinations which transform according to irreducible representations of
$SL(2,\mathbb C)$, so it is no loss in generality to assume from the outset
that $A\mapsto S(A)$ is an irreducible representation, say
$\mathcal D^{(j/2,k/2)}$.  We conclude immediately that $j+k$ is even if the
$f_{\alpha}$ are not all zero, because \eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform} evaluated for $A=-\mathbf{1}$
reads, according to \eqref{eq:ch1-sl2-irreps},

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=076 print=64 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  (-1)^{j+k}f_{\alpha}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)
  =f_{\alpha}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n) .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=076 print=64 kind=prose
This implies that $A\mapsto S(A)$ is in fact a representation of
$L^{\uparrow}_{+}(\mathbb R)$, since, if $j+k$ is even, $S(A)=S(-A)$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=076 print=64 kind=prose
In \eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform} let us fix $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ and
consider both sides as functions of the six real parameters of the group
$SL(2,\mathbb C)$, suitably chosen, or equivalently, the six real parameters
of the Lorentz group $L^{\uparrow}_{+}$.  The matrix
$S(A)=S[\Lambda(A)]$ is an analytic function of the parameters in $A$, defined
for $A$ real, and so possesses the unique analytic continuation, $S(A,B)$ say,
to complex Lorentz transformations $\Lambda(A,B)$ in some neighborhood of the
set of real $\Lambda\in L^{\uparrow}_{+}$.  Here and in the following, a
neighborhood of a complex Lorentz transformation $\Lambda_1$ will mean all
$\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$ such that with suitable parametrization the six
parameters of $\Lambda$ lie in a complex neighborhood of the six parameters
defining $\Lambda_1$.  A neighborhood of a set of Lorentz transformations is
defined analogously.  The analytic continuation of the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform} is
$f_{\alpha}[\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_n]$; thus the
analytically continued equation is

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=076 print=64 kind=equation id=2-85
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\beta}S(A,B)_{\alpha\beta}
  f_{\beta}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)
  =f_{\alpha}\bigl[\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_1,\ldots,
    \Lambda(A,B)\zeta_n\bigr] .
  \tag{2-85}\label{eq:ch2-analytic-transform}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=076 print=64 kind=prose
which holds in some complex neighborhood of any real $\Lambda\in
L^{\uparrow}_{+}$, in particular, in some complex neighborhood of the
identity, provided $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n$ and
$\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_n$ lie in $\mathcal{T}_{n}$.
If $\Lambda\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda\zeta_n$ is outside $\mathcal{T}_{n}$, the
right-hand side is not defined initially, and so \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} is no longer an
equality between known functions.  In that case, we can try to use \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} to
extend the domain of $f_{\alpha}$ outside $\mathcal{T}_{n}$.  We get a
definition of $f_{\alpha}$ as a function of $n$ four-vectors
$z_1,\ldots,z_n$ at all points of the extended tube $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$; if
$z_1,\ldots,z_n\in\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ we define $f_{\alpha}(z_1,\ldots,z_n)$ by
\eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform}, where $z_j=\Lambda\zeta_j$, [$\zeta_j\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$,
$\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$].  It is not trivial to show that if a point
$z\in\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ can be reached from two different points of
$\mathcal{T}_{n}$, say $\zeta$ and $w$, by complex Lorentz transformations,
then the two ways of

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=prose-continuation
defining $f_{\alpha}(z_1,\ldots,z_n)$, via \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform}, lead to the same value.
That is, we have yet to show that the continuation is single-valued.  Thus, if
$\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$,
$w_1,\ldots,w_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$, and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  z_j=\Lambda(A_1,B_1)\zeta_j
      =\Lambda(A_2,B_2)w_j,\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,n ,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=prose
we have to prove that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{\beta}S(A_1,B_1)_{\alpha\beta}
  f_{\beta}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)
  =\sum_{\beta}S(A_2,B_2)_{\alpha\beta}
  f_{\beta}(w_1,\ldots,w_n) .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=prose
or equivalently, if we use the multiplication law of the group,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=equation id=2-86
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  f_{\alpha}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)
  ={}&\sum_{\beta}S(A_1^{-1}A_2,B_1^{-1}B_2)_{\alpha\beta}
  f_{\beta}(w_1,\ldots,w_n),\\[-2pt]
  &\hspace{1.5em}\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\quad
  w_1,\ldots,w_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n} .
  \end{split}
  \tag{2-86}\label{eq:ch2-single-valuedness}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=prose
If we put $A=A_1^{-1}A_2$, $B=B_1^{-1}B_2$, then
$\zeta_j=\Lambda(A,B)w_j$, and \eqref{eq:ch2-single-valuedness} reduces to \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform}.  Thus the
single-valuedness is ensured if \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} holds for all $\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$
with $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ and
$\Lambda\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$.  So far we have
proved \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} only if $\Lambda$ lies in some neighborhood of
$L^{\uparrow}_{+}$, that is, if $\Lambda$ is nearly real.  The main part of
the proof that \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} holds for all complex $\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$ is
the following lemma, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:2-2}.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=077 print=65 kind=lemma id=2-10
\begin{lemma*}
Suppose $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ and
$\Lambda\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ with
$\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$.  Then there exists a continuous curve of
proper complex Lorentz transformations
$\{\Lambda(t),\ 0\leq t\leq1\}$ such that
$\Lambda(0)=\mathbf{1}$, $\Lambda(1)=\Lambda$ and
$\Lambda(t)\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda(t)\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ for all
$0\leq t\leq1$.
\end{lemma*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=prose-continuation
Lorentz transformations $\{\Lambda(t),\ 0\leq t\leq1\}$ such that
$\Lambda(0)=\mathbf{1}$, $\Lambda(1)=\Lambda$ and
$\Lambda(t)\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda(t)\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ for all
$0\leq t\leq1$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=prose
We shall prove this lemma in a minute; using it we can prove

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=theorem id=2-11
\begin{theorem}[2-11]\label{thm:ch2-11}
If $f_{\alpha}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)$ transforms according to \eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform} under
$SL(2,\mathbb C)$, and is holomorphic in the tube $\eta_j\in V_{+}$, where
$\zeta_j=\xi_j-i\eta_j$, $j=1,2,\ldots,n$, then $f_{\alpha}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)$
possesses a single-valued analytic continuation into the extended tube
$\mathcal{T}'_{n}$, and transforms according to \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} under
$L_{+}(\mathbb C)$.
\end{theorem}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=proof id=2-11
\begin{proof}
Suppose $w_j=\Lambda\zeta_j$, $j=1,2,\ldots,n$, where
$\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$ and $w_1,\ldots,w_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$,
$\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ and let $\Lambda(t)$ be the curve
in $L_{+}(\mathbb C)$ given by the lemma.  We know that \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} holds if
$\Lambda$ is in some complex neighborhood of the identity, and this includes a
portion of the curve $\Lambda(t)$, say $0\leq t\leq t_1$.  Since
$\Lambda(t_1)\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda(t_1)\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ we can
apply \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} to some complex neighborhood of $\Lambda(t_1)$, and using the
group property of $S(A,B)$ we show \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} holds for all $\Lambda(t)$ on the
curve up to, say, $t_2>t_1$.  Proceeding in this way (the well-known method of
analytic continuation by means of overlapping neighborhoods) in a finite
number of steps we see that \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} holds for $\Lambda=\Lambda(1)$.  The
remarks made above then show that the continuation into $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$
given by \eqref{eq:ch2-analytic-transform} is single-valued.  That the function so defined is holomorphic
is an immediate consequence of the identities

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=display
{\small
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial[\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_j]^{\mu}}
&\quad f_{\alpha}\bigl[\\[-2pt]
&\qquad\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_1,\ldots,\\[-2pt]
&\qquad\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_n\bigr]
={}&\sum_{\beta}S(A,B)_{\alpha\beta}
 \sum_{\nu=0}^{3}
 \frac{\partial f_{\beta}(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)}{\partial\zeta_j^{\nu}}
 \frac{\partial\zeta_j^{\nu}}
      {\partial[\Lambda(A,B)\zeta_j]^{\mu}} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
}
\end{proof}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=prose
\begin{proof}[Proof of the lemma]

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=prose
It is convenient to work with the $2\times2$ matrix formalism described in
Section \ref{sec:1.3}, in which $\Lambda(A,B)$ is realized by the transformation

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  z\longmapsto AzB^{\mathsf T}
  \qquad\text{(we write $z$ instead of $\zeta$).}
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=prose
We can use the invariance of the tube $\mathcal{T}_{n}$ under restricted real
Lorentz transformations to bring the $A$ and $B$ into a simple form so that the
construction of the required curve is especially easy.  We have

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=078 print=66 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  CAzB^{\mathsf T}C^*
  =\bigl[CA(B^*)^{-1}C^{-1}\bigr]CB^*zB^{\mathsf T}C^* .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose-continuation
For any $B,C$ of determinant $1$,
$z\longmapsto CB^*zB^{\mathsf T}C^*$ is a restricted real Lorentz
transformation.  Furthermore, by a suitable choice of the matrix $C$, the
square bracket can be brought into one of the two Jordan canonical forms
% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=footnote
\begingroup
\renewcommand{\thefootnote}{\ensuremath{\dagger}}
\footnote{See P.~R.~Halmos, \emph{Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces}, 2nd Ed.,
Van Nostrand, Princeton, N.J., 1958, p.~113.}
\endgroup

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=equation id=2-87
\begin{equation}
  K=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\[2pt]0&1\end{pmatrix} .
  \tag{2-87}\label{eq:ch2-jordan-unipotent}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
or

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=equation id=2-88
\begin{equation}
  K=\begin{pmatrix}
    \exp(\alpha+i\beta)&0\\[2pt]
    0&\exp[-(\alpha+i\beta)]
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \alpha,\beta\ \text{real} .
  \tag{2-88}\label{eq:ch2-jordan-diagonal}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
Thus, it suffices to show that the curves can be constructed for the Lorentz
transformations, $z\longmapsto Kz$.  We can immediately drop the case of the
minus sign in \eqref{eq:ch2-jordan-unipotent} because that $K$ carries all vectors in the tube out of
the tube.  That leaves two cases for which we propose the curves

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=equation id=2-89
\begin{equation}
  K(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\[2pt]0&1\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad 0\leq t\leq1 ,
  \tag{2-89}\label{eq:ch2-k-unipotent-curve}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=equation id=2-90
\begin{equation}
  K(t)=\begin{pmatrix}
    \exp[t(\alpha+i\beta)]&0\\[2pt]
    0&\exp[-t(\alpha+i\beta)]
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad 0\leq t\leq1 ,
  \tag{2-90}\label{eq:ch2-k-diagonal-curve}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
respectively.  Consider first \eqref{eq:ch2-k-unipotent-curve}.  If we write $z(t)=K(t)z$ we have

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  z(0)=z,\qquad
  z(1)=z+\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\[2pt]0&0\end{pmatrix}z,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
and therefore

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  z(t)=z+t\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\[2pt]0&0\end{pmatrix}z
  =(1-t)z(0)+tz(1),\qquad 0\leq t\leq1 .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
This shows that $z(t)$ is a convex linear combination of $z(0)$ and $z(1)$.
But $\mathcal{T}_{1}$ is convex, so if $z(0)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$ and
$z(1)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$, $(1-t)z(0)+tz(1)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$.  Therefore
the curve $K(t)\zeta_1,\ldots,K(t)\zeta_n$ lies in $\mathcal{T}_{n}$ if its
end points $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n$ and $K\zeta_1,\ldots,K\zeta_n$ do.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=079 print=67 kind=prose
For the other case, we shall also exploit the convexity of the tube but the
required argument is a bit more arduous.  We shall use the following criterion
for a real vector to be in the open cone $V_{+}$: $y\in V_{+}$ if and only if
$n\mathbin{\cdot}y<0$ for every $n\in C_{+}$, $n\neq0$, where $C_{+}$ is the
boundary of $V_{+}$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose-continuation
This can be proved with a straightforward application of Schwarz's inequality
for the space parts of the vectors.  It is convenient to use the notation

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  y^a=y^0+y^3,\qquad y^b=y^0-y^3,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
so the scalar product may be written

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  n\mathbin{\cdot}y
  =-\tfrac12\bigl(n^ay^b+n^by^a\bigr)+n^1y^1+n^2y^2 .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
Clearly, for every vector $n\in C_{+}$, $n^a\geq0$, $n^b\geq0$, and
$n^a+n^b=2n^0>0$, so at least one of $n^a$ and $n^b$ must be positive.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
Let us write $z(t)=K(t)z$, $z(0)=z$.  We make one more real restricted
Lorentz transformation,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \rho(t)=K(-t/2)z(t)K(-t/2)^*
  =K(t/2)zK(-t/2)^* .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
Then $z(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$ if and only if $\rho(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$.
Let $\rho^{\mu}(t)=\xi^{\mu}(t)-i\eta^{\mu}(t)$.  The proof of the lemma is complete if we
show $\eta(t)\in V_{+}$, $0\leq t\leq1$.  To obtain the vector of the matrix
$\rho(t)$, we evaluate

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}
 e^{\frac{t}{2}(\alpha+i\beta)}&0\\[2pt]
 0&e^{-\frac{t}{2}(\alpha+i\beta)}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
 z^a&z^1-iz^2\\[2pt]
 z^1+iz^2&z^b
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
 e^{-\frac{t}{2}(\alpha-i\beta)}&0\\[2pt]
 0&e^{\frac{t}{2}(\alpha-i\beta)}
\end{pmatrix} .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
that is,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}
 \rho^a(t)&\rho^1(t)-i\rho^2(t)\\[2pt]
 \rho^1(t)+i\rho^2(t)&\rho^b(t)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
 e^{i\beta t}z^a&e^{\alpha t}(z^1-iz^2)\\[2pt]
 e^{-\alpha t}(z^1+iz^2)&e^{-i\beta t}z^b
\end{pmatrix} .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
Thus

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \eta^a(t)=-\frac{1}{2i}
  \bigl(e^{i\beta t}z^a-e^{-i\beta t}(z^a)^*\bigr)
  =y^a\cos\beta t-x^a\sin\beta t,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=prose
where $z^a=x^a-iy^a$.  Similarly,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \eta^b(t)=y^b\cos\beta t+x^b\sin\beta t,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
  \eta^1(t)
  &=-\frac{\operatorname{Im}}{2}
    \bigl[e^{\alpha t}(z^1-iz^2)+e^{-\alpha t}(z^1+iz^2)\bigr]\\
  &=y^1\cosh\alpha t+x^2\sinh\alpha t,
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=080 print=68 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
  \eta^2(t)
  &=-\frac{\operatorname{Re}}{2}
    \bigl[e^{\alpha t}(z^1-iz^2)-e^{-\alpha t}(z^1+iz^2)\bigr]\\
  &=-x^1\sinh\alpha t+y^2\cosh\alpha t .
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose-continuation
The first step of the proof is to show that $\eta^a(t)>0$ and
$\eta^b(t)>0$ for $0\leq t\leq1$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
We remark that since the sign of $\sin\beta$ can be changed by replacing
$x^a,x^b$ by $-x^a,-x^b$, we may assume $0<\beta<\pi$ without loss in
generality; $\beta=0$ is trivial; $\beta=\pi$ is not possible, because then
$\eta^a(1)<0$, $\eta^b(1)<0$.  For $0<\beta<\pi$ we have the identity

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  (\sin\beta)\eta^a(t)
  =\sin[(1-t)\beta]\eta^a(0)+(\sin\beta t)\eta^a(1)
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  (\sin\beta)\eta^b(t)
  =\sin[(1-t)\beta]\eta^b(0)+(\sin\beta t)\eta^b(1) .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
This displays $\eta^a(t)$ and $\eta^b(t)$ as positive linear combinations of
their initial and final values, and so proves that they are positive.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
The second step uses the condition mentioned above for the vector $\eta(t)$ to
lie in $V_{+}$.  Let $n\in C_{+}$ be a fixed light-like vector and set

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  g(t)=-n\mathbin{\cdot}\eta(t)=f_1(t)-f_2(t)
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  f_1(t)=\tfrac12\bigl[n^a\eta^b(t)+n^b\eta^a(t)\bigr]
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  f_2(t)=n^1\eta^1(t)+n^2\eta^2(t) ,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
or, substituting,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  f_1(t)=K_1\cos\beta t+K_2\sin\beta t,
  \qquad
  f_2(t)=\lambda_1e^{\alpha t}+\lambda_2e^{-\alpha t},
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
where $K_1,K_2,\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ are some real constants.  We easily see
that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d^2f_1}{dt^2}=-\beta^2f_1,
  \qquad
  \frac{d^2f_2}{dt^2}=\alpha^2f_2,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d^2g}{dt^2}
  =-\beta^2f_1-\alpha^2f_2
  =\alpha^2g-(\alpha^2+\beta^2)f_1 .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
Because $\eta(0),\eta(1)\in V_{+}$, we have $g(0)>0$, $g(1)>0$.  Of
course $g(t)$ is an elementary function, and if $g(t)<0$ in the interval
$(0,1)$ it must have a minimum in the interval; for the minimum

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d^2g}{dt^2}\geq0,
  \qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad
  g\geq\frac{(\alpha^2+\beta^2)f_1}{\alpha^2}>0 .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
Therefore $g(t)>0$ in $(0,1)$, which proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=081 print=69 kind=prose
Since holomorphic functions with a transformation law such as \eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform} have
single-valued analytic continuations to the extended tube, it is of some
interest to characterize the extent of this domain more

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose-continuation
precisely.  We will not indulge in this sport here, except to determine its
\emph{real points} which are of great importance in the \emph{PCT} theorem.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose
By its very definition, the tube $\mathcal{T}_{n}$ does not contain real
points: $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ requires
$-\operatorname{Im}\zeta_j\in V_{+}$, $j=1,2,\ldots,n$, and therefore not
zero.  However, the extended tube $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ does contain real points,
commonly called \emph{Jost points}, as will now be explained.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose
We first consider the special case of one vector.  If $\zeta\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$,
$\zeta=\xi-i\eta$, $\eta\in V_{+}$, and the extended tube $\mathcal{T}'_{1}$
consists of all points $\Lambda\zeta$, $\Lambda\in L_{+}(\mathbb C)$,
$\zeta\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$.  Since $\Lambda\zeta\mathbin{\cdot}\Lambda\zeta
=\zeta\mathbin{\cdot}\zeta$, the values of $\zeta^2$ for $\zeta$ in
$\mathcal{T}'_{1}$ are the same as those for $\zeta$ in $\mathcal{T}_{1}$.
Now $\zeta^2=\xi^2-\eta^2-2i\xi\mathbin{\cdot}\eta$, so if $\zeta^2$ is
real and $\zeta\in\mathcal{T}'_{1}$, $\xi$ is orthogonal to a time-like
vector and therefore is space-like.  Thus $\xi^2>0$.  Hence a real point of
$\mathcal{T}'_{1}$ must have $\xi^2>0$.  This condition is also sufficient for
$\xi\in\mathcal{T}'_{1}$; for if $\xi^2>0$, and $\xi$ is real, we can choose
a coordinate system such that $\xi=(\xi^0,\xi^1,0,0)$ with
$\xi^1>|\xi^0|$; then the complex Lorentz transformation

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=equation id=2-91
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \xi^0+\xi^1&\longmapsto
    e^{i\alpha}(\xi^0+\xi^1)=\zeta^0+\zeta^1,\\
  \xi^0-\xi^1&\longmapsto
    e^{-i\alpha}(\xi^0-\xi^1)=\zeta^0-\zeta^1
\end{aligned}
\tag{2-91}\label{eq:ch2-complex-boost}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose
gives $\zeta^0=i\sin\alpha\,\xi^1+\cos\alpha\,\xi^0$,
$\zeta^1=i\sin\alpha\,\xi^0+\cos\alpha\,\xi^1$.  This transformation
takes $\xi$ into the tube if $\sin\alpha<0$ since then
$\operatorname{Im}\zeta^0<-|\operatorname{Im}\zeta^1|$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose
In the general case, we have the following theorem due to Jost.

\begin{theorem}[2-12 \emph{(Jost's Theorem)}]\label{thm:ch2-12-jost}
A real point
$\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n$ lies in the extended tube $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ if and
only if all vectors of the form

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=display id=2-92a
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\zeta_j,
  \qquad \lambda_j\geq0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j>0
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose
are space-like; that is,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=equation id=2-92
\begin{equation}
  \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\zeta_j\right)^2>0
  \quad\text{for all }\lambda_j\geq0\text{ with }\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j>0 .
  \tag{2-92}\label{eq:ch2-jost-condition}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=proof id=2-12
\begin{proof}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=prose
We first prove the necessity of \eqref{eq:ch2-jost-condition}.  By definition
$\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ if there exists a proper complex
Lorentz transformation $\Lambda$ such that
$\Lambda\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda\zeta_n\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$.  Now
$(\Lambda\zeta_j)^2=\zeta_j^2$, and we have already seen that $z^2$ real,
$z\in\mathcal{T}_{1}$ is possible only if $z^2>0$.  Therefore
$\zeta_j^2>0$, $j=1,2,\ldots,n$.  Moreover, $\mathcal{T}_{1}$ is convex so
that if each of the complex four-vectors
$\Lambda\zeta_1,\ldots,\Lambda\zeta_n$ is in $\mathcal{T}_{1}$, every linear
combination of the form

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=082 print=70 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\Lambda\zeta_j,
  \qquad \lambda_j\geq0,\quad j=1,2,\ldots,n,\quad
  \sum_j\lambda_j>0
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose-continuation
is in $\mathcal{T}_{1}$.  Therefore

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \left[\Lambda\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\zeta_j\right)\right]^2>0,
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
which reduces to

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \left(\sum_j\lambda_j\zeta_j\right)^2>0 .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
This proves the necessity of \eqref{eq:ch2-jost-condition}.  To prove the sufficiency, suppose the
vectors $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n$ satisfy \eqref{eq:ch2-jost-condition}.  They then span a convex
space-like cone $K$, which intersects neither the plus nor the minus light
cone.  We can find two planes $(\alpha)$, $(\beta)$, touching the forward and
backward cones, respectively, which separate $K$ from these cones (see
Figure~\ref{fig:2-3}).  This is possible because any two such convex sets can be so
separated.%
% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=footnote
\begingroup
\renewcommand{\thefootnote}{\ensuremath{\dagger}}
\footnote{Instead of using this general property of convex sets one can argue
more directly.  We know that if $\rho$ is a time-like and $\zeta$ a space-like
vector then $\alpha\rho+\beta\zeta$ cannot be zero unless both $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ are zero.  Now we look for solutions, $n$, of the sets of simultaneous
inequalities $n\mathbin{\cdot}\rho>0$, $n\mathbin{\cdot}\zeta>0$, where $\rho$
runs over the minus light cone, $V_{-}$, and $\zeta$ over the convex cone
spanned by the $\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n$.  It is easy to see that this set of
inequalities will have no solution if and only if the convex cone spanned by
the $\rho$'s and $\zeta$'s contains the zero vector.  From the above argument
this is impossible, so there is at least one solution, $n$.  Then the plane
$n\mathbin{\cdot}\zeta=0$ is a separating plane, and it is not difficult to see
that one of the solutions lies in $C_{+}$, the boundary of $V_{+}$.}
\endgroup

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
Let the equations of $(\alpha)$, $(\beta)$ be

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  \alpha_{\mu}\zeta^{\mu}=0,\qquad \beta_{\mu}\zeta^{\mu}=0 .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are light-like, and $\alpha\mathbin{\cdot}\beta>0$.
We can choose a coordinate system so that
$\alpha=(1,1,0,0)$, $\beta=(-1,+1,0,0)$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
For points in $V_{+}$, $\zeta\mathbin{\cdot}\alpha<0$, so for points below the
plane $\alpha$, $\zeta\mathbin{\cdot}\alpha>0$, which, in the particular
coordinate system, means

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=display
\begin{equation*}
  -\zeta^0+\zeta^1>0\qquad\text{if }\zeta\in K .
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
Similarly, if $-\zeta\in V_{+}$, $\beta\mathbin{\cdot}\zeta<0$, so
$\beta\mathbin{\cdot}\zeta>0$ for points above $(\beta)$, that is,
$\zeta^0+\zeta^1>0$ for points in $K$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=083 print=71 kind=prose
Therefore $\zeta^1>|\zeta^0|$ for all points in $K$, in particular for
$\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n$ given.  We therefore can apply the complex Lorentz
transformation used above, \eqref{eq:ch2-complex-boost}, which takes $\zeta_k$ into the forward
tube, $k=1,2,\ldots,n$.  This shows the sufficiency of \eqref{eq:ch2-jost-condition}.
\end{proof}

Theorem \ref{thm:ch2-11} shows that any functions holomorphic in $\mathcal{T}_{n}$ and
satisfying a transformation law such as \eqref{eq:ch2-tube-transform} possess single-valued analytic
continuations to $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$.  However, nothing which has been said so
far excludes that they can be continued even farther.  For

\input{figures/fig2_3}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=084 print=72 kind=prose-continuation
real points Theorem \ref{thm:ch2-12-jost} leads the way to simple examples which show that
such a continuation is generally impossible: the function

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=084 print=72 kind=display id=2-12a
\begin{equation*}
  \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\cdots\!\int_{0}^{\infty}
  dK\,d\lambda_1\,\cdots\,d\lambda_n\,
  \frac{\rho(K,\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)}
  {\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\zeta_j\right)^2-K^2}
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=084 print=72 kind=prose
is analytic in $\mathcal{T}_{n}$, invariant under Lorentz transformations but
singular at all real points which are not Jost points if $\rho$ is suitably
chosen.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=084 print=72 kind=prose
It is clear that the Jost points of $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ form a real environment
for holomorphic functions in $\mathbb C^{4n}$; thus if a holomorphic function
vanishes at Jost points, it vanishes everywhere.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=084 print=72 kind=prose
Later on, in our study of the commutation relations of fields, we shall be
concerned not only with the extended tube $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ but also with the
so-called \emph{permuted extended tubes}.  There is one of these domains for
each element of the permutation group, $S_{n+1}$, of $n+1$ objects,
% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=085 print=73 kind=prose-continuation
and they are obtained by linear transformations from $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$.  For later
purposes it suffices to discuss the particular domain which corresponds to the
transposition of the $j$ with the $j+1$ object.  The corresponding linear
transformation is denoted $P(j,j+1)$ and is defined by

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=085 print=73 kind=equation id=2-93
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \zeta'_k&=\zeta_k, &&1\leq k<j-1\quad\text{and}\quad j+1<k\leq n,\\
  \zeta'_{j-1}&=\zeta_{j-1}+\zeta_j,\\
  \zeta'_j&=-\zeta_j,\\
  \zeta'_{j+1}&=\zeta_{j+1}+\zeta_j .
\end{aligned}
\tag{2-93}\label{eq:ch2-permutation}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=085 print=73 kind=prose
with the convention that if a $\zeta$ appears with index $<1$ or $>n$ the
equation is to be deleted.  All the rest of the permuted extended tubes can be
generated from these.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=085 print=73 kind=prose
Our purpose here is to establish that $\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ and
$P(j,j+1)\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ have a real environment in common.  To this end,
we choose $\zeta_k=(0,b,0,0)$ for $k\neq j-1,j$, or $j+1$, and
$\zeta_{j-1}=(a,b,0,0)$, $\zeta_j=(0,0,\varepsilon,0)$,
$\zeta_{j+1}=(-a,b,0,0)$, where $0<|a|<b$.  All the vectors,
$\lambda_{j-1}\zeta_{j-1}+\lambda_j\zeta_j+\lambda_{j+1}\zeta_{j+1}$ with
$\lambda$'s $\geq0$ and not all zero, are space-like, and so are all the
vectors
$\lambda_{j-1}(\zeta_{j-1}+\zeta_j)-\lambda_j\zeta_j+
\lambda_{j+1}(\zeta_{j+1}+\zeta_j)$ (see Figure~2-4).  It is easy to see
that adjoining the rest of the $\zeta_k$ gives a Jost point of both
$\mathcal{T}'_{n}$ and $P(j,j+1)\mathcal{T}'_{n}$, and that the same is true
for all real points in a sufficiently small neighborhood of these.
