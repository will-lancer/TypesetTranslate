\chapterbibliography

The standard work on distribution theory is

\begin{thebibliography}{25}\small
\bibitem{pct-1}
L. Schwartz, \emph{Th\'eorie des distributions}, Hermann, Paris, Part I,
1957, Part II, 1959.

Another systematic treatise is

\bibitem{pct-2}
I. Gelfand and co-authors, \emph{Generalized Functions I\ldots V} (in
Russian) Gosizdatmatlit, Moscow, 1958.

A very useful summary for physicists is

\bibitem{pct-3}
L. G\aa rding and J. Lions, ``Functional Analysis,'' \emph{Nuovo Cimento
Suppl.}, 14, 9 (1959).  The brief exposition of Sections \ref{sec:2-1} and \ref{sec:ch2-fourier} is not
a substitute for G\aa rding--Lions, which in turn, as those authors themselves
remark, is not a substitute for Schwartz's book.  The proofs of Theorems
\ref{thm:ch2-laplace-forward-cone}, \ref{thm:ch2-laplace-boundary-value}, and
\ref{thm:ch2-laplace-tempered-bound} follow unpublished remarks of L. G\aa rding.

The most elementary proof of the nuclear theorem is due to Gelfand and
Vilenkin, \cite{pct-2}, Vol.~IV. Other proofs may be found in

\bibitem{pct-4}
L. Ehrenpreis, ``On the Theory of the Kernels of Schwartz,'' \emph{Proc.
Amer. Math. Soc.}, 7, 713 (1956).

\bibitem{pct-5}
H. Gask, ``A Proof of Schwartz's Kernel Theorem,'' \emph{Math. Scand.}, 8,
327 (1960).

For the Laplace transform, see

\bibitem{pct-6}
L. Schwartz, ``Transformation de Laplace des distributions,'' \emph{Medd.
Lunds Mat. Sem. Supplementband}, p.~196 (1952).

Holomorphic functions of several complex variables are treated in

\bibitem{pct-7}
S. Bochner and W. T. Martin, \emph{Several Complex Variables}, Princeton
University Press, Princeton, N.J., 1948.

A summary for physicists, with further references, is

\bibitem{pct-8}
A. S. Wightman, ``Analytic Functions of Several Complex Variables,'' pp.~159--221
in \emph{Dispersion Relations and Elementary Particles}, Wiley, New York,
1960.

Theorem \ref{thm:ch2-11} was first proved for invariant holomorphic functions in the
thesis of D. Hall; see

\bibitem{pct-9}
D. Hall and A. S. Wightman, ``A Theorem on Invariant Analytic Functions with
Applications to Relativistic Quantum Field Theory,'' \emph{Dan. Mat. Fys.
Medd.}, 31, No.~5 (1957).

The extension to sets of fields with an arbitrary transformation law is in

\bibitem{pct-10}
R. Jost, ``Properties of Wightman Functions'' in \emph{Lectures on Field
Theory and the Many-Body Problem}, E. R. Caianiello (ed.), Academic Press,
New York, 1961; and

\bibitem{pct-11}
A. S. Wightman, ``Quantum Field Theory and Analytic Functions of Several
Complex Variables,'' \emph{J. Indian Math. Soc.}, 24, 625 (1960).

The proof of the crucial lemma given in the text is due to V. Bargmann
(unpublished).

Theorem \ref{thm:ch2-12-jost} is due to R. Jost:

\bibitem{pct-12}
R. Jost, ``Eine Bemerkung zum CTP Theorem,'' \emph{Helv. Phys. Acta}, 30,
409 (1957).

Painlev\'e's proof of Theorem \ref{thm:edge-of-wedge-one-variable} appears in

\bibitem{pct-13}
P. Painlev\'e, ``Sur les lignes singuli\`eres des fonctions analytiques,''
\emph{Ann. Fac. Toulouse}, 2, B27 (1888).

The edge of the wedge theorem so named first appears in

\bibitem{pct-14}
H. J. Bremmermann, R. Oehme, and J. G. Taylor, ``A Proof of Dispersion
Relations in Quantized Field Theories,'' \emph{Phys. Rev.}, 109, 2178 (1958).
The proof given is only valid under very strong restrictions on the behavior
of the functions and their derivatives near the boundary, and is not valid
for distribution boundary values.  Earlier, in the course of a proof of
dispersion relations, Bogoliubov, Medvedev, and Polivanov had given arguments
which are quite adequate to handle the case of distribution boundary values,
but since the whole matter appeared in a specific context they did not try to
isolate the edge of the wedge theorem.

\bibitem{pct-15}
N. N. Bogoliubov and D. V. Shirkov, \emph{Quantum Theory of Fields},
Interscience, New York, 1958, Russian edition, 1956.

\bibitem{pct-16}
N. N. Bogoliubov, B. V. Medvedev, M. K. Polivanov, \emph{Questions of the
Theory of Dispersion Relations} (in Russian), Moscow, 1958.

More specifically, the theorem as here stated is a local theorem about
holomorphic functions, whereas in the context of dispersion relations one has
analytic functions which are typically Laplace transforms of functions with
restricted support.  See, however, V. S. Vladimirov, On Bogoliubov's ``Edge of
the Wedge Theorem,'' \emph{Izvestia Akad. Nauk USSR}, 26, 825 (1962).

Dyson outlined a proof ``without pretensions to rigor'' which is very simple
and natural.  Its ideas are at the heart of the proof we present here.

\bibitem{pct-17}
F. J. Dyson, ``Connection between Local Commutativity and Regularity of
Wightman Functions,'' \emph{Phys. Rev.}, 110, 579 (1958).  This proof was
made precise by G\aa rding and Beurling in a much quoted article which has
never appeared; Theorem \ref{thm:edge-of-wedge-distributional} follows' their method.

The edge of the wedge theorem was generalized to the case in which the cones
to which the imaginary parts are confined are not opposite by Epstein:

\bibitem{pct-18}
H. Epstein, ``Generalization of the `Edge-of-the-Wedge' Theorem,''
\emph{J. Math. Phys.}, 1, 524 (1960).

A proof of the edge of the wedge theorem which may have advantages to the
mathematician, because of the extensive exercise in functional analysis it
provides, is

\bibitem{pct-19}
F. Browder, ``On the `Edge of the Wedge' Theorem,'' \emph{Can. Math. J.}, 15,
125 (1963).

In the elementary proof of Theorems \ref{thm:edge-of-wedge-product-half-planes} and \ref{thm:edge-of-wedge-cone} given here we have
incorporated unpublished suggestions of H. Borchers and V. Glaser.

The classic introduction to the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics
is

\bibitem{pct-20}
J. von Neumann, \emph{Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics},
Princeton University Press, Princeton, N.J., 1955.  The separability of the
Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is established on p.~64.

An economical account of the closed graph theorem is on pp.~17--18 of

\bibitem{pct-21}
L. H. Loomis, \emph{Abstract Harmonic Analysis}, Van Nostrand, Princeton,
N.J., 1953.

The S, N, A, G in the SNAG theorem stand for Stone, Naimark, Ambrose, and
Godement. An account of it may be had by combining Chapter 10 of \cite{pct-25}
with Chapter II of

\bibitem{pct-22}
J. Dixmier, \emph{Les alg\`ebres d'op\'erateurs dans l'espace hilbertien
(Alg\`ebres de von Neumann)}, Gauthier Villars, Paris, 1956.

An example of two noncommuting self-adjoint operators which commute on a
common dense domain of essential self-adjointness is given in

\bibitem{pct-23}
E. Nelson, ``Analytic Vectors,'' \emph{Ann. Math.}, 70, 572 (1959), especially
pp.~603--604.

The separability of $\mathcal{H}$ is proved on p.~373 of

\bibitem{pct-24}
G. K\"othe, \emph{Topologische lineare R\"aume}, I, Springer, Berlin, 1960.

Another useful reference on the commutability of unbounded operators is

\bibitem{pct-25}
F. Riesz and B. Sz.-Nagy, \emph{Functional Analysis}, Ungar, New York, 1955,
especially Section 116.
\end{thebibliography}
