% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=section-heading id=2-6
\subsection{Hilbert Space}\label{sec:2-6}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=prose id=2-6-opening
Throughout Chapter \ref{ch:relativistic-transformation-laws}, we have taken for granted that the reader is
acquainted with the elementary theory of Hilbert space.  Here we make a
series of disconnected remarks which we hope will relate the elementary
theory to some of the arguments used elsewhere in the book.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=prose id=2-6-hilbert-definition
Recall that a Hilbert space, $\mathcal{H}$, is a vector space with scalars
which are complex numbers,\footnote{There are of course also real Hilbert
spaces which use real scalars but they will not be considered here.} and a
scalar product $\braket{\Phi}{\Psi}$ which is a complex-valued function of
two vectors in $\mathcal{H}$ satisfying

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=display id=2-6-scalar-product
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\Phi}{\Psi}=\overline{\braket{\Psi}{\Phi}},
  \qquad
  \braket{\Phi}{\alpha\Psi+\beta\chi}
  =\alpha\braket{\Phi}{\Psi}+\beta\braket{\Phi}{\chi},
\end{equation*}
and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=display id=2-111
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\ket{\Phi}\rVert^2\equiv\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}\geq 0,
  \tag{2-111}\label{eq:pct-2-111}
\end{equation}
and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=display id=2-112
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=0
  \quad\text{implies}\quad
  \ket{\Phi}=0.
  \tag{2-112}\label{eq:pct-2-112}
\end{equation}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=prose id=2-6-completeness
Furthermore, $\mathcal{H}$ is required to be \emph{complete} in the sense
that any Cauchy sequence of vectors has a limit.  That means if
$\ket{\Phi_n}$, $n=1,2,\ldots$, is a sequence of vectors such that for each
$\epsilon>0$ there is an integer $N$ such that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=display id=2-6-cauchy-condition
\begin{equation*}
  \lVert\ket{\Phi_n}-\ket{\Phi_m}\rVert<\epsilon
  \qquad\text{for all }n,m\geq N
\end{equation*}
(then $\ket{\Phi_n}$, $n=1,2,\ldots$, is called a \emph{Cauchy sequence}),
there exists a vector $\ket{\Phi}$ such that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=96 print=84 kind=display id=2-6-cauchy-limit
\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert\ket{\Phi_n}-\ket{\Phi}\rVert=0.
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=97 print=85 kind=prose id=2-6-separability
The first point we want to discuss is the separability of the Hilbert spaces
occurring in quantum field theory.  Recall that a set $S$ of vectors is
\emph{dense} in $\mathcal{H}$ if, for each vector $\ket{\Phi}\in\mathcal{H}$
and $\epsilon>0$, there exists a vector $\ket{\Psi}\in S$ such that
$\lVert\ket{\Phi}-\ket{\Psi}\rVert<\epsilon$.  A Hilbert space is
\emph{separable} if it contains a denumerable dense set, or, in other words,
there is a sequence of vectors which is dense.  In non-separable Hilbert
spaces a continuous index is necessary to label a dense set.  An alternative
way of describing this distinction is in terms of complete orthonormal sets.
A Hilbert space is separable if it contains a denumerable complete
orthonormal set; it is non-separable if complete orthonormal sets are not
denumerable.  One can pass from one description to the other by
orthonormalizing the dense set to get a denumerable complete orthonormal set,
or forming finite linear combinations of the denumerable complete
orthonormal set with complex numbers whose real and imaginary parts are
rational to get a denumerable dense set.  In von Neumann's original
axiomatization the requirement of separability was included as a defining
property of Hilbert space.  Nowadays, it has become conventional to use the
name to cover the non-separable case also.  Of late, physicists have started
considering vector spaces with a scalar product in which \eqref{eq:pct-2-111} and \eqref{eq:pct-2-112}
are abandoned (indefinite metric).  We shall not call such spaces Hilbert
spaces, nor, in fact, consider them at all.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=97 print=85 kind=prose id=2-6-nonrelativistic
In non-relativistic quantum mechanics it is natural to consider only
separable Hilbert spaces because one is usually dealing with a finite number
of particles and can realize the states as vectors in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$,
i.e., as equivalence classes of square integrable functions on $n$-dimensional
euclidean space, two functions being equivalent if they differ on a set of
measure zero.  It is well known that this Hilbert space is separable (Ref.~20).
It is sometimes argued that in quantum field theory one is dealing with a
system of an infinite number of degrees of freedom and so must use a
non-separable Hilbert space.  Roughly, the idea is

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=97 print=85 kind=display id=2-6-separability-schematic
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
&\left(\begin{array}{c}
  \text{system of a finite number}\\
  \text{of degrees of freedom}
\end{array}\right)
\longleftrightarrow
\left(\begin{array}{c}
  \text{separable Hilbert}\\
  \text{space}
\end{array}\right)\\[1ex]
&\left(\begin{array}{c}
  \text{system of an infinite number of}\\
  \text{degrees of freedom or something}
\end{array}\right)
\longleftrightarrow
\left(\begin{array}{c}
  \text{non-separable Hilbert}\\
  \text{space}
\end{array}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=97 print=85 kind=prose id=2-6-separability-conclusion
Our next task is to explain why this is wrong, or at best grossly
misleading.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=97 print=85 kind=prose id=2-6-direct-sum-opening
In the first place note that if $\mathcal{H}_1,\mathcal{H}_2,\mathcal{H}_3,\ldots$
is a sequence of separable Hilbert spaces then their direct sum
$\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_j$ is a separable Hilbert

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=98 print=86 kind=prose id=2-6-direct-sum-opening-continuation
space.  The direct sum has elements which are sequences
$\{\ket{\Phi_1},\ket{\Phi_2},\ldots\}$ with $\ket{\Phi_j}\in\mathcal{H}_j$,
and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=98 print=86 kind=display id=2-6-direct-sum-norm
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lVert\ket{\Phi_j}\rVert^2<\infty.
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=98 print=86 kind=prose id=2-6-direct-sum-product
The scalar product in $\bigoplus_j\mathcal{H}_j$ is

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=98 print=86 kind=display id=2-6-direct-sum-product-display
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\Phi}{\Psi}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\braket{\Phi_j}{\Psi_j},
\end{equation*}
where $\braket{\Phi_j}{\Psi_j}$ is the scalar product in $\mathcal{H}_j$ and
$\ket{\Phi}$ stands for $\{\ket{\Phi_1},\ket{\Phi_2},\ldots\}$,
$\ket{\Psi}$ for $\{\ket{\Psi_1},\ket{\Psi_2},\ldots\}$.  It is not hard to
see that sequences which have only a finite number of elements different from
zero, and the non-zero ones belonging to denumerable dense sets of their
respective $\mathcal{H}_j$, form a denumerable dense set in
$\bigoplus_j\mathcal{H}_j$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=98 print=86 kind=prose id=2-6-fock-direct-sum
The direct sum can be used to construct state vectors which are
superpositions of states for an arbitrarily large number of particles.  One
merely takes $\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_n$, where $n=0,1,2,\ldots$ and
$\mathcal{H}_n$ describes the states of $n$ particles.  Just this Hilbert
space will be used in Chapter \ref{sec:ch3-fields-and-vacuum-expectation-values} to give explicit formulas for the theory of a
free field.  This is a clear-cut counter example to the above assertion; a
free field is a system of an infinite number of degrees of freedom.  Of
course, this example does not describe interacting particles, but, to the
extent that the arguments of Chapter \ref{ch:relativistic-transformation-laws} are correct, the presence of
interaction makes no difference.  The collision states of an asymptotically
complete theory span a separable Hilbert space, because they are again just of
the form $\bigoplus_n\mathcal{H}_n$.  [Here, $\mathcal{H}_n$ is the subspace
spanned by the ingoing (or outgoing) collision states with $n$ colliding
particles.]  All these arguments make it clear that there is no evidence
that separable Hilbert spaces are not the \emph{natural state spaces} for
quantum field theory.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=98 print=86 kind=prose id=2-6-nonseparable-cases
When, in fact, do non-separable Hilbert spaces appear in quantum mechanics?
There are two cases which deserve mention.  The first arises when one takes
an infinite tensor product of separable Hilbert spaces.  We shall not give the
rather technical definition of infinite tensor product here but only remark
that it is a natural generalization of the ordinary tensor product which is
used to describe a composite system.  Infinite tensor products of Hilbert
spaces (of dimension greater than 1!) are always non-separable.  Since a
(Bose) field can be thought as a system composed of an infinity of
oscillators, one might think that such an infinite tensor product is the
natural state space.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=99 print=87 kind=prose id=2-6-separable-subspace
However, it is characteristic of field theory that some of its observables
involve all the oscillators at once and it turns out that such observables can
be naturally defined only on vectors belonging to a tiny separable subset of
the infinite tensor product.  It is the subspace spanned by such a subset
which is the natural state space rather than the whole infinite tensor
product itself.  Thus, while it may be a matter of convenience to regard the
state space as part of the infinite tensor product, it is not necessary.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=99 print=87 kind=prose id=2-6-statistical-mechanics
A second example of the occurrence of non-separable Hilbert spaces appears
in statistical mechanics when one passes to the limit in which the
conventional box containing the system becomes arbitrarily large, the
density being maintained constant.  Two states of the limiting system which
have different densities actually differ by the presence of an infinite
number of particles.  One might expect them to be orthogonal and in fact that
is the case in all examples worked out so far.  Thus there is an orthonormal
system labeled by a continuous parameter, the density, and the Hilbert space
is non-separable.  It seems to us that such phenomena are consequences of
considering systems in which every state contains an actual infinity of
physical particles and provide no argument for analogous phenomena in
relativistic quantum field theory.  This completes our discussion of
separability.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=99 print=87 kind=prose id=2-6-operators-opening
Now we turn to some remarks on linear operators in Hilbert space.  Later on
in the book there is a good deal of muttering about domains of unbounded
operators.  There is a general feeling among physicists that anything that
depends in an important way on such matters cannot be physics.  We would
like to offer some arguments to the contrary.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=99 print=87 kind=prose id=2-6-operator-definition
Recall that an operator, $A$, from a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_1$ to a
Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_2$ is a function defined on a subset of
$\mathcal{H}_1$, called the \emph{domain} of $A$, $D(A)$, and taking values
in $\mathcal{H}_2$, the set of those values being called the \emph{range} of
$A$, $R(A)$.  The \emph{graph}, $\Gamma(A)$, of $A$ is the set of all pairs
$\{\ket{\Phi},A\ket{\Phi}\}$ with $\ket{\Phi}\in D(A)$.  It is a subset of
$\mathcal{H}_1\oplus\mathcal{H}_2$.  $A$ is linear if its graph is a linear
manifold in $\mathcal{H}_1\oplus\mathcal{H}_2$.  This amounts to saying that
if $\ket{\Phi_1}$ and $\ket{\Phi_2}\in D(A)$, then
$\alpha\ket{\Phi_1}+\beta\ket{\Phi_2}\in D(A)$ and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=99 print=87 kind=display id=2-6-linearity
\begin{equation*}
  A\bigl(\alpha\ket{\Phi_1}+\beta\ket{\Phi_2}\bigr)
  =\alpha A\ket{\Phi_1}+\beta A\ket{\Phi_2},
\end{equation*}
which is the usual definition of linearity.  $A$ is closed if its graph is a
closed set in $\mathcal{H}_1\oplus\mathcal{H}_2$, that is, if
$\ket{\Phi_n}$, $n=1,2,\ldots\in D(A)$, and
$\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert\ket{\Phi_n}-\ket{\Phi}\rVert=0$, and
$\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert A\ket{\Phi_n}-\ket{\Psi}\rVert=0$, then
$\ket{\Phi}\in D(A)$ and $A\ket{\Phi}=\ket{\Psi}$.  An operator $B$ is an
extension of $A$ if $\Gamma(A)\subset\Gamma(B)$.  That means $D(A)\subset
D(B)$ and $A\ket{\Phi}=B\ket{\Phi}$ for all $\ket{\Phi}\in D(A)$.  We write
$A\subset B$ in this case.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=prose id=2-6-orthogonal-complement
If $S$ is any set in $\mathcal{H}_1\oplus\mathcal{H}_2$ its \emph{orthogonal
complement} is the set $S^\perp$ of all vectors orthogonal to every vector
$S$.  $S^\perp$ is always a closed linear manifold.  If $S$ happens to be the
graph of an operator from $\mathcal{H}_1$ to $\mathcal{H}_2$, it may happen
that $S^\perp$ is the graph of an operator from $\mathcal{H}_2$ to
$\mathcal{H}_1$.  Whether it is or not depends entirely on whether
$\{\ket{\Phi},\ket{\Psi}\}$ and $\{\ket{\chi},\ket{\Psi}\}\in S^\perp$
implies $\ket{\Phi}=\ket{\chi}$; if not, the presumed operator would not be
single-valued, as it must be by the definition of the notion operator.  The
condition that $\{\ket{\Phi},\ket{\Psi}\}$ and
$\{\ket{\chi},\ket{\Psi}\}\in S^\perp$ is

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=display id=2-6-orthogonal-condition-one
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\Phi}{\Phi_1}+\matrixel{\Psi}{A}{\Phi_1}=0
\end{equation*}
and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=display id=2-6-orthogonal-condition-two
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\chi}{\Phi_1}+\matrixel{\Psi}{A}{\Phi_1}=0
  \qquad\text{for all }\ket{\Phi_1}\in D(A).
\end{equation*}
Subtracting we see that $\braket{\Phi-\chi}{\Phi_1}=0$, so that if
$D(A)$ is dense, $\ket{\Phi}=\ket{\chi}$ and $S^\perp$ is the graph of a
transformation.  The negative of this transformation is called the
\emph{adjoint} $A^\dagger$ of $A$.  In other words, $A^\dagger$ is the
transformation from $\mathcal{H}_2$ to $\mathcal{H}_1$ defined for just those
$\ket{\Psi}\in\mathcal{H}_2$ such that there exists a $\ket{\chi}\in
\mathcal{H}_1$ satisfying

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=display id=2-6-adjoint-definition
\begin{equation*}
  \matrixel{\Psi}{A}{\Phi}=\braket{\chi}{\Phi}
  \qquad\text{for all }\ket{\Phi}\in D(A).
\end{equation*}
Then, by definition, $\ket{\chi}=A^\dagger\ket{\Psi}$.  Clearly, by
definition $A^\dagger$ is a closed linear transformation when it exists at
all.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=prose id=2-6-adjoint-extensions
The adjoint plays an important role in the construction of closed linear
extensions of a given operator.  Clearly, any closed linear extension of $A$
must be an extension of the operator whose graph is $\overline{\Gamma(A)}$,
the closure in $\mathcal{H}_1\oplus\mathcal{H}_2$ of $\Gamma(A)$.  Since
$\overline{\Gamma(A)}=\{\Gamma(A)^\perp\}^\perp$, the preceding criterion
says that $(A^\dagger)^\dagger$ is this minimal closed linear extension of
$A$ if the domains of $A$ and $A^\dagger$ are dense so that both
$A^\dagger$ and $(A^\dagger)^\dagger$ exist.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=prose id=2-6-self-adjoint
An operator from $\mathcal{H}_1$ to $\mathcal{H}_1$ is \emph{hermitian} if
$A\subset A^\dagger$, and \emph{self-adjoint} if $A=A^\dagger$.
(Sometimes these are respectively denoted symmetric and
hypermaximal-symmetric.)  The operator is \emph{essentially self-adjoint} if
$A^\dagger=(A^\dagger)^\dagger$.  Clearly, $A$ is hermitian if and only if

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=100 print=88 kind=display id=2-6-hermitian-condition
\begin{equation*}
  \matrixel{\Phi}{A}{\Psi}=\braket{A\Phi}{\Psi}
  \qquad\text{for all }\ket{\Phi},\ket{\Psi}\in D(A),
\end{equation*}
and $D(A)$ is dense.  This is what is customarily meant by hermitian in
elementary quantum mechanics, but what is relevant if $A$ is to be observable
is self-adjointness.  Only then are the eigenfunctions of $A$ complete.  (For
a full explanation see Ref.~20.)  Evidently, essential self-adjointness is
just as good as self-adjointness from this point of view because
$(A^\dagger)^\dagger$ is uniquely determined by $A$.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=101 print=89 kind=prose id=2-6-bounded-definition
An operator, $A$, is \emph{bounded} if
$\lVert A\ket{\Phi}\rVert/\lVert\ket{\Phi}\rVert$ is bounded as $\ket{\Phi}$
runs over $D(A)$, that is,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=101 print=89 kind=display id=2-6-bounded-inequality
\begin{equation*}
  \lVert A\ket{\Phi}\rVert\leq M\lVert\ket{\Phi}\rVert
  \qquad \ket{\Phi}\in D(A)
\end{equation*}
for some $M$ independent of $\ket{\Phi}$.  A bounded linear operator is
evidently continuous because
$\lVert A\ket{\Phi}-A\ket{\Psi}\rVert\leq
M\lVert\ket{\Phi}-\ket{\Psi}\rVert$.  Conversely, a linear operator is
continuous at every point of its domain if it is continuous at $0$, and if it
is continuous at $0$ it is bounded.  The first of these statements is an
obvious consequence of linearity.  The second requires only the following
simple argument.  Suppose $\lVert A\ket{\Phi}\rVert<\epsilon$ for all
$\ket{\Phi}\in D(A)$ such that $\lVert\ket{\Phi}\rVert<\delta$.  Then, if
$\ket{\Psi}$ is any element of $D(A)$,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=101 print=89 kind=display id=2-6-bounded-proof
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\delta}{2\lVert\ket{\Psi}\rVert}\ket{\Psi}\in D(A)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \left\lVert\frac{\delta}{2\lVert\ket{\Psi}\rVert}\ket{\Psi}\right\rVert<\delta
  \quad\text{so}\quad
  \left\lVert A\left(\frac{\delta}{2\lVert\ket{\Psi}\rVert}\ket{\Psi}\right)\right\rVert
  <\epsilon,
\end{equation*}
that is, $\lVert A\ket{\Psi}\rVert<(2\epsilon/\delta)\lVert\ket{\Psi}\rVert$,
proving $A$ is bounded.  A bounded linear operator $A$ always has a linear
extension whose domain is all of $\mathcal{H}_1$.  We shall outline the proof.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=101 print=89 kind=prose id=2-6-bounded-extension
One first extends $A$ by continuity to all limit points of $D(A)$, defining
$A$ at the limit point as the limit of its values at neighboring points of
$D(A)$.  This extension is just $(A^\dagger)^\dagger$.  If the closure of
$D(A)$ is all of $\mathcal{H}_1$, the construction is complete.  If not, one
can define $A$ arbitrarily (but linearly and boundedly) on the orthogonal
complement of $D(A)$ and complete the definition to $\mathcal{H}_1$ by
linearity.  If $A$ is an operator from $\mathcal{H}_1$ to $\mathcal{H}_1$ and
is hermitian, linear, and bounded, clearly it can always be arranged that
its extension to all of $\mathcal{H}_1$ is self-adjoint.  Thus in a
consideration of bounded linear operators, there is no loss in generality in
regarding them as everywhere defined.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=101 print=89 kind=prose id=2-6-unbounded
The situation for unbounded linear operators is completely different.  An
unbounded linear operator is discontinuous at every point of its domain.
Furthermore, if it is closed it cannot be everywhere defined because a closed
everywhere-defined linear operator is necessarily bounded.  (This last
statement is the \emph{closed graph theorem}.  For a proof see Ref.~21.)  We
are particularly interested in closed operators for two reasons.  On the one
hand, we need to study self-adjoint extensions of hermitian operators $A$.  An
hermitian $A$ always has a closed linear extension $(A^\dagger)^\dagger$ and
any self-adjoint extension of $A$ is an extension of $(A^\dagger)^\dagger$.
On the other hand, we shall want to study non-hermitian operators $A$ with
dense domains, and their adjoints are always closed.  It is clear that the
best we can hope for in these situations are operators which are densely but
not everywhere defined and everywhere discontinuous on their domains.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=102 print=90 kind=prose id=2-6-pathology
These facts are part of what is generally regarded as the pathology of
Hilbert space, and a natural reaction to them is to make a declaration that
only bounded operators will be considered: the only operators are bounded
operators!  There are even good physical grounds for this position, for, as is
explained at great length in Ref.~20, an unbounded operator which represents
an observable must be self-adjoint and statements about observations of it can
be re-expressed in terms of its spectral projections which are bounded
operators.  There are several reasons why, nevertheless, the axioms of
Chapter \ref{sec:ch3-fields-and-vacuum-expectation-values} have been expressed in terms of unbounded operators.  First, it is
these quantities, corresponding directly to the classical fields, which are
the source of inspiration for the quantum theory of fields.  Second, the
equations which describe local interaction between fields in space-time are
expressed in terms of such unbounded operators.  It may be said that these
arguments express exactly what is wrong with quantum field theory and that is
a defensible view, but the main point of the enterprise which Chapters 3 and 4
represent is the exploration, with all the resources of modern mathematics,
of the physical ideas which have developed in the course of the last fifty
years.  To him who wants to make a more radical alteration in the foundations
we say Bravo! and Bon voyage!

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=102 print=90 kind=prose id=2-6-domain-problems
Having once resigned ourselves to dealing with unbounded operators, we face
a couple of practical problems having to do with their domains.  It can
happen that $A^2$ is undefined except on the zero vector because it may be
that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=102 print=90 kind=display id=2-6-domain-intersection
\begin{equation*}
  \{A D(A)\}\cap D(A)=\{0\}.
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=102 print=90 kind=prose id=2-6-common-domain
What is worse, we shall have to deal with sets of unbounded operators and
shall want to compute polynomials in them.  It requires a special assumption
to be sure that such operations make sense even on a dense set of vectors.
One is led naturally to assume a common dense domain, $D$, for all the
unbounded operators in question on which all polynomials are applicable.  Once
one has $D$, another problem arises: to what extent do the values of the
unbounded operators on $D$ determine them uniquely wherever else they can be
defined?  This is particularly acute for observables because an operator which
is hermitian, when restricted to vectors in $D$, might have several different
self-adjoint extensions, and to specify a theory one would have to tell which
self-adjoint extension is meant.  Furthermore, equations valid on vectors of
$D$ need not be valid for other vectors.  For example, one can have two
operators, each essentially self-adjoint when restricted to vectors on a
dense domain $D$ and commuting on such

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=prose id=2-6-common-domain-continuation
vectors, but whose self-adjoint extensions do not commute (Ref.~23).

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=prose id=2-6-domain-continuation
Whether such behavior can happen in relativistic quantum field theory is not
known at the moment, and it is somewhat amazing how complete a theory one can
get without settling it.  The main point of the preceding remarks is to
bring these important problems to the reader's attention.  The rest of the
book is devoted to by-passing them.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=prose id=2-6-translation-representations
The remaining subject we will discuss is the theory of continuous unitary
representations of the translation group of space-time.  Here the main point
is to connect the statements of Chapter \ref{ch:relativistic-transformation-laws} about the energy momentum spectrum
of a physical theory to the criteria which will be used in Chapters 3 and 4.
More specifically, what we have in mind is this.  In Chapters 3 and 4 we will
have matrix elements of the form

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=display id=2-113
\begin{equation}
  \matrixel{\Phi}{U(a,\mathbf{1})}{\Psi}
  \tag{2-113}\label{eq:pct-2-113}
\end{equation}
for certain special vectors $\ket{\Phi}$ and $\ket{\Psi}$, and will argue
that

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=display id=2-114
\begin{equation}
  \int \dd^4a\,\ee^{+\ii p\cdot a}
  \matrixel{\Phi}{U(a,\mathbf{1})}{\Psi}=0
  \tag{2-114}\label{eq:pct-2-114}
\end{equation}
unless $p$ belongs to the physical spectrum which lies in $\overline{V}_{+}$.
The operation indicated in \eqref{eq:pct-2-114} is mathematically meaningful because
\eqref{eq:pct-2-113} will be shown to be an infinitely differentiable bounded function,
and so has a Fourier transform as a tempered distribution.  It makes sense
to assert that a tempered distribution vanishes in an open set.  On the other
hand, how do we know that this mathematical assertion is related to the
statement that the theory has an energy momentum spectrum in $\overline{V}_{+}$?
Formally, this is usually stated by writing an expansion over intermediate
states of physical momentum $Q$ and some other quantum numbers $\alpha$:

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=display id=2-6-intermediate-states
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\Phi}{\Psi}
  =\sum_{\alpha}\int\dd Q\,
  \braket{\Phi}{Q,\alpha}\braket{Q,\alpha}{\Psi}.
\end{equation*}
Then

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=display id=2-6-formal-fourier
\begin{align*}
  \int\dd^4a\,\ee^{+\ii p\cdot a}
  \matrixel{\Phi}{U(a,\mathbf{1})}{\Psi}
  &=\sum_{\alpha}\int\dd Q\int\dd^4a\,
  \ee^{+\ii(p-Q)\cdot a}
  \braket{\Phi}{Q,\alpha}\braket{Q,\alpha}{\Psi}\\
  &=(2\pi)^4\sum_{\alpha}\int\dd Q\,
  \delta(Q-p)\braket{\Phi}{Q,\alpha}\braket{Q,\alpha}{\Psi},
\end{align*}
because

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=103 print=91 kind=display id=2-6-translation-eigenvalue
\begin{equation*}
  \matrixel{Q,\alpha}{U(a,\mathbf{1})}{\Psi}
  =\ee^{-\ii Q\cdot a}\braket{Q,\alpha}{\Psi}.
\end{equation*}
These manipulations are not rigorous, because the states $\ket{Q,\alpha}$
may have infinite norm.  The method can be completely justified by using the
theory of direct integrals (Ref.~22), but we want to avoid that.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=prose id=2-6-snag-opening
Alternatively, we can look a little more closely at the form of
$U(a,\mathbf{1})$.  According to the SNAG theorem (Ref.~22),
$U(a,\mathbf{1})$ may be written as a Stieltjes integral over momentum space

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-snag-integral
\begin{equation*}
  U(a,\mathbf{1})=\int\ee^{-\ii p\cdot a}\,\dd E(p),
\end{equation*}
where $E$ is a projection valued measure on momentum space.  That means that
for each sphere of momentum space, and for each set $S$ which can be obtained
from spheres by a denumerable number of operations of taking unions,
intersections, and passing to complements, a projection operator $E(S)$ is
defined.  $E(S)$ has to satisfy

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-projection-measure
\begin{align*}
  E(S_1)E(S_2)&=E(S_1\cap S_2),\\
  \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}E(S_j)&=E\left(\bigcup_j S_j\right)
  \qquad\text{if }S_j\cap S_k\text{ is empty for }j\ne k,
\end{align*}
and

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-projection-identity
\begin{equation*}
  E(\mathbb{R}^4)=\mathbf{1}.
\end{equation*}

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=prose id=2-6-spectrum-measure
In terms of $E$, the statement that a set $S$ is not in the physical energy
momentum spectrum is simply $E(S)=0$ or, equivalently,

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-spectrum-equivalence
\begin{equation*}
  \int\dd^4a\,\rho(a)U(a,\mathbf{1})
  =\int\widetilde{\rho}(p)\,\dd E(p)=0
\end{equation*}
for all $\rho\in\mathcal{S}$ such that $\supp\widetilde{\rho}$ lies in $S$.
Here

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-fourier-inverse
\begin{equation*}
  \rho(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^4}\int\ee^{+\ii p\cdot x}
  \widetilde{\rho}(p)\,\dd p.
\end{equation*}
Evidently, this implies

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-spectrum-matrix-element
\begin{equation*}
  \int\dd^4a\,\rho(a)\matrixel{\Phi}{U(a,\mathbf{1})}{\Psi}=0,
\end{equation*}
which is another way of stating \eqref{eq:pct-2-114}.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=prose id=2-6-snag-qualification
These few words are evidently no substitute for a complete exposition of the
SNAG theorem, but they give the essential ideas which make possible a precise
statement of the physical hypothesis about the energy momentum spectrum.

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=prose id=2-6-discrete-spectrum
The $E(S)$ that can appear in a theory which is invariant under the
restricted Lorentz group as well as the translation group cannot be arbitrary.
In particular, there is only one $p$ at which a discrete point spectrum is
possible: $p=0$.  It is easy to see why this must be.  If
$P^\mu\ket{\Psi_p}=p^\mu\ket{\Psi_p}$ and
$\braket{\Psi_p}{\Psi_p}=1$ for some $p\ne0$, then
$U(0,\Lambda)\ket{\Psi_p}$ would satisfy

% PCT-SOURCE: pdf=104 print=92 kind=display id=2-6-lorentz-eigenvalue
\begin{equation*}
  P^\mu U(0,\Lambda)\ket{\Psi_p}
  =(\Lambda p)^\mu U(0,\Lambda)\ket{\Psi_p},
\end{equation*}
and, as $\Lambda$ runs over $L_+^\uparrow$, the
$U(0,\Lambda)\ket{\Psi_p}$ would be a continuous family of normalized states
orthogonal when $\Lambda_1p\ne\Lambda_2p$.  That is impossible in a separable
Hilbert space.  It is interesting that this makes it possible to characterize
the vacuum state in simpler ways: it is the only normalizable eigenfunction of
$P^0$, or $\mathbf{P}$, or $P^1$.
