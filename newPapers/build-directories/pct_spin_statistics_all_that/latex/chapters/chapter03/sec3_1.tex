\subsection{Axioms for the Notions of Field and Field Theory}
\label{sec:ch3-1-axioms}

It was recognized early in the analysis of field measurements for the
electromagnetic field in quantum electrodynamics that, in their dependence on
a space-time point, the components of fields are in general more singular
than ordinary functions. This suggests that only smeared fields be required
to yield well-defined operators. For example, in the case of the electric
field, $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},t)$ is not a well-defined operator, while
\begin{equation*}
  \int \dd^3\mathbf{x}\,\dd t\,f(\mathbf{x},t)\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},t)
  =\mathcal{E}(f)
\end{equation*}
is. Here $f$ is any infinitely differentiable function of compact support
defined in space-time. There is another point that should be mentioned. A
smeared field like $\mathcal{E}(f)$ can have an arbitrarily large expectation
value in a suitably chosen state; hence we expect to have to deal with
unbounded operators. It is known that in general unbounded operators cannot
be defined on every vector in any natural way. We shall therefore be obliged
to make some assumptions about the domain of vectors on which the smeared
fields are definable. Typical of the states on which fields cannot be defined
are those for which the expectation value is infinite. This is familiar from
elementary quantum mechanics where the position operator is not definable on
states $\Psi(\mathbf{x})$ which are normalizable,
\begin{equation*}
  \int \abs{\Psi(\mathbf{x})}^2\,\dd^3\mathbf{x}<\infty,
\end{equation*}
but which are such that
\begin{equation*}
  \int \abs{\mathbf{x}}^2\abs{\Psi(\mathbf{x})}^2\,\dd^3\mathbf{x}
\end{equation*}
does not converge.

The assumptions we make fall into four groups.

\subsubsection*{$O$. \emph{Assumptions of Relativistic Quantum Theory}}

The states of the theory are described by unit rays in a separable Hilbert
space $\Hilbert$. The relativistic transformation law of the states is given
by a continuous unitary representation of the inhomogeneous $SL(2,\C)$:
\begin{equation*}
  \{a,A\}\longmapsto U(a,A).
\end{equation*}

Since $U(a,\mathbf{1})$ is unitary it can be written as
$U(a,\mathbf{1})=\exp(-\ii P^\mu a_\mu)$, where $P^\mu$ is an unbounded
hermitian operator, interpreted as the energy momentum operator of the
theory. The operator $P^\mu P_\mu=-m^2$ is interpreted as the square of the
mass. The eigenvalues of $P^\mu$ lie in or on the plus cone (\emph{spectral
condition}).

There is an invariant state, $\ket{\Omega}$,
\begin{equation}
  U(a,A)\ket{\Omega}=\ket{\Omega}
  \tag{3-1}\label{eq:ch3-vacuum-invariant}
\end{equation}
unique up to a constant phase factor (uniqueness of the vacuum).

According to the discussion of Section \ref{sec:2-1} we could, on firm physical
grounds, assume much more about the multiplicity of states but that is
unnecessary in what follows.

Next we give the properties defining a field, whose transformation law under
$SL(2,\C)$ is given by the $n\times n$ matrix representation,
$S:A\mapsto S(A)$.

\subsubsection*{$I$. \emph{Assumptions about the Domain and Continuity of the Field}}

For each test function $f\in\mathcal{S}$, defined on space-time, there
exists a set $\varphi_1(f),\ldots,\varphi_n(f)$ of operators. These
operators, together with their adjoints $\varphi_1(f)^\dagger,\ldots,
\varphi_n(f)^\dagger$ are defined on a domain $D$ of vectors, dense
\begingroup
\renewcommand{\thefootnote}{$\dagger$}%
\footnote{Defined in Section \ref{sec:2-6}.}%
\endgroup
in $\Hilbert$. Furthermore, $D$ is a linear set containing $\ket{\Omega}$
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Omega}\in D
  \tag{3-2}\label{eq:ch3-domain-vacuum}
\end{equation}
and the $U(a,A)$ and the $\varphi_j(f)$ and $\varphi_j(f)^\dagger$ carry
vectors in $D$ into vectors in $D$
\begin{equation}
  U(a,A)D\subset D,\qquad
  \varphi_j(f)D\subset D,\qquad
  \varphi_j(f)^\dagger D\subset D,
  \tag{3-3}\label{eq:ch3-domain-invariance}
\end{equation}
where $j=1,2,\ldots,n$. If $\Phi,\Psi\in D$, then
$\bra{\Phi}\varphi_j(f)\ket{\Psi}$ is a tempered distribution, regarded as
a functional of $f$.

Two remarks concerning the domain $D$ are in order. First, $D$ always
contains the domain, $D_0$, consisting of those vectors which are obtained
from the vacuum state by applying polynomials in the smeared fields; that is
an immediate consequence of \eqref{eq:ch3-domain-vacuum} and \eqref{eq:ch3-domain-invariance}. Second, an unbounded operator,
defined and hermitian on a domain, may have several self-adjoint extensions
to vectors not in the domain, even if the domain is dense. This is a
well-known mathematical phenomenon first analyzed by von Neumann (see
Section \ref{sec:2-6} and \cite{pct-20}). It is only for a
self-adjoint operator that one can assert the usual spectral resolution,
completeness of the expansion into eigenfunctions, etc., which are essential
features for an operator which describes an observable in quantum mechanics.
Thus, for observable fields to be completely specified from a physical point
of view, the domain $D$ must be large enough so that such fields when defined
on $D$ have unique self-adjoint extensions. It is an attractive conjecture
that $D_0$ is already large enough for this purpose, but that is unproved at
the moment. Thus, to make a definition of the notion of field sufficiently
flexible to cope with whatever happens, we shall leave $D$ unspecified except
by the requirements listed under $I$. It is comforting to note that the
collision states and collision matrix of the theory are uniquely determined
by the fields as defined on $D_0$ \cite{pct-ch3-5,pct-ch3-15}.

Furthermore, it can be proved that the infinitesimal operators of the
Poincar\'e group, $P^\mu$ and $M^{\mu\nu}$, have unique self-adjoint
extensions when defined only on $D_0$. We shall see at the beginning of
Section \ref{sec:ch3-2-independence} that there is a domain $D_1$ larger than $D_0$ to which the fields
can always be extended. For all the practical purposes occurring in
Chapter \ref{ch:general-theorems-relativistic-qft} one can identify $D$ with $D_1$ or $D_0$.

\subsubsection*{$II$. \emph{Transformation Law of the Field}}

The equation
\begin{equation}
  U(a,A)\varphi_j(f)U(a,A)^{-1}
  =\sum_k S_{jk}(A^{-1})\varphi_k(\{a,A\}f)
  \tag{3-4}\label{eq:ch3-field-transform}
\end{equation}
is valid when each side is applied to any vector in $D$. Here
\begin{equation}
  \{a,A\}f(x)=f\bigl(\Lambda(A^{-1})(x-a)\bigr).
  \tag{3-5}\label{eq:ch3-test-transform}
\end{equation}

So far, the $\varphi_j$ are components of some representation of
$SL(2,\C)$ or the group $SL(2,\C)$ combined with reflections, depending on
the relativity group. Since, as has already been remarked in Chapter \ref{ch:relativistic-transformation-laws}, all
finite-dimensional representations are sums of irreducible representations,
it is natural to regard the components of an irreducible representation as
forming a field. Other irreducible representations may then be regarded as
different fields occurring in the same theory. However, it is sometimes
useful to group together components which transform according to a reducible
representation, and regard them as being components of the same field. This
is purely a matter of convenience. Our notation in the following will be
flexible. When we need to distinguish different sets of components we shall
usually use different Greek letters for them.

\paragraph{\emph{Examples:}}

\noindent\textbf{(a) Spin Zero Field}

This has one component and if we rewrite \eqref{eq:ch3-field-transform} in the usual unsmeared form,
we get
\begin{equation*}
  U(a,A)\varphi(x)U(a,A)^{-1}
  =\varphi\bigl(\Lambda(A)x+a\bigr).
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textbf{(b) Vector Field}

This has four components which we write $j_\mu$, $\mu=0,1,2,3$:
\begin{equation*}
  U(a,A)j_\mu(x)U(a,A)^{-1}
  =\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu(A^{-1})j_\nu\bigl(\Lambda(A)x+a\bigr).
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textbf{(c) Dirac Field $\psi$}

Here again there are four components $\psi_\alpha(x)$, $\alpha=1,2,3,4$,
but the $S(A)$ is the set of $4\times4$ matrices defined by means of
\eqref{eq:ch1-dirac-representation-explicit}.

\begin{equation*}
  U(a,A)\psi_\alpha(x)U(a,A)^{-1}
  =\sum_{\beta=1}^{4}S(A^{-1})_{\alpha\beta}
   \psi_\beta\bigl(\Lambda(A)x+a\bigr).
\end{equation*}

The next assumption is based on the idea that field measurements should
commute if they are carried out at points which are space-like separated.

\subsubsection*{$III$. \emph{Local Commutativity, Sometimes Called Microscopic
Causality}}

If the support of $f$ and the support of $g$ are space-like separated [i.e.,
if $f(x)g(y)=0$ for all pairs of points such that $(x-y)^2\leq0$], then one
or the other of
\begin{equation}
  [\varphi_j(f),\varphi_k(g)]_{\pm}
  \equiv \varphi_j(f)\varphi_k(g)\pm\varphi_k(g)\varphi_j(f)=0
  \tag{3-6}\label{eq:ch3-local}
\end{equation}
holds for all $j,k$ when the left-hand side is applied to any vector in $D$.
Similarly,
\begin{equation*}
  [\varphi_j(f),\varphi_k(g)^\dagger]_{\pm}=0.
\end{equation*}

Put in terms of unsmeared fields, this assumption is simply
\begin{equation*}
  [\varphi_j(x),\varphi_k(y)]_{\pm}=0
\end{equation*}
if $(x-y)^2>0$, and
\begin{equation*}
  [\varphi_j(x),\varphi_k^\dagger(y)]_{\pm}=0,
\end{equation*}
where $\varphi^\dagger(y)$ is the field that to the test function $g(y)$
gives the operator $[\varphi(\overline{g})]^\dagger$, i.e.,
$\varphi^\dagger(g)=\varphi(\overline{g})^\dagger$.

For observable fields, we would expect the minus sign to hold. When the +
sign holds we shall say that the operators $\varphi_j$, $\varphi_k$
anti-commute; in that case bilinear combinations of the fields will commute.
The famous theorem on the connection of spin with statistics, proved in
Chapter \ref{ch:general-theorems-relativistic-qft}, tells us which sign must be taken. (One alternative gives a
contradiction!) The fact that anti-commutation relations are of interest is
not indicated by any classical analogue; this was one of the discoveries of
the founders of our subject.

The preceding assumptions define what we mean by a \emph{field} in a
relativistic quantum theory; however they do not yet characterize a
\emph{field theory}. For example, in any relativistic quantum theory the
scalar field $\varphi(x)=c\mathbf{1}$, $c$ a constant, satisfies our
assumptions, so any relativistic quantum theory has at least a one-parameter
family of fields, albeit trivial ones. To be a field theory, a relativistic
quantum theory must have enough fields so its states can be uniquely
characterized using fields and functions of fields.

In old-fashioned field theory, this requirement was often met by assuming
that the fields provided an irreducible set of operators satisfying the
canonical commutation relations at a given time:
\begin{equation}
  [\varphi_j(\mathbf{x},t),\pi_k(\mathbf{y},t)]
  =\ii\,\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\delta_{jk}
  \tag{3-7}\label{eq:ch3-canonical}
\end{equation}

(Here $j$ runs over a suitable subset of the indices and the $\pi_j$ are
some combinations of the fields and their space-time derivatives.) However,
\eqref{eq:ch3-canonical} requires that the fields make sense as operators when smeared in
$\mathbf{x}$ only, and this is an additional strong assumption which goes
beyond our axioms. Furthermore there are hints from examples that, in
general, $[\varphi(\mathbf{x},t),
(\partial\varphi/\partial t')(\mathbf{y},t')]$ has singularities at
$t-t'=0$, even after being smeared in $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$. In this
case it is difficult to give \eqref{eq:ch3-canonical} a meaning. Thus, one is reluctant to
accept canonical commutation relations as an indispensable requirement on a
field theory.

Of all the assumptions usually made in canonical quantum field theory, one
of the least objectionable is the following: \emph{the smeared fields form an
irreducible set of operators in Hilbert space}. Precisely what this means is
this:
\begingroup
\renewcommand{\thefootnote}{$\dagger$}%
\footnote{This idea was introduced in \cite{pct-ch3-5}.}%
\endgroup
if $B$ is any bounded operator which satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Phi}B\varphi_j(f)\ket{\Psi}
  =\bra{\varphi_j(f)^\dagger\Phi}B\ket{\Psi}
  \tag{3-8}\label{eq:ch3-irreducibility}
\end{equation}
for all $\Phi,\Psi\in D$, all $j$ and all $f\in\mathcal{S}$, then $B$ is a
constant multiple of the identity operator. Notice that \eqref{eq:ch3-irreducibility} is a
formulation of the condition that $B$ commutes with $\varphi_j(f)$ which
avoids assuming that $\varphi_j(f)$ can be defined on vectors $B\Psi$, where
$\Psi\in D$. The irreducibility of fields means, roughly, that every operator
is a function of the field operators.

There is another notion of commutativity which is commonly used. (See
Riesz-Nagy, \cite{pct-25}, pp.~301--303.) It is the following: a
bounded operator $B$ commutes with a (not necessarily bounded) operator $T$ if
(1) $B$ carries the domain, $D(T)$, of $T$ into itself:
$BD(T)\subset D(T)$, and (2) for vectors $\Phi\in D(T)$,
$BT\Phi=TB\Phi$. A bounded operator which commutes with $\varphi_j(f)$ in
this sense surely commutes with $\varphi_j(f)$ in the sense of \eqref{eq:ch3-irreducibility}.

Interestingly enough, there is an apparently weaker assumption than
irreducibility which by virtue of $O$, $I$, $II$, and $III$ ensures
irreducibility, and we shall adopt it as our definition.

\paragraph{\emph{Definition:}}

A relativistic quantum theory satisfying axiom $O$ with a field $\varphi_j$,
$j=1,\ldots,n$ satisfying $I$, $II$, and $III$, is a \emph{field theory} if
the vacuum state is \emph{cyclic} for the smeared fields, that is, if
polynomials in the smeared field components
$P(\varphi_1(f),\varphi_2(g),\ldots)$, when applied to the vacuum state
$\ket{\Omega}$, yield a set $D_0$ of vectors dense in the Hilbert space of
states.

The proof that the cyclicity of the vacuum implies the irreducibility of the
field is given in Section \ref{sec:ch4-2-polynomial-algebra}.

We have said that the cyclicity of the vacuum is an apparently weaker
assumption than irreducibility. For a set of bounded operators, the cautious
word ``apparently'' can be dropped; any non-zero vector is a cyclic vector of
an irreducible set of bounded operators invariant under the adjoint operation.
\emph{(Proof:} Suppose $\ket{\Psi}\neq0$ is \emph{not} cyclic. The subspace
$\mathcal{H}_1$, spanned by the $\mathcal{P}\ket{\Psi}$, where the $\mathcal{P}$
are all polynomials in the bounded operators, is evidently invariant under
any of the operators and, by assumption, is not the whole Hilbert space. The
projection on $\mathcal{H}_1$ is a non-trivial operator which commutes with all
the given bounded operators and contradicts the irreducibility. Therefore
$\ket{\Psi}$ is cyclic.) In our case, in which the relation between $D$ and
$D_0$ is not explicitly known, it is not obvious that the irreducibility of
the fields as defined on $D$ implies the cyclicity of $\ket{\Omega}$. If
$D=D_0$ it is not difficult to show that it follows.

Up to this point our assumptions about field theory have made no contact with
collision theory. In accordance with the discussion in Section \ref{sec:1.4}, we should
like to assume

\subsubsection*{$IV$. \emph{Asymptotic Completeness}}
\begin{equation*}
  \Hilbert=\Hilbert^{\mathrm{in}}=\Hilbert^{\mathrm{out}},
\end{equation*}
but in order to do this, we must have some prescription for computing the
collision states of all elementary systems of a field theory given the fields.
There are several approaches to this problem, but the one which makes closest
contact with O, I, II, and III is due to Haag and Ruelle. Ruelle has shown
that O, I, II, and III imply the existence of collision states, that is,
incoming and outgoing states of one, two, or more particles, provided that the
one-particle states can be created by a polynomial in the smeared fields. Then
one can formulate axiom IV in terms of these collision states.

It should be mentioned that there need be no connection between the number of
fields necessary to describe a field theory and the number of elementary
systems it describes. One can have one field and many elementary systems or
many fields and only one elementary system. In this book we shall not explain
the Haag--Ruelle theory (for a systematic account the reader is referred to
the original papers and to Jost's book). We will use only axioms O, I, II, and
III, and the assumption that we are dealing with a field theory.
