\(
\varphi'=-\varphi,\qquad \psi'=\psi
\)
on the subspace spanned by the odd polynomials applied to the vacuum.  Then
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\varphi\psi\Omega}{\Omega}
  =\braket{\psi\Omega}{\varphi\Omega},
  \qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad
  -\braket{\varphi'\psi'\Omega}{\Omega}
  =\braket{\psi'\Omega}{\varphi'\Omega},
\end{equation*}
so $(\varphi')^\dagger=-\varphi'$; a Klein transformation may take a
hermitian field into a non-hermitian field.

Thus, in general, we will get
\begin{equation*}
  \bigl(\varphi_i'\bigr)^\dagger
  =\pm\bigl(\varphi_i^\dagger\bigr)',
\end{equation*}
and combining this with \eqref{eq:ch4-69-pct-abnormal} we arrive at a transformation law for the fields
$\varphi_i$ which differs from the usual one by a change of sign for some of
the fields.  The interpretation of $\Theta$ as the $\PCT$ operator can be
established just as for the normal case given at the end of the last section.
It remains to show that the particles of the theory obey the ``normal''
statistics. This can be done using the asymptotic condition proved in
\cite{ch4-6}.
A state containing several incoming particles can be obtained by taking the
strong limit, as $t\to-\infty$, of a state $\Psi_t$ labeled by a time
parameter.  The state $\Psi_t$ may be created from the vacuum by applying a
monomial in the Klein-transformed fields.  Since these fields obey normal
commutation relations, $\Psi_t$ satisfies normal statistics at finite times,
and therefore asymptotically.

\subsection{Haag's Theorem and Its Generalizations}
\label{sec:ch4-5-haag}

We have already remarked in Section \ref{sec:ch3-1-axioms} that in the traditional approach to
field theory one assumed that the operators of an irreducible set satisfied
the canonical commutation relations at a given time.  In this section we
follow tradition and pursue this idea further.

It can be proved for systems with a finite number of degrees of freedom that
under certain conditions any two solutions of the commutation relations are
connected by a canonical (unitary) transformation.  If this result were
taken over to field theory we could define the so-called \emph{interaction
picture}.  The canonical variables at each time are assumed to be equivalent
to the canonical variables of a free field $\varphi_{\mathrm{int}}$.  In
particular,
\begin{equation}
  V(t)\varphi(x,t)V(t)^{-1}=\varphi_{\mathrm{int}}(x,t).
  \tag{4-70}\label{eq:ch4-interaction-picture}
\end{equation}

The time dependence of the operator $V$ reflects the presence of
interactions, and various interesting quantities can be calculated in terms
of it.  For example, the collision operator $S$ is given by
\begin{equation*}
  S=\lim_{t\to\infty}V(t)V(-t)^\dagger.
\end{equation*}

It was soon realized that the argument leading to \eqref{eq:ch4-interaction-picture} is inconclusive
because there are many inequivalent representations of the canonical
commutation relations [that is, representations which cannot be connected by
a unitary transformation \eqref{eq:ch4-interaction-picture}].  However, the
argument is not only inconclusive, it is \emph{wrong}: unless $\varphi(x,t)$
is a free field there is no $V$ satisfying \eqref{eq:ch4-interaction-picture}, as will now be shown by
arguments which realize ideas of R. Haag.

Evidently, in order for this theorem to make sense we have to assume fields
which are distributions in their space arguments for each value of their time
arguments.

Our first step is to show that the unitary equivalence of two irreducible
sets of operators implies the equality of their equal time vacuum expectation
values.

\begin{theorem}[4-14]\label{thm:ch4-14-unitary-equivalence}
Let $\varphi_{1\alpha}(f,t)$ and $\varphi_{2\beta}(f,t)$,
$f\in\Schwartz(\mathbb R^3)$ be any two irreducible sets of field operators at
time $t$ defined respectively in Hilbert spaces $\Hilbert_1$ and
$\Hilbert_2$, in which there are continuous unitary representations of the
inhomogeneous $SU_2$ group,
\begin{equation*}
  \{a,A\}\longrightarrow U_j(a,A)\qquad j=1,2,
\end{equation*}
respectively, such that
\begin{equation}
  U_j(a,A)\varphi_{j\alpha}(f,t)U_j(a,A)^{-1}
  =\sum_\beta S_{\alpha\beta}(A^{-1})
  \varphi_{j\beta}(\{a,A\}f,t).
  \tag{4-71}\label{eq:ch4-field-transformation}
\end{equation}
where $A\longrightarrow S(A)$ is a matrix representation of the unitary
unimodular group $SU_2$.  Suppose the representations $U_j$ possess unique
invariant states, $\Psi_{j0}$.

\begin{equation}
  U_j(a,A)\ket{\Psi_{j0}}=\ket{\Psi_{j0}}\qquad j=1,2.
  \tag{4-72}\label{eq:ch4-invariant-vacuum}
\end{equation}

Suppose finally that there exists a unitary operator $V$ such that at time
$t$
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_{2\alpha}(f,t)
  =V\varphi_{1\alpha}(f,t)V^{-1}.
  \tag{4-73}\label{eq:ch4-unitary-field-equivalence}
\end{equation}

Then
\begin{equation}
  U_2(a,A)=VU_1(a,A)V^{-1}
  \tag{4-74}\label{eq:ch4-representation-equivalence}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  c\ket{\Psi_{20}}=V\ket{\Psi_{10}},
  \tag{4-75}\label{eq:ch4-vacuum-phase}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is a complex number of modulus one.
\end{theorem}

The proof is a slightly modified version of that of Theorem \ref{thm:ch3-symmetry-uniqueness}.  One
considers the operator $U_2(a,A)^{-1}VU_1(a,A)V^{-1}$ and shows that it is a
constant multiple of the identity in $\Hilbert_2$.  We leave the details to
the reader.  The relations \eqref{eq:ch4-unitary-field-equivalence} and \eqref{eq:ch4-vacuum-phase} immediately imply the following
corollary.

\begin{corollary*}

In any two theories satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-14-unitary-equivalence} the equal time
vacuum expectation values are the same:
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Psi_{10}}\varphi_{1\alpha}(x_1,t)\cdots
  \varphi_{1\beta}(x_n,t)\ket{\Psi_{10}}
  =
  \bra{\Psi_{20}}\varphi_{2\alpha}(x_1,t)\cdots
  \varphi_{2\beta}(x_n,t)\ket{\Psi_{20}}.
  \tag{4-76}\label{eq:ch4-equal-time-vacuum}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary*}

We are now in a position to prove Haag's theorem, which says that if one of
the two fields occurring in Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-14-unitary-equivalence} is a free field so is the other.
This will follow easily from a more general result of R. Jost and B. Schroer,
so we prove the latter.  For simplicity we consider the case of a neutral
scalar field, though the result is true more generally.

\begin{theorem}[4-15]\label{thm:ch4-15-jost-schroer}
If $\varphi(x)$ is a hermitian scalar local field for which the vacuum is
cyclic, and if
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\ket{\Omega}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x-y;m)
  \tag{4-77}\label{eq:ch4-jost-schroer-two-point}
\end{equation}
with $m>0$ then $\varphi(x)$ is a free field of mass $m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If we define $j(x)=(-\Box_x+m^2)\varphi(x)$ we see from \eqref{eq:ch4-jost-schroer-two-point} [using
$(-\Box_x+m^2)\Delta^+(x-y;m)=0$] that
$\bra{\Omega}j(x)j(y)\ket{\Omega}$ vanishes.  Hence
$\norm{j(f)\ket{\Omega}}=0$, which leads, by Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-3-separating}, to $j(x)=0$, i.e.,
$(\Box-m^2)\times\varphi(x)=0$.  Thus the field $\varphi$ satisfies the
equation of a free field.

We must show that it is a free field in the sense of Section \ref{sec:ch3-2-independence}.  The main
part of the proof consists in showing that the commutator is a multiple of the
identity operator.

In momentum space we have $(p^2+m^2)\widetilde{\varphi}(p)=0$, so the spectrum
of $\widetilde{\varphi}$ is contained in the hyperboloid $p^2=-m^2$.  We can
therefore decompose $\varphi$ into a positive and a negative frequency part
in a Lorentz invariant way
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi(x)=\varphi_+(x)+\varphi_-(x).
\end{equation*}

For example, $\varphi_+(h)$ is defined for a test function $h$ to be
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi_+(h)=\widetilde{\varphi}
  \bigl(\widetilde{\widetilde{h}}\bigr).
\end{equation*}
where $\widetilde{\widetilde h}$ is an infinitely differentiable function 0
if $p^2=-m^2$, $p^0<0$ and one if $p^2<0$, $p^0>0$.  Since negative energies
(positive frequencies!) do not exist
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_+(f)\ket{\Omega}=0.
  \tag{4-78}\label{eq:ch4-positive-part-annihilates-vacuum}
\end{equation}

Next consider the state
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_+(x)\varphi_-(y)\ket{\Omega}.
  \tag{4-79}\label{eq:ch4-positive-negative-state}
\end{equation}

Any momentum occurring in this state is the sum of a forward and a backward
time-like vector, each of mass $m$; so this momentum is space-like or zero.
Since there are no states with space-like momentum, \eqref{eq:ch4-positive-negative-state} must be a multiple
of $\ket{\Omega}$.  But from \eqref{eq:ch4-positive-part-annihilates-vacuum},
\begin{equation*}
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi_+(x)\varphi_-(y)\ket{\Omega}
  =
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\ket{\Omega}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x-y).
\end{equation*}

Thus
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_+(x)\varphi_-(y)\ket{\Omega}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x-y)\ket{\Omega},
  \tag{4-80}\label{eq:ch4-positive-negative-action}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x-y)\ket{\Omega}
  =[\varphi_+(x),\varphi_-(y)]\ket{\Omega}.
  \tag{4-81}\label{eq:ch4-positive-negative-commutator}
\end{equation}

Combining \eqref{eq:ch4-positive-negative-commutator} with the trivial equation
\begin{equation*}
  [\varphi_+(x),\varphi_+(y)]\ket{\Omega}=0
\end{equation*}
we get
\begin{equation}
  [\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]\ket{\Omega}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta(x-y)\ket{\Omega}
  +[\varphi_-(x),\varphi_-(y)]\ket{\Omega},
  \tag{4-82}\label{eq:ch4-commutator-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta=\Delta^+ +\overline{\Delta^+}.
\end{equation*}

Let $\ket{\Psi}$ be any state in the domain of definition of $\varphi$, and
define
\begin{equation*}
  F(x,y)=\bra{\Psi}[\varphi_-(x),\varphi_-(y)]\ket{\Omega}.
\end{equation*}

The argument given in the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-2-reeh-schlieder} shows that $F(x,y)$ is a
tempered distribution.  The Fourier transform is $0$\footnote{%
In this argument, and in several other places in the rest of the proof, we
work with the unsmeared fields.  This is purely a matter of convenience.  The
reader can easily supply the required smearing.  For example, to deal with
\eqref{eq:ch4-positive-negative-state} one considers the tempered distribution $T$ on $\mathbb R^8$ defined
by
\[
  \bra{\Psi}\varphi_+(f)\varphi_-(g)\ket{\Omega}=T(f,g)
\]
and argues that its Fourier transform $\widetilde T(p_1,p_2)$ is zero unless
$p_1^2=-m^2$, $p_1^0>0$, $p_2^2=-m^2$, $p_2^0<0$.  Thus, in the variables
$p_1+p_2$, its support consists of vectors which are space-like or zero.  The
conclusion then follows as above.}%
unless the variables conjugate to $x$ and $y$ lie in the backward light cone.
Therefore, by Theorems \ref{thm:ch2-laplace-holomorphy} and \ref{thm:ch2-laplace-halfspace}, $F(x,y)$ possess an analytic continuation
$F$ into the tube $\operatorname{Im}x,\operatorname{Im}y\in V_+$.  We can
easily see that $F(x,y)$ must vanish if $x$ and $y$ are real, and
$(x-y)^2>0$.  This is obtained from \eqref{eq:ch4-commutator-decomposition} by noting that if
$(x-y)^2>0$, $(1/\ii)\Delta(x-y)=0$, and
$[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]=0$.  Thus by analytic continuation (Theorem \ref{thm:edge-of-wedge-zero-boundary})
$F(x,y)=0$ at points of holomorphy and therefore the boundary value $F$
vanishes.  Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  [\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]\ket{\Omega}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta(x-y)\ket{\Omega}.
  \tag{4-83}\label{eq:ch4-commutator-vacuum-final}
\end{equation}

From \eqref{eq:ch4-commutator-vacuum-final} it follows that the operator
$[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]-(1/\ii)\Delta(x-y)$, suitably smeared,
annihilates the vacuum, and so vanishes, since, again by Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-3-separating}, no
annihilation operator can be constructed out of the field operators in a
finite region.

In the usual field theory, it is customary to define a free scalar field as
one satisfying \eqref{eq:ch4-positive-part-annihilates-vacuum} and
\begin{equation}
  (\Box-m^2)\varphi(x)=0,\qquad
  [\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]=\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta(x-y),
  \tag{4-84}\label{eq:ch4-free-field-equations}
\end{equation}
so that in that sense the theorem is already proved.  However, it is also
easy to verify that the equations \eqref{eq:ch4-free-field-equations} imply our previous definition of the
free field.  It suffices to show that the vacuum expectation values are those
given by \eqref{eq:pct-3-41}.  From \eqref{eq:ch4-free-field-equations} we have immediately
\begin{equation*}
  [\varphi_+(x_1),\varphi(x_j)]
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x_1-x_j)
\end{equation*}
and therefore
\begin{align*}
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi(x_1)\cdots\varphi(x_n)\ket{\Omega}
  &=
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi_+(x_1)\varphi(x_2)\cdots
  \varphi(x_n)\ket{\Omega} \\
  &=
  \bra{\Omega}[\varphi_+(x_1),\varphi(x_2)]
  \varphi(x_3)\cdots\varphi(x_n)\ket{\Omega} \\
  &\quad+
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi(x_2)[\varphi_+(x_1),\varphi(x_3)]
  \varphi(x_4)\cdots\varphi(x_n)\ket{\Omega} \\
  &\quad+\cdots+
  \bra{\Omega}\varphi(x_2)\varphi(x_3)\cdots\varphi(x_{n-1})
  [\varphi_+(x_1),\varphi(x_n)]\ket{\Omega},
\end{align*}
where we have made use of $\varphi_+(x_1)\ket{\Omega}=0$.

Hence
\begin{equation*}
  \Wightman_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
  =\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x_1-x_j)
  \Wightman_{n-2}(x_2,\ldots,\widehat{x}_j,\ldots,x_n),
\end{equation*}
where $\widehat{x}_j$ means omit $x_j$.  Starting from the two point
function we arrive at the formula \eqref{eq:pct-3-41} for the vacuum expectation values of
the free field.  Appealing to the reconstruction theorem we see that
$\varphi(x)$ is the free field defined in Section \ref{sec:ch3-2-independence}.
\end{proof}

From Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-15-jost-schroer} we get

\begin{theorem}[4-16 \emph{(Haag's Theorem)}]\label{thm:ch4-16-haag}
Suppose that $\varphi_1(x)$ is a free hermitian scalar field of mass $m>0$,
and $\varphi_2(x)$ is a local field covariant under the inhomogeneous
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$.  Suppose further that the fields
$\varphi_1(x)$, $\dot{\varphi}_1(x)$, $\varphi_2(x)$,
$\dot{\varphi}_2(x)$, satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-14-unitary-equivalence}.  Then
$\varphi_2(x)$ is a free field of mass $m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the corollary to Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-14-unitary-equivalence} we deduce
\begin{equation}
  \bra{\Psi_{20}}\varphi_2(x,t)\varphi_2(y,t)\ket{\Psi_{20}}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x-y,0;m).
  \tag{4-85}\label{eq:ch4-haag-two-point-equal-time}
\end{equation}

Any two vectors $(x,t_1)$ and $(y,t_2)$ can be brought into the equal time
plane $t_1=t_2$ by a restricted Lorentz transformation if their difference is
space-like.  Thus combining the given covariance of $\varphi_2$ with \eqref{eq:ch4-haag-two-point-equal-time}
we get by the standard analytic continuation argument
\begin{equation*}
  \bra{\Psi_{20}}\varphi_2(x,t_1)\varphi_2(y,t_2)\ket{\Psi_{20}}
  =\frac{1}{\ii}\Delta^+(x-y;m).
\end{equation*}

The theorem is then an immediate consequence of Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-15-jost-schroer}.
\end{proof}

Haag's theorem is very inconvenient; it means that the interaction picture
exists only if there is no interaction.

Using the same techniques we can prove a more general result relating any two
fields which are unitary equivalent at a given time.  It is known as the
\emph{generalized Haag's theorem}.

\begin{theorem}[4-17]\label{thm:ch4-17-generalized-haag}
Let there be given two theories as described in the hypotheses of Theorem
\ref{thm:ch4-14-unitary-equivalence}, and suppose, in addition, that the theories are invariant under the
inhomogeneous $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$, and that some of the operators
$\varphi_{j\alpha}$ transform under $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$ according to
\begin{equation}
  U_j(a,A)\varphi_{j\alpha}(x)U_j(a,A)^{-1}
  =\sum_\beta S_{\alpha\beta}(A^{-1})
   \varphi_{j\beta}\bigl(\Lambda(A)x+a\bigr).
  \tag{4-86}\label{eq:ch4-generalized-haag-covariance}
\end{equation}

Then the expectation values involving four or fewer of those fields coincide
in the two theories.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Remark:}

The statement of Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-17-generalized-haag} allows us to apply it to the usual case, where
the basic fields are covariant under the inhomogeneous $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb
C)$, but their non-covariant conjugate momenta are necessary to form an
irreducible set if we restrict ourselves to a given time.

\begin{proof}
Let $\Wightman^{(n)}_{j\alpha\ldots\beta}
(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_{n-1})$, $j=1,2$ be the holomorphic functions whose boundary
values are the left- (respectively right-) hand side of \eqref{eq:ch4-equal-time-vacuum}, where the
$\varphi$'s have been chosen from among the fields satisfying \eqref{eq:ch4-generalized-haag-covariance}.

The points at which \eqref{eq:ch4-equal-time-vacuum} holds are totally space-like and among them are
Jost points.\footnote{%
See Theorem \ref{thm:ch2-12-jost}.}%
To prove the theorem it suffices to show that these Jost points, together with
those which are obtainable from them by real Lorentz transformations, form a
real environment for the holomorphic functions
$\Wightman^{(n)}_{j\alpha\ldots\beta}$ with $n=2,3,4$.  (For $n=1$ the
whole affair is trivial.)  For $n=2$ the argument was given after \eqref{eq:ch4-haag-two-point-equal-time} in
the proof of the previous theorem: any space-like vector $\xi$ can be brought
into the form $\xi^0=0$ by a real restricted Lorentz transformation.  For
$n=3$, any two real space-like vectors $\xi_1,\xi_2$ can be brought into the
equal time plane $\xi_1^0=\xi_2^0=0$ by a real restricted Lorentz
transformation provided the two-plane they span consists of space-like
vectors only (if they are collinear we apply the argument given for $n=2$).
The criterion is simply
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\xi_1\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_2}
  <\sqrt{\xi_1^2\xi_2^2},\qquad
  \xi_1^2>0,\qquad \xi_2^2>0,
  \tag{4-87}\label{eq:ch4-jost-criterion-two}
\end{equation}
which is surely satisfied by an open set of real Jost points.  Finally, for
$n=4$ any three real space-like vectors $\xi_1,\xi_2,\xi_3$ can be brought
into the equal time plane $\xi_1^0=\xi_2^0=\xi_3^0=0$ by a real restricted
Lorentz transformation provided the three-plane they span consists entirely
of space-like vectors.  Here the criterion is that the matrix
\begin{equation}
  \left\{
  \begin{matrix}
    \xi_1^2 & \xi_1\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_2 & \xi_1\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_3\\
    \xi_2\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_1 & \xi_2^2 & \xi_2\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_3\\
    \xi_3\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_1 & \xi_3\mathbin{\cdot}\xi_2 & \xi_3^2
  \end{matrix}
  \right\}
  \tag{4-88}\label{eq:ch4-jost-criterion-three}
\end{equation}
be positive definite, a condition which is again satisfied by an open set of
Jost points.
\end{proof}

In Theorem \ref{thm:ch4-17-generalized-haag} we cannot prove the equality of the vacuum expectation values
of the two theories for five or more operators by the same method, since four
or more vectors cannot be transformed to the equal time plane unless they lie
on a special subset, and this is too small to provide a unique analytic
continuation for functions defined on it.  However the theorem is powerful
enough: if two theories are to give different vacuum expectation values for
products of 2, 3, or 4 field operators, then inequivalent representations of
the canonical commutation relations have to be used.

We believe that the theorems of this section show that the relation between a
free field and one with interaction cannot be as simple as the analogy with
systems of a finite number of degrees of freedom might suggest.  In particular,
the kinematics gets mixed up with the dynamics in the sense that the dynamics
determine which representation of the canonical commutation relations we must
use.  What is even more likely in physically interesting quantum field theories
is that equal time commutation relations will make no sense at all; the field
might not be an operator unless smeared in time as well as space.

In mentioning these awkward theorems we did not intend to suggest that we think
that no sense can be made of equations of motion such as
\begin{equation}
  (-\Box+m^2)\varphi(x)=\lambda\varphi(x)^3
  \tag{4-89}\label{eq:ch4-equation-of-motion-example}
\end{equation}
and that their study is not worthwhile.  The results of this section merely make
clear certain facts of life which must be taken into account in interpreting
them.  In a certain sense all the theorems of this chapter can be regarded as a
girding of the loins preparatory to a study of things like \eqref{eq:ch4-equation-of-motion-example}.  To show the
equation has solutions we must find a way of defining $\varphi(x)^3$, and to do
this we must have some idea of what a quantized field $\varphi(x)$ looks like.
