\subsection{The Theta Angle}
\label{sec:23.6}

We have seen that there are configurations of arbitrary integer winding
number, but how do we know that these configurations must be included in
the path integral? To keep an open mind, suppose we add up configurations
with arbitrary weight factors $f(\nu)$ for each winding number, leaving
open the possibility that some or most of these weight factors might
vanish. The expectation value of a local observable $\mathcal O$ located
within a large Euclidean spacetime volume $\Omega$ is then
\begin{equation}
\langle\mathcal O\rangle_\Omega=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_\nu f(\nu)\int_\nu[d\phi]\,
\exp\bigl(I_\Omega[\phi]\bigr)\mathcal O[\phi]}
{\displaystyle\sum_\nu f(\nu)\int_\nu[d\phi]\,
\exp\bigl(I_\Omega[\phi]\bigr)}.
\tag{23.6.1}\label{eq:23.6.1}
\end{equation}
Here $\phi$ stands for all the fields of the theory; the subscript $\nu$
on the integrals over $\phi$ indicates that we are to include only
configurations of winding number $\nu$; and $I_\Omega[\phi]$ is the
integral of the Lagrangian density over the spacetime volume $\Omega$.
Now suppose that $\Omega$ is divided into two very large volumes
$\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$, with $\mathcal O$ in the volume $\Omega_1$.
The integral over all fields with winding number $\nu$ may be written as
an integral over all fields with winding number $\nu_1$ in volume
$\Omega_1$ and winding number $\nu_2$ in volume $\Omega_2$, with $\nu_1$
and $\nu_2$ summed over all values with $\nu_1+\nu_2=\nu$, and so to a
good approximation Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.6.1} becomes
\begin{equation}
\langle\mathcal O\rangle_\Omega=
\frac{\displaystyle
\sum_{\nu_1,\nu_2}f(\nu_1+\nu_2)
\int_{\nu_1}[d\phi]\,\exp\bigl(I_{\Omega_1}[\phi]\bigr)\mathcal O[\phi]
\int_{\nu_2}[d\phi]\,\exp\bigl(I_{\Omega_2}[\phi]\bigr)}
{\displaystyle
\sum_{\nu_1,\nu_2}f(\nu_1+\nu_2)
\int_{\nu_1}[d\phi]\,\exp\bigl(I_{\Omega_1}[\phi]\bigr)
\int_{\nu_2}[d\phi]\,\exp\bigl(I_{\Omega_2}[\phi]\bigr)}.
\tag{23.6.2}\label{eq:23.6.2}
\end{equation}
But then for general weight factors the average is not the same as if we
omitted the volume $\Omega_2$, in contradiction with our general ideas
about cluster decomposition. (See Chapter 4.) In order for the factors
involving the volume $\Omega_2$ to cancel in this ratio, we must have
\[
f(\nu_1+\nu_2)=f(\nu_1)f(\nu_2).
\]
This will be the case if and only if $f(\nu)$ is of the form
\begin{equation}
f(\nu)=\exp(i\theta\nu),
\tag{23.6.3}\label{eq:23.6.3}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is a free parameter. Thus in particular we cannot
arbitrarily discard all configurations with non-zero winding number,
because then an instanton of winding number $\nu$ in one region would
have to be balanced with an instanton of winding number $-\nu$ in some
other region, making it impossible to calculate expectation values
without considering what is happening far from the location of the
operators being measured.

The factor $f(\nu)$ may be put in a more familiar form. According to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.5.18}, with gauge fields normalized so that in the
standard $SU(2)$ subgroup the structure constants are
$\epsilon_{ijk}$, the winding number may be written as an integral:
\begin{equation}
\nu=\frac{1}{64\pi^2}\int(d^4x)_E\,
\epsilon^E_{ijkl}F_{\alpha ij}F_{\alpha kl}.
\tag{23.6.4}\label{eq:23.6.4}
\end{equation}
This can be expressed in terms of a Minkowskian path integral; since
$(d^4x)_E=i\,d^4x$; $F_{\alpha34}=-iF_{\alpha30}$; and
$\epsilon^{1230}=-1$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.6.4} may be written
\begin{equation}
\nu=-\frac{1}{64\pi^2}\int d^4x\,
\epsilon^{\kappa\lambda\rho\sigma}
F_{\alpha\kappa\lambda}F_{\alpha\rho\sigma}.
\tag{23.6.5}\label{eq:23.6.5}
\end{equation}
Thus inclusion of the weighting factor \eqref{eq:23.6.3} is therefore
equivalent to adding a term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_\theta=-\frac{\theta}{64\pi^2}
\epsilon^{\kappa\lambda\rho\sigma}
F_{\alpha\kappa\lambda}F_{\alpha\rho\sigma}
\tag{23.6.6}\label{eq:23.6.6}
\end{equation}
to the Lagrangian density. But as mentioned at the beginning of
\hyperref[sec:15.2]{Section 15.2}, we might have included such a term in
the Lagrangian of any non-Abelian gauge theory anyway, with arbitrary
real $\theta$.

The inclusion of a term \eqref{eq:23.6.6} in the Lagrangian density would
violate $P$ and $CP$ conservation. We could of course simply set
$\theta=0$, but this would invalidate what in
\hyperref[sec:18.7]{Section 18.7} was scored as one of the successes of
quantum chromodynamics: it made it automatic for $P$ and $CP$ to be
conserved by the strong interactions even though they are evidently
violated by the weak interactions.

To assess the physical consequences of including the term
\eqref{eq:23.6.6} in the Lagrangian, consider the effect of redefinition
of all the fermion fields
\begin{equation}
\psi_f\longrightarrow\exp(i\gamma_5\alpha_f)\psi_f,
\tag{23.6.7}\label{eq:23.6.7}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is a flavor index, and $\alpha_f$ are a set of real phases.
According to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:22.2.10} and \eqref{eq:22.2.24}, the effect
on the measure for path integrals over fermion fields is
\begin{align}
[d\psi][d\bar\psi]\longrightarrow{}&
\exp\left\{-\frac{i}{32\pi^2}\int d^4x\,
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_\alpha^{\mu\nu}F_\alpha^{\rho\sigma}
\sum_f\alpha_f\right\}
[d\psi][d\bar\psi].
\tag{23.6.8}\label{eq:23.6.8}
\end{align}
(We are using generators normalized so that
$\operatorname{Tr}(t_\alpha t_\beta)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}/2$.)
Comparing this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.6.6} shows that this is equivalent
to shifting $\theta$ by
\begin{equation}
\theta\longrightarrow\theta+2\sum_f\alpha_f.
\tag{23.6.9}\label{eq:23.6.9}
\end{equation}
The redefinition of the fermion fields will also change the mass terms in
the Lagrangian density. In order to take account of masses involving
$\gamma_5$, let us write the fermion mass term in the Lagrangian as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_m=-\frac12\sum_f\mathcal M_f\bar\psi_f(1+\gamma_5)\psi_f
-\frac12\sum_f\mathcal M_f^*\bar\psi_f(1-\gamma_5)\psi_f,
\tag{23.6.10}\label{eq:23.6.10}
\end{equation}
with mass parameters $\mathcal M_f$ which, if complex, would violate
$P$ and $CP$ conservation. Then the redefinition \eqref{eq:23.6.7}
changes these parameters by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M_f\longrightarrow\exp(2i\alpha_f)\mathcal M_f.
\tag{23.6.11}\label{eq:23.6.11}
\end{equation}
A mere change of path integration variables cannot have any physical
effect, so observable quantities cannot depend separately on $\theta$ or
the phases of the mass parameters $\mathcal M_f$, but only on the
combination
\begin{equation}
\exp(-i\theta)\prod_f\mathcal M_f.
\tag{23.6.12}\label{eq:23.6.12}
\end{equation}
In particular, we can always define the fermion fields so that $\theta=0$,
but at the price of perhaps introducing $P$ and $CP$ violating phases in
the mass parameters.

This discussion shows that if any of the quark masses were to vanish,
then the theta angle would have no effect and there would be no $P$ or
$CP$ non-conservation in quantum chromodynamics. The analysis of quark
mass ratios in \hyperref[sec:19.7]{Section 19.7} indicates that the quarks
all have non-zero masses, though it is sometimes suggested that the
inclusion of terms of second order in $m_s$ in this analysis might allow
$m_u$ to vanish.~\hyperref[ch23-ref-28]{[28]} But there is no question
that the $u$ and $d$ quarks are quite light, which leads to some
suppression of the effects of a non-zero theta angle. We saw in
\hyperref[sec:19.4]{Section 19.4} that $m_u$ and $m_d$ are roughly of
order $m_\pi^2/m_N$ (where $m_N$ is used here as a typical quantum
chromodynamic mass scale), so we might expect the effects of the theta
angle to be suppressed by four factors of $m_\pi$, but this is not quite
correct. With $P$ and $CP$ not conserved there would be a non-zero
amplitude for the $\pi^0$ to disappear into the vacuum, proportional to
$m_\pi^4$, but the `tadpole' graphs with pion lines ending in such vacuum
transition vertices would be enhanced by a factor $m_\pi^{-2}$ from the
pion propagator, giving a net effect proportional to $m_\pi^2$, not
$m_\pi^4$. In particular, if we define fermion fields so that all
$\mathcal M_f$ are real, then a non-zero theta angle would produce a
$P$- and $T$-non-conserving neutron electric dipole moment proportional
to $|\theta|$ and $m_\pi^2$, and hence on dimensional grounds of
order~\hyperref[ch23-ref-29]{[29]}
\begin{equation}
d_n\approx|\theta|e m_\pi^2/m_N^3
\approx10^{-16}|\theta|\,e\ \mathrm{cm}.
\tag{23.6.13}\label{eq:23.6.13}
\end{equation}
The neutron electric dipole moment is known to be less than about
$10^{-25}\,e\ \mathrm{cm}$, so $|\theta|<10^{-9}$.

In order to explain in a natural way why $\theta$ is so small, Peccei and
Quinn~\hyperref[ch23-ref-30]{[30]} proposed a theory in which $\theta$
became in effect a dynamical variable, which could relax to a minimum of
the effective potential, at which $P$ and $CP$ would be conserved. Their
idea was taken up by Wilczek and myself,~\hyperref[ch23-ref-31]{[31]} who
noted that it would require the existence of a light spinless particle,
the axion. The axion that appeared in the original Peccei--Quinn model
has been ruled out by experiment, but there are more general
possibilities,~\hyperref[ch23-ref-32]{[32]} in which the axion couples too
weakly to ordinary matter to have been observed.

The common feature of all versions of the axion theory is that there is
some $U(1)$ symmetry that is spontaneously broken at energies much
higher than those associated with quantum chromodynamics, and is also
broken by an anomaly involving the gluon fields. According to the
general formalism of Chapters 19 and 22, the low energy effective field
theory will contain a Goldstone boson field $\phi$, so that, under the
symmetry transformation
\begin{equation}
\phi\longrightarrow\phi+F_\phi\epsilon,
\tag{23.6.14}\label{eq:23.6.14}
\end{equation}
the effective Lagrangian undergoes the transformation
\begin{equation}
\delta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{eff}}=
-\frac{\epsilon A}{64\pi^2}
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_\alpha^{\mu\nu}F_\alpha^{\rho\sigma},
\tag{23.6.15}\label{eq:23.6.15}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is a dimensionless constant of order unity, characterizing the
anomaly, and $F_\phi$ is a constant of the order of the energy scale at
which the symmetry is spontaneously broken (with $\phi$ defined to be
canonically normalized). Then the terms in the effective Lagrangian
involving $\phi$ are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_\phi=-\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi
-\frac{1}{64\pi^2}\frac{\phi}{M}
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_\alpha^{\mu\nu}F_\alpha^{\rho\sigma}
+\cdots,
\tag{23.6.16}\label{eq:23.6.16}
\end{equation}
where $M=F_\phi/A$ and ``$\cdots$'' denotes possible interactions
involving derivatives of $\phi$. Comparing Eqs.~\eqref{eq:23.6.16} and
\eqref{eq:23.6.6}, we see that, for a constant $\phi$, all observables
will be functions not of $\theta$ and $\phi$ separately, but only of
$\theta+\phi/M$. (This is with fermion fields defined to make the mass
parameters $\mathcal M_f$ all real; otherwise observables will depend on
$\theta-\sum_f\operatorname{Arg}\mathcal M_f+\phi/M$.) If everything in
the theory but the theta term \eqref{eq:23.6.6} and the $\phi$ interaction
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.6.16} conserves $P$ or $CP$, then the effective
potential will be even in $\theta+\phi/M$, so it will have a stationary
point at $\theta+\phi/M=0$, preserving the conservation of both $P$ and
$CP$. In the real world $P$ and $CP$ are not exact, but the only observed
violations are in the weak interactions, and would shift the expectation
value of $\phi$ only a small way~\hyperref[ch23-ref-33]{[33]} from
$-M\theta$.

Even without specifying the underlying theory, by using effective field
theory techniques it is possible to say a fair amount about the general
properties of the axion. The most general Lagrangian for the axion field
$\phi$, now including the theta term and all interactions with $u$ and
$d$ quarks up to order $1/M$, is of the form
\begin{align}
\mathcal L_\phi={}&-\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi
+\frac{1}{64\pi^2}\left[\frac{\phi}{M}+\theta\right]
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_\alpha^{\mu\nu}F_\alpha^{\rho\sigma}
\notag\\
&-\frac{i f_u}{M}\partial_\mu\phi\,
\bar u\gamma_5\gamma^\mu u
-\frac{i f_d}{M}\partial_\mu\phi\,
\bar d\gamma_5\gamma^\mu d,
\tag{23.6.17}\label{eq:23.6.17}
\end{align}
where $f_u$ and $f_d$ are dimensionless coupling constants, expected to
be of order unity. As shown by Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.6.8}, the redefinition
\eqref{eq:23.6.7} of the quark fields has the effect of subjecting the
quantity $\phi(x)/M+\theta$ in the second term of the Lagrangian
\eqref{eq:23.6.17} to the replacement
\begin{equation}
\frac{\phi(x)}{M}+\theta\longrightarrow
\frac{\phi(x)}{M}+\theta+2[\alpha_u(x)+\alpha_d(x)].
\tag{23.6.18}\label{eq:23.6.18}
\end{equation}
By choosing $\alpha_f=-(\theta+\phi/M)c_f/2$ with constant coefficients
$c_f$ satisfying $c_u+c_d=1$, we eliminate the term in the Lagrangian
involving $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_\alpha^{\mu\nu}F_\alpha^{\rho\sigma}$,
and change the mass term in the low energy quantum chromodynamic
Lagrangian density to
\begin{align}
\mathcal L_m={}&-m_u\bar u
\exp[-ic_u(\theta+\phi/M)\gamma_5]u
\notag\\
&-m_d\bar d
\exp[-ic_d(\theta+\phi/M)\gamma_5]d.
\tag{23.6.19}\label{eq:23.6.19}
\end{align}
In addition, from the kinematic part of the quark Lagrangian we pick up
a derivative interaction term
\begin{equation}
\frac{i c_u}{2M}(\bar u\gamma^\mu\gamma_5)u\,\partial_\mu\phi
+\frac{i c_d}{2M}(\bar d\gamma^\mu\gamma_5)d\,\partial_\mu\phi,
\tag{23.6.20}\label{eq:23.6.20}
\end{equation}
so that $f_u$ and $f_d$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:23.6.17} are replaced with
$f'_u=f_u-c_u/2$ and $f'_d=f_d-c_d/2$. To derive an effective Lagrangian
for low-energy pions and axions, we follow the procedure of
\hyperref[sec:19.5]{Section 19.5} (but now with $u$ and $d$ quarks in
place of protons and neutrons), and make the replacements:
\begin{alignat}{2}
\bar u u&\longrightarrow v\cos(\pi^0/F_\pi),\qquad&
\bar d d&\longrightarrow v\cos(\pi^0/F_\pi),
\notag\\
\bar u\gamma_5u&\longrightarrow iv\sin(\pi^0/F_\pi),&
\bar d\gamma_5d&\longrightarrow-iv\sin(\pi^0/F_\pi),
\notag\\
i\bar u\gamma^\mu\gamma_5u&\longrightarrow
\frac12F_\pi\partial^\mu\pi^0+\cdots,\qquad&
i\bar d\gamma^\mu\gamma_5d&\longrightarrow
\frac12F_\pi\partial^\mu\pi^0+\cdots.
\tag{23.6.21}\label{eq:23.6.21}
\end{alignat}
where $v$ is the constant $v=\langle\bar u u\rangle=\langle\bar d d\rangle$,
and ``$\cdots$'' denotes terms that do not have the one-pion pole. From
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:23.6.19} and \eqref{eq:23.6.20} we find the effective
pion-axion Lagrangian
\begin{align}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{eff}}={}&
-\frac12\partial_\mu\pi^0\partial^\mu\pi^0
-\frac12\partial_\mu\phi'\partial^\mu\phi'
-\frac{f'_u-f'_d}{2M}F_\pi
\partial_\mu\phi'\partial^\mu\pi^0
\notag\\
&-m_uv\cos\left(\frac{\pi^0}{F_\pi}-c_u\frac{\phi'}{M}\right)
-m_dv\cos\left(\frac{\pi^0}{F_\pi}+c_d\frac{\phi'}{M}\right),
\tag{23.6.22}\label{eq:23.6.22}
\end{align}
where $\phi'$ is the difference between the axion field and its
expectation value
\begin{equation}
\phi'\equiv\phi-\langle\phi\rangle=\phi+M\theta.
\tag{23.6.23}\label{eq:23.6.23}
\end{equation}
Since $c_u$ and $c_d$ are arbitrary except for the condition that
$c_u+c_d=1$, we are free to eliminate the
$\partial_\mu\phi'\partial^\mu\pi^0$ cross term by taking
$c_u=\frac12+f_u-f_d$ and $c_d=\frac12+f_d-f_u$, so that
$f'_u=f'_d$. The quadratic part of the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:23.6.22} is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{quad}}=
-\frac12\partial_\mu\pi^0\partial^\mu\pi^0
-\frac12\partial_\mu\phi'\partial^\mu\phi'
-\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}\pi^0\\\phi'\end{pmatrix}^{T}
\mathcal M_0^2
\begin{pmatrix}\pi^0\\\phi'\end{pmatrix},
\tag{23.6.24}\label{eq:23.6.24}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M_0^2=
\begin{pmatrix}
(m_u+m_d)v/F_\pi^2&
(-m_uc_u+m_dc_d)v/F_\pi M\\
(-m_uc_u+m_dc_d)v/F_\pi M&
(m_uc_u^2+m_dc_d^2)v/M^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{23.6.25}\label{eq:23.6.25}
\end{equation}
For $M\gg F_\pi$ one eigenvalue of $\mathcal M_0^2$ is
$(m_u+m_d)v/F_\pi^2$, which according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.7.20} is the
$\pi^0$ squared mass. The other eigenvalue is then the axion mass
\begin{equation}
m_a^2=\frac{v}{M^2}\frac{m_dm_u}{m_d+m_u}
=\frac{F_\pi^2}{M^2}\frac{m_dm_u}{(m_d+m_u)^2}m_\pi^2.
\tag{23.6.26}\label{eq:23.6.26}
\end{equation}
For the quark mass ratio derived in \hyperref[sec:19.7]{Section 19.7},
this gives $m_a=13\ \mathrm{MeV}/M(\mathrm{GeV})$.

We can also use this formalism to say something about the interactions
of axions with hadrons. The eigenvector of $\mathcal M_0^2$ with
eigenvalue $m_a^2$ has a component along the original $\pi^0$ direction
equal to $(m_uc_u-m_dc_d)F_\pi/(m_u+m_d)M$. As mentioned earlier,
because of the one-pion pole this is the dominant axion-hadron coupling.
We see that the ratio of the axion and pion production interaction
amplitudes will typically be of order $F_\pi/M$. The fact that axions
are not observed in such collisions indicates that $M>3\ \mathrm{TeV}$,
in contradiction with the original expectation
~\hyperref[ch23-ref-30]{[30]},~\hyperref[ch23-ref-31]{[31]} that the
anomalous $U(1)$ symmetry is spontaneously broken by the same scalar
vacuum expectation values of order $0.3\ \mathrm{TeV}$ that break the
electroweak $SU(2)\cdot U(1)$ symmetry. It is possible to explain why
axions are not found in reactor or accelerator experiments by taking $M$
as an independent parameter,~\hyperref[ch23-ref-32]{[32]} much larger
than the electroweak breaking scale, but there are still astrophysical
limitations. Limits on the rate of cooling of red giant stars
give~\hyperref[ch23-ref-34]{[34]} $M>10^7\ \mathrm{GeV}$, while
observations of the supernova SN1987A
indicate~\hyperref[ch23-ref-35]{[35]} that
$M>10^{10}\ \mathrm{GeV}$.\footnote{For $M>10^7\ \mathrm{GeV}$ the
axion mass would be less than about $1\ \mathrm{eV}$, so that stars are
hot enough to produce axions. The ratio of the axion and $\pi^0$ decay
rates into two photons is expected to be of order $(F_\pi/M)^2$ times a
phase space ratio of order $(m_a/m_\pi)^3$, or
\[
\frac{\Gamma(a\to\gamma+\gamma)}
{\Gamma(\pi^0\to\gamma+\gamma)}
=(F_\pi/M)^2(m_a/m_\pi)^3\approx(F_\pi/M)^5.
\]
Hence for $M>10^7\ \mathrm{GeV}$ the axion lifetime is expected to be
longer than about $10^{24}\ \mathrm{s}$, which is ample time for the
axion to travel even cosmological distances before decaying.}
Cosmological arguments suggest~\hyperref[ch23-ref-36]{[36]} an upper
bound $M<10^{12}\ \mathrm{GeV}$, leaving an open but narrow window of
allowed axion parameters.
