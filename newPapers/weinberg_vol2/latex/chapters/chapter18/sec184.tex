\subsection{Multiple Couplings and Mass Effects}

Up to now, we have considered theories with only one dimensionless coupling $g$. It is easy to extend the formalism to incorporate several such couplings $g^\ell$: for each $g^\ell$, we have a renormalization group equation that for $\mu\gg m$ takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}g^\ell(\mu)=\beta^\ell\bigl(\mathbf{g}(\mu)\bigr),
\tag{18.4.1}\label{eq:18.4.1}
\end{equation}
with each $\beta^\ell$ depending in general on all the $g$s. There are now many more possibilities for the asymptotic behavior of the $g^\ell(\mu)$ as $\mu\to\infty$; in a given theory we may have some trajectories in $g$-space that go off to infinity, at finite or infinite values of $\mu$, other trajectories that approach fixed points, and yet other trajectories that approach closed curves known as `limit cycles'. To get a taste of some of the various possibilities, let's consider the behavior of $g^\ell(\mu)$ near a fixed point.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.4.1} has a fixed-point solution $g^\ell(\mu)=g_*^\ell$ if
\begin{equation}
\beta^\ell(\mathbf{g}_*)=0.
\tag{18.4.2}\label{eq:18.4.2}
\end{equation}
In the neighborhood of this point, Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.4.1} becomes
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\bigl[g^\ell(\mu)-g_*^\ell\bigr]
=\sum_k M^\ell{}_k\bigl[g^k(\mu)-g_*^k\bigr],
\tag{18.4.3}\label{eq:18.4.3}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the matrix
\begin{equation}
M^\ell{}_k\equiv\left[\frac{\partial\beta^\ell(\mathbf{g})}{\partial g^k}\right]_{\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}_*}.
\tag{18.4.4}\label{eq:18.4.4}
\end{equation}
The solution can be expanded in eigenvectors of this matrix
\begin{equation}
g^\ell(\mu)=g_*^\ell+\sum_m c_m V_m^\ell\mu^{\lambda_m},
\tag{18.4.5}\label{eq:18.4.5}
\end{equation}
where $V_m$ is an eigenvector of $M$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_m$ (normalized in any convenient way):
\begin{equation}
\sum_k M^\ell{}_k V_m^k=\lambda_m V_m^\ell,
\tag{18.4.6}\label{eq:18.4.6}
\end{equation}
and the $c_m$ are a set of expansion coefficients.\footnote{We are assuming here that the eigenvectors $V_m$ form a complete set. This is not always so, but it is the generic case; the eigenvectors of a finite matrix $M$ will form a complete set if all of the roots of the secular equation $\operatorname{Det}(M-\lambda\mathbf{1})=0$ are different. A matrix whose eigenvectors do not form a complete set can be regarded as a limiting case of a matrix with a complete set of eigenvectors when some of its eigenvalues become degenerate.} (The summation convention is suspended in this section.)

Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.4.5} shows that the coupling constants approach the fixed point as $\mu\to\infty$ if and only if $c_m=0$ for all eigenvectors with $\lambda_m>0$. (For simplicity we are assuming here that none of the eigenvalues vanish.) Thus in general the trajectories that are attracted to the fixed point lie on an $N_-$-dimensional surface, where $N_-$ is the number of negative eigenvalues of $M$; the tangents to this surface at $\mathbf{g}_*$ are the eigenvectors with negative eigenvalues. Trajectories that are not on this surface may approach close to the fixed point, but are eventually repelled, predominantly in the direction of eigenvectors with the largest positive eigenvalues. Of course, if all eigenvalues are negative then there is a finite region around the fixed point within which all trajectories converge on this point.

Since the eigenvalues $\lambda_m$ are evidently important in learning the asymptotic behavior of trajectories that approach a fixed point, it is useful to note that these eigenvalues are independent of the definition of the couplings. Suppose we introduce a new set of couplings $\widetilde g^\ell$, defined as functions of the $g$s. These satisfy renormalization group equations
\[
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\widetilde g^\ell(\mu)
=\left.\sum_m\frac{\partial\widetilde g^\ell(\mathbf{g})}{\partial g^m}\right|_{\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}(\mu)}
\beta^m\bigl(\mathbf{g}(\mu)\bigr)
\equiv\widetilde\beta^\ell\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}(\mu)\bigr),
\]
so
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\beta^\ell(\widetilde{\mathbf{g}})
=\sum_m\frac{\partial\widetilde g^\ell(\mathbf{g})}{\partial g^m}\beta^m(\mathbf{g}).
\tag{18.4.7}\label{eq:18.4.7}
\end{equation}
(That is, $\beta$ transforms as a contravariant vector in coupling-constant space.) Differentiating, we have
\[
\sum_m\frac{\partial\widetilde\beta^\ell(\widetilde{\mathbf{g}})}{\partial\widetilde g^m}
\frac{\partial\widetilde g^m}{\partial g^k}
=\sum_m\frac{\partial^2\widetilde g^\ell}{\partial g^m\partial g^k}\beta^m(\mathbf{g})
+\sum_m\frac{\partial\widetilde g^\ell}{\partial g^m}\frac{\partial\beta^m(\mathbf{g})}{\partial g^k}.
\]
At a fixed point $\mathbf{g}_*$ the first term on the right vanishes, so this gives the matrix equation
\begin{equation}
\widetilde M S=SM,
\tag{18.4.8}\label{eq:18.4.8}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
\widetilde M^\ell{}_k&\equiv
\left[\frac{\partial\widetilde\beta^\ell}{\partial\widetilde g^k}\right]_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{g}_*)},
\tag{18.4.9}\label{eq:18.4.9}\\
S^\ell{}_k&\equiv
\left[\frac{\partial\widetilde g^\ell}{\partial g^k}\right]_{\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}_*}.
\tag{18.4.10}\label{eq:18.4.10}
\end{align}
As long as the transformation $\mathbf{g}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}$ is non-singular, Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.4.8} is a similarity transformation, and hence $M$ and $\widetilde M$ have the same eigenvalues $\lambda_m$.

The renormalization group formalism may be extended to non-renormalizable as well as renormalizable theories. As explained in Section 12.3, the infinities in non-renormalizable theories are eliminated by a suitable renormalization of coupling constants and masses, just as in renormalizable theories; the only difference is that in non-renormalizable theories the Lagrangian must be supposed to contain all possible interactions allowed by the symmetries of the theory. If $g_B^\ell$ is the unrenormalized coupling constant multiplying an operator of dimensionality $D_\ell$ in the Lagrangian (that is, a product of fields and spacetime derivatives of fields whose dimensionality in powers of mass or energy is $D_\ell$), then $g_B^\ell$ will have a dimensionality $\Delta_\ell=4-D_\ell$. We may then re-express the bare couplings in terms of a set of \emph{dimensionless} renormalized couplings $g^\ell(\mu)$ and a cut-off $\Lambda$, through relations of the general form
\begin{equation}
g_B^\ell\equiv\mu^{\Delta_\ell}
\left[g^\ell(\mu)+\sum_{k,m}b^\ell{}_{km}g^k(\mu)g^m(\mu)\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\right)
+O\bigl(g(\mu)^3\bigr)\right],
\tag{18.4.11}\label{eq:18.4.11}
\end{equation}
with the dimensionless numerical coefficients $b^\ell{}_{km}$ and similar coefficients in higher-order terms chosen to cancel the cut-off dependence of physical quantities. (In some theories the leading term might be trilinear or even higher order in couplings; the modifications that would be needed here are obvious.) From the requirement that $g_B^\ell$ be cut-off-independent, we obtain the renormalization group equation \eqref{eq:18.4.1}, with
\begin{equation}
\beta^\ell(\mathbf{g})=-\Delta_\ell g^\ell-\sum_{k,m}b^\ell{}_{km}g^k g^m+O(g^3).
\tag{18.4.12}\label{eq:18.4.12}
\end{equation}
Non-renormalizable interactions are those with $D_\ell>4$, or $\Delta_\ell<0$, so as long as the $g^\ell(\mu)$ all remain sufficiently small we expect the non-renormalizable renormalized couplings to have positive $\beta^\ell$ and hence to grow with $\mu$, but no one knows what happens when the couplings become large enough to invalidate perturbation theory.

However, as explained in the next section, even theories with infinite number of independent parameters commonly have fixed points $\mathbf{g}_*$ at which the number $N_-$ of negative eigenvalues of the matrix \eqref{eq:18.4.6} is \emph{finite}, just as it is at zero coupling. (In particular, often $N_-=1$.) Where $N_-\ne0$, the fixed point lies on an $N_-$-dimensional \emph{critical surface}, consisting of trajectories that are attracted into the fixed point as $\mu\to\infty$. A non-renormalizable theory with coupling parameters on such a critical surface, although not of course asymptotically free, is said to be asymptotically \emph{safe}~\hyperref[ch18-ref-5]{[5]}, because the renormalized couplings remain finite for large values of $\mu$. The condition of asymptotic safety in such a theory would play the role that used to be associated with the principle of renormalizability, of eliminating all but a finite number of free parameters, the coordinates of the critical surface.

In a renormalizable theory, all physical quantities are made cut-off-independent by adjusting the cut-off dependence of a \emph{finite} number of bare couplings. These bare couplings may be expressed in terms of an equal number of $\mu$-dependent renormalized couplings, and the condition that the bare couplings are $\mu$-independent yields renormalization group equations relating only these renormalized couplings. From the broader point of view which allows non-renormalizable as well as renormalizable couplings, a renormalizable theory just corresponds to a finite-dimensional \emph{invariant surface} in the infinite-dimensional space of all renormalizable and non-renormalizable theories; that is, it is a surface for which $\beta^\ell(\mathbf{g})$ at any point $\mathbf{g}$ on the surface is tangent to the surface at that point.

So far in this section we have tacitly assumed that $\mu\gg m$, so that we could neglect the dependence of $\beta^\ell$ on $m/\mu$. However, this is not necessary; we can if we like treat a mass as just another coupling parameter~\hyperref[ch18-ref-6]{[6]}. That is, all renormalized couplings can be defined as before in terms of various Green's functions at off-mass-shell momenta of order $\mu$, but now evaluated with all bare masses zero. The dimensionless renormalized mass parameters for Dirac fields $\psi$ or scalar fields $\phi$ may be defined as
\begin{align}
m_\psi(\mu)&\equiv N^{(\bar\psi\psi)}(\Lambda/\mu)^{-1}
\frac{m_{\psi,\mathrm{BARE}}(\Lambda)}{\mu},
\tag{18.4.13}\label{eq:18.4.13}\\
m_\phi^2(\mu)&\equiv N^{(\phi^2)}(\Lambda/\mu)^{-1}
\frac{m_{\phi,\mathrm{BARE}}^2(\Lambda)}{\mu^2},
\tag{18.4.14}\label{eq:18.4.14}
\end{align}
where $N^{(\mathcal O)}(\Lambda/\mu)$ are the dimensionless constants which, when multiplied into corresponding operators $\mathcal O$, cancel the infinities in the matrix elements of these operators, also evaluated with all bare masses zero. (See Section 18.1.) These new renormalized masses and couplings have no direct physical significance, but the true physical masses and all physical matrix elements can be expressed in terms of them. These matrix elements take the form of sums of matrix elements for zero bare mass, with any number of insertions of the renormalized mass operators $N^{(\phi^2)}\phi^2$ and $N^{(\bar\psi\psi)}\bar\psi\psi$, times the corresponding renormalized mass parameters.

In this renormalization scheme the beta-functions for the various couplings are obviously mass-independent, and the beta-functions for the mass parameters are proportional to these parameters, with coefficients that depend on all the various couplings; using Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.2.25}, we have
\begin{align}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}m_\psi(\mu)
&=\bigl[-1-\gamma_{\bar\psi\psi}(\mathbf{g}_\mu)\bigr]m_\psi(\mu),
\tag{18.4.15}\label{eq:18.4.15}\\
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}m_\phi^2(\mu)
&=\bigl[-2-\gamma_{\phi^2}(\mathbf{g}_\mu)\bigr]m_\phi^2(\mu).
\tag{18.4.16}\label{eq:18.4.16}
\end{align}
For instance, we noted in Section 18.2 that in the scalar field theory with Lagrangian \eqref{eq:18.1.2}, the mass operator $\phi^2$ has anomalous dimension \eqref{eq:18.2.29} for $m=0$, so here
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}m_\phi^2(\mu)
=\left[-2+\frac{g_\mu}{16\pi^2}+O(g_\mu^2)\right]m_\phi^2(\mu).
\tag{18.4.17}\label{eq:18.4.17}
\end{equation}

Also, Eq.~(11.4.3) shows that the effect of higher-order corrections to the electron propagator is to replace the electron mass by $m_e-\Sigma^*(p,m_e)$, so the effect of these corrections on matrix elements of the operator $\bar\psi_e\psi_e$ between one-electron states of four-momentum $p^\mu$ is to multiply them by a factor
\[
F(p)=1-\left(\frac{\partial\Sigma^*(p,m_e)}{\partial m_e}\right)_{m_e=0}.
\]
The renormalization constant $N^{(\bar\psi\psi)}$ for the operator $\bar\psi_e\psi_e$ is therefore equal to $F^{-1}(p)$, evaluated with $p^2$ equal to some renormalization scale, say $+\mu^2$. According to Eq.~(11.4.8), to one-loop order this is
\begin{align}
N^{(\bar\psi\psi)}
&=1+\left(\frac{\partial\Sigma^*_{\text{1 loop}}(p,m_e)}{\partial m_e}\right)_{m_e=0,\,p^2=\mu^2}
\notag\\
&=1-\frac{4\pi^2}{(2\pi)^4}\int_0^1 dx\,
\ln\left[1+\left(\frac{\Lambda^2}{\mu^2}\right)(1-x)\right]
\notag\\
&\longrightarrow 1-\frac{e^2}{4\pi^2}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda^2}{\mu^2}\right)-1\right],
\tag{18.4.18}\label{eq:18.4.18}
\end{align}
where $\Lambda$ is an ultraviolet cut-off,\footnote{This notation is different from that of Eq.~(11.4.8), where the ultraviolet cut-off was called $\mu$.} and we take the limit $\Lambda\gg\mu$. The anomalous dimension of the operator $\bar\psi_e\psi_e$ is therefore given by \eqref{eq:18.2.25} as
\begin{equation}
\gamma^{(\bar\psi\psi)}=\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\ln N^{(\bar\psi\psi)}
=\frac{e_\mu^2}{2\pi^2}+O(e_\mu^4),
\tag{18.4.19}\label{eq:18.4.19}
\end{equation}
so Eq.~\eqref{eq:18.4.15} here reads
\begin{equation}
\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}m_e(\mu)
=\left[-1-\frac{e_\mu^2}{2\pi^2}+O(e_\mu^4)\right]m_e(\mu).
\tag{18.4.20}\label{eq:18.4.20}
\end{equation}
The same formula holds in general gauge theories, with $e^2$ replaced with the value of $\sum_\alpha(t_\alpha)^2$ for the particular species of fermion in question.

The important difference between the $m(\mu)$ and the other renormalized parameters of the theory is of course that bare masses have positive dimensionality, so as long as the couplings remain small the $m(\mu)$ all decrease in magnitude. Our previous assumption that masses may be neglected as $\mu\to\infty$ is justified if in fact $m(\mu)$ does vanish for $\mu\to\infty$. However this is only known to be the case in asymptotically free theories, where the couplings all do remain small for $\mu\to\infty$; in all other cases this assumption is just an educated guess.
