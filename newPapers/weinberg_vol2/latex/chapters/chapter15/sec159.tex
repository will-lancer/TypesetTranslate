\subsection[The Batalin--Vilkovisky Formalism]{The Batalin--Vilkovisky Formalism\footnotemark}
\footnotetext{This section lies somewhat out of the book's main line of development, and may be omitted in a first reading.}

This section will describe a powerful formalism, widely known as the
Batalin--Vilkovisky~\hyperref[ch15-ref-19]{[19]} method. It is developed in the
Lagrangian framework, but has its roots in the earlier
Batalin--Fradkin--Vilkovisky formalism~\hyperref[ch15-ref-20]{[20]}, which had
been derived in the Hamiltonian framework. (The two schemes have been proved
perturbatively equivalent~\hyperref[ch15-ref-21]{[21]}.) As we shall see in
Section 17.1, the same formal machinery had been developed even earlier by
Zinn--Justin~\hyperref[ch15-ref-22]{[22]} in order to deal with the
renormalization of gauge theories. There are at least three areas where this
formalism has proved invaluable:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Up to this point, we have considered only irreducible symmetries
with an algebra that closes in the sense of Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.10}. In some
theories, such as supergravity (without auxiliary fields)~\hyperref[ch15-ref-23]{[23]},
the algebra is open: it closes only when the field equations are satisfied,
so that terms appear in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.10} proportional to
$\delta I/\delta\chi^n$. Similar terms will also then appear in the
consistency conditions \eqref{eq:15.8.12}. Equation~\eqref{eq:15.8.11} then
shows that in such theories $s^2$ will not vanish, but rather will equal a
linear combination of the derivatives $\delta I/\delta\chi^n$. As we will see
in this section, the Batalin--Vilkovisky method allows us to deal with very
general gauge theories, including those with open or reducible gauge symmetry
algebras.

\item[(ii)] As mentioned above, essential aspects of the
Batalin--Vilkovisky formalism were originally developed by Zinn--Justin in
order to prove the renormalizability of gauge theories. The crucial point, to
be explained in Section 17.1, is that although the sum of all
one-particle-irreducible diagrams in a background field does not obey the BRST
symmetries of the original action, it does share one of the key properties of
the action, known as the master equation.

\item[(iii)] The Batalin--Vilkovisky method provides a convenient way of
analyzing the possible violations of symmetries of the action by quantum
effects. It is used for this purpose in Section 22.6.
\end{enumerate}

The starting point of the Batalin--Vilkovisky formalism is the introduction of
what are called ``antifields,'' one for each field in the theory. We let
$\chi^n$ run over all the fields $\phi^r$, $\omega^A$, $\omega^{*A}$, and
$h^A$, and for each $\chi^n$ we introduce an external antifield
$\chi_n^{\ddagger}$, with the same Bose or Fermi statistics and opposite ghost
number as that of the BRST-transformed field $s\chi^n$.
\footnote{The symbol $\ddagger$ is used here in place of the more usual $*$ in
order to emphasize that it has nothing whatever to do with complex
conjugation or charge conjugation. In particular, the antighost field
$\omega^{*A}$ is \emph{not} the same as the antifield
$\omega_A^{\ddagger}$ of $\omega^A$.}
That is, $\chi_n^{\ddagger}$ has the opposite statistics to $\chi^n$, and a
ghost number equal to $-\operatorname{gh}(\chi^n)-1$, where
$\operatorname{gh}(\chi^n)$ is the ghost number of $\chi^n$. In the simplest
cases, including Yang--Mills theories and quantum gravity, the original
gauge-invariant action $I[\phi]$ is supplemented with a term coupling the
antifields $\chi_n^{\ddagger}$ to the $s\chi^n$, giving an action
\begin{equation}
S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]=I[\phi]+(s\chi^n)\chi_n^{\ddagger}.
\tag{15.9.1}\label{eq:15.9.1}
\end{equation}
This satisfies what is called the \emph{master equation}
\begin{equation}
0=\frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
  \frac{\delta_L S}{\delta\chi^n},
\tag{15.9.2}\label{eq:15.9.2}
\end{equation}
with `$R$' and `$L$' here denoting right- and left-differentiation. To check
this, note that the terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.2} of zeroth order in
antifields just yield the condition of gauge invariance
\begin{equation}
0=(s\phi^r)\frac{\delta_L I}{\delta\phi^r}
  =\omega^A\delta_A I[\phi],
\tag{15.9.3}\label{eq:15.9.3}
\end{equation}
while the terms linear in the antifields $\phi_r^{\ddagger}$ provide the
condition of nilpotence:
\begin{equation}
0=(s\chi^m)\frac{\delta_L(s\chi^n)}{\delta\chi^m}=s^2\chi^n.
\tag{15.9.4}\label{eq:15.9.4}
\end{equation}

The $\chi_n^{\ddagger}$ are external fields, and must be given suitable values
before we use $S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ to calculate the $S$-matrix. For this
purpose, we introduce an arbitrary fermionic functional $\Psi[\chi]$ with
ghost number $-1$, and set
\footnote{It is not necessary to distinguish between left- and
right-differentiation here, because either $\chi^n$ or
$\delta\Psi/\delta\chi^n$ must be bosonic.}
\begin{equation}
\chi_n^{\ddagger}=\frac{\delta\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi^n}.
\tag{15.9.5}\label{eq:15.9.5}
\end{equation}
Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.1} becomes
\begin{equation}
S[\phi,\delta\Psi/\delta\chi]
=I[\phi]+(s\chi^n)\frac{\delta\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi^n}
=I[\phi]+s\Psi[\chi].
\tag{15.9.6}\label{eq:15.9.6}
\end{equation}
Comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.8.14} shows that this is the same as the
gauge-fixed action $I_{\mathrm{NEW}}[\chi]$. Thus, using the same arguments as
in the previous section, physical matrix elements are not affected by small
changes in $\Psi$. The action \eqref{eq:15.8.7} constructed by the
Faddeev--Popov--De Witt method corresponds to the choice
$\Psi=-\omega^{*A}f_A$, for which
$\phi_r^{\ddagger}=\omega^{*A}\delta f_A/\delta\phi^r$,
$\omega_C^{\ddagger}=0$, and $\omega_A^{*\ddagger}=-f_A$.

So far, nothing new has been accomplished. The first new point is that the
master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.2} can be used for more general theories by
letting $S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ be a non-linear functional of the antifields
$\chi_n^{\ddagger}$. (For reducible theories we must also include ghosts for
ghosts among the $\chi^n$, as discussed in the previous section, along with
their antifields.) As above, we take the statistics of $\chi_n^{\ddagger}$ to
be opposite to that of $\chi^n$, and its ghost number equal to
$-\operatorname{gh}(\chi^n)-1$, and we require $S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ to be
a bosonic operator of ghost number zero. Because $\omega^{*A}$ and $h_A$ have
linear BRST transformations they are unaffected by the complications affecting
the other $\chi^n$ (in this connection, see Section 16.4), so they and their
antifields enter in the action $S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ in the same way as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.1}. That is,
\begin{equation}
S=S_{\min}[\phi,\omega,\phi^{\ddagger},\omega^{\ddagger}]
  -h^A\omega_A^{*\ddagger},
\tag{15.9.7}\label{eq:15.9.7}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_r^{\ddagger}$, $\omega_A^{\ddagger}$, and
$\omega_A^{*\ddagger}$ are the antifields of $\phi^r$, $\omega^A$, and
$\omega^{*A}$, with ghost numbers $-1$, $-2$, and $0$, respectively, and
$S_{\min}[\phi,\omega,\phi^{\ddagger},\omega^{\ddagger}]$ is some bosonic
functional of ghost number zero. The last term in \eqref{eq:15.9.7} has no
effect on the master equation, so $S_{\min}$ satisfies the master equation by
itself.
\footnote{The fields $\phi^r$, $\omega^A$, $\phi_r^{\ddagger}$,
$\omega_A^{\ddagger}$ are sometimes called \emph{minimal variables}, while
fields like $\omega^{*A}$ and $h_A$ which together with their antifields enter
bilinearly as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.7}, are called \emph{trivial pairs}.}

Because $S_{\min}$ has ghost number zero, its expansion in powers of antifields
must take the form
\begin{align}
S_{\min}={}&I[\phi]+\omega^A f_A^r[\phi]\phi_r^{\ddagger}
+\frac12\omega^A\omega^B f^C{}_{AB}[\phi]\omega_C^{\ddagger}
\notag\\
&+\frac12\omega^A\omega^B f^{rs}_{AB}[\phi]
  \phi_r^{\ddagger}\phi_s^{\ddagger}
+\omega^A\omega^B\omega^C f^{rD}_{ABC}[\phi]
  \phi_r^{\ddagger}\omega_D^{\ddagger}
\notag\\
&+\frac12\omega^A\omega^B\omega^C\omega^D f^{EF}_{ABCD}[\phi]
  \omega_E^{\ddagger}\omega_F^{\ddagger}+\cdots.
\tag{15.9.8}\label{eq:15.9.8}
\end{align}
The term in the master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.2} of zeroth order in
antifields (and hence first order in $\omega^A$) yields
\begin{equation}
0=f_A^r[\phi]\frac{\delta I[\phi]}{\delta\phi^r},
\tag{15.9.9}\label{eq:15.9.9}
\end{equation}
which is just the statement that $I[\phi]$ is invariant under the
transformation
\begin{equation}
\phi^r\longrightarrow\phi^r+\epsilon^A f_A^r[\phi]
\tag{15.9.10}\label{eq:15.9.10}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon^A$ arbitrary infinitesimals. The term in the master equation
proportional to $\phi_s^{\ddagger}$ on the right and $\omega^A\omega^B$ on the
left yields
\begin{equation}
0=f_A^r[\phi]\frac{\delta f_B^s[\phi]}{\delta\phi^r}
-f_B^r[\phi]\frac{\delta f_A^s[\phi]}{\delta\phi^r}
+f^C{}_{AB}[\phi]f_C^s[\phi]
+\frac{\delta I[\phi]}{\delta\phi^r}f^{rs}_{AB}[\phi],
\tag{15.9.11}\label{eq:15.9.11}
\end{equation}
which, when the field equations $\delta I/\delta\phi=0$ are satisfied, becomes
the commutation relation (with structure constant $f^C{}_{AB}[\phi]$) for the
transformation \eqref{eq:15.9.10}. The other term in the master equation
linear in antifields is proportional to $\omega^A\omega^B\omega^C$ on the
left and $\omega_D^{\ddagger}$ on the right; it yields
\begin{equation}
0=f^r_{[A}[\phi]\frac{\delta f^D{}_{BC]}[\phi]}{\delta\phi^r}
-f^E{}_{[AB}[\phi]f^D{}_{C]E}[\phi]
+f^{rD}_{ABC}[\phi]\frac{\delta I[\phi]}{\delta\phi^r},
\tag{15.9.12}\label{eq:15.9.12}
\end{equation}
where square brackets in the subscripts indicate antisymmetrization with
respect to the enclosed indices $A$, $B$, and $C$. When the field equations
are satisfied this becomes the generalized Jacobi identity
\eqref{eq:15.8.12}. Equation~\eqref{eq:15.9.11} is necessary for the
consistency of the symmetry condition \eqref{eq:15.9.9} (assuming that the
$f_A^r$ furnish a complete set of gauge symmetries), while
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.12} is necessary for the consistency of the commutation
relations \eqref{eq:15.9.11}. Note that the terms in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.9.11}
and \eqref{eq:15.9.12} that arise from the terms in $S_{\min}$ quadratic in
antifields are proportional to $\delta I[\phi]/\delta\chi$, so they vanish when
the field equations are satisfied, and in this sense are characteristic of
open symmetry algebras. Terms in the master equation of second or higher order
in antifields involve terms in $S_{\min}$ that are of third and/or higher
order in antifields. These provide consistency conditions for
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.9.11} and \eqref{eq:15.9.12}, consistency conditions for
these consistency conditions, and so on. It is a virtue of the
Batalin--Vilkovisky formalism that all these consistency conditions are
incorporated in the one master equation.

The master equation may be reinterpreted as a statement of invariance of $S$
under a generalized BRST transformation. In order to see this, and for future
purposes, it is useful to introduce a formal device known as the
\emph{antibracket}. Returning now to our previous notation, the antibracket of
two general functionals $F[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ and
$G[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
(F,G)\equiv
\frac{\delta_R F}{\delta\chi^n}\frac{\delta_L G}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
-\frac{\delta_R F}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}\frac{\delta_L G}{\delta\chi^n}.
\tag{15.9.13}\label{eq:15.9.13}
\end{equation}
Note that right- and left-functional derivatives of a bosonic functional like
$S$ with respect to a bosonic or fermionic field variable are equal to each
other or negatives of each other, respectively. Since just one of either
$\chi_n^{\ddagger}$ or $\chi^n$ is always fermionic and the other bosonic, it
follows that for the antibracket $(S,S)$ the second term on the right-hand side
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.13} changes sign if we reverse left- and
right-differentiation
\[
\frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_L S}{\delta\chi^n}
=-\frac{\delta_L S}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi^n}
=-\frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi^n}
\frac{\delta_L S}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}.
\]
(The last step is allowed because, since one of the factors here is bosonic,
their order is immaterial.) We see that the second term on the right in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.13} for $(S,S)$ is the negative of the first. The master
equation \eqref{eq:15.9.2} may therefore be written as the requirement that
the antibracket of $S$ with itself vanishes:
\begin{equation}
(S,S)=0.
\tag{15.9.14}\label{eq:15.9.14}
\end{equation}

This is a non-trivial requirement, because the antibracket has the general
symmetry property
\begin{equation}
(F,G)=\pm(G,F),
\tag{15.9.15}\label{eq:15.9.15}
\end{equation}
the sign being $+1$ where $F$ and $G$ are both bosonic, and $-1$ otherwise. In
particular, $(F,F)$ automatically vanishes if $F$ is fermionic, but not if $F$
is bosonic.

The generalized BRST transformation is defined by
\begin{equation}
\widehat\delta_\theta\chi^n
=\theta\frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
=-\theta(S,\chi^n),
\tag{15.9.16}\label{eq:15.9.16}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\widehat\delta_\theta\chi_n^{\ddagger}
=-\theta\frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi^n}
=-\theta(S,\chi_n^{\ddagger}),
\tag{15.9.17}\label{eq:15.9.17}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is a fermionic infinitesimal constant. (When $S$ is of the form
\eqref{eq:15.9.1}, the transformation of $\chi$ is the same as the original
BRST transformation $\delta_\theta\chi^n=\theta s\chi^n$.) To evaluate the
effect of this transformation on general functionals, we note that the
antibracket acts as a derivative, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
(F,GH)=(F,G)H\pm G(F,H),
\tag{15.9.18}\label{eq:15.9.18}
\end{equation}
where the sign is $-1$ if $G$ is fermionic and $F$ is bosonic, and $+1$
otherwise. Hence if $G$ and $H$ are arbitrary functionals of $\chi$ and
$\chi^{\ddagger}$ with $\widehat\delta_\theta G=-\theta(S,G)$ and
$\widehat\delta_\theta H=-\theta(S,H)$, then
\[
\widehat\delta_\theta(GH)
=-\theta(S,G)H-G\theta(S,H)
=\theta\bigl[(S,G)H\pm G(S,H)\bigr],
\]
where the sign is $+$ or $-$ if $G$ is bosonic or fermionic. Taking $F$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.18} equal to the bosonic functional $S$, we see then that
\[
\widehat\delta_\theta(GH)=-\theta(S,GH).
\]
Together with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:15.9.16} and \eqref{eq:15.9.17}, this shows that
for any functional $F$ formed as a sum of products of fields and antifields
\begin{equation}
\widehat\delta_\theta F=-\theta(S,F).
\tag{15.9.19}\label{eq:15.9.19}
\end{equation}
The master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.14} may be interpreted as the statement
that these generalized BRST transformations leave $S$ invariant
\begin{equation}
\widehat\delta_\theta S=-\theta(S,S)=0.
\tag{15.9.20}\label{eq:15.9.20}
\end{equation}
Like the original BRST transformation, this symmetry transformation is
nilpotent. To see this, we use the Jacobi identity for the antibracket:
\begin{equation}
\pm(F,(G,H))+\text{cyclic permutations}=0,
\tag{15.9.21}\label{eq:15.9.21}
\end{equation}
where the sign in the first term is $-$ if $F$ and $H$ are bosonic and $+$
otherwise, with corresponding signs for the other two cyclic permutations of
$F$, $G$, and $H$. Taking $F=G=S$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.21} becomes
\[
0=\mp(S,(S,H))\mp(H,(S,S))+(S,(H,S))
=\mp2(S,(S,H))\mp(H,(S,S)),
\]
where the sign is $-$ or $+$ if $H$ is bosonic or fermionic. The master
equation \eqref{eq:15.9.14} then yields the nilpotency condition
\begin{equation}
(S,(S,H))=0.
\tag{15.9.22}\label{eq:15.9.22}
\end{equation}

Because of this symmetry, the solution of the master equation is not unique.
For instance, Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.22} shows that for any given solution $S$ we
can find another solution given by the infinitesimal transformation
\begin{equation}
S'=S+(\delta F,S),
\tag{15.9.23}\label{eq:15.9.23}
\end{equation}
with $\delta F$ an infinitesimal functional of $\chi$ and $\chi^{\ddagger}$
that is arbitrary, except that it must be fermionic and have ghost number
$-1$ in order that $S'$ should be bosonic and have ghost number zero. In
particular, taking $\delta F$ to be a fermionic functional
$\epsilon\Psi$ of $\chi^n$ alone gives
\begin{equation}
S'[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]
=S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]
+\epsilon\frac{\delta\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi^n}
 \frac{\delta_R S}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
=S\left[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}
+\epsilon\frac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\chi}\right].
\tag{15.9.24}\label{eq:15.9.24}
\end{equation}
These infinitesimal transformations may be trivially integrated, and show
that the master equation is still satisfied if we shift the antifields to new
variables
$\chi_n^{\ddagger\prime}\equiv
\chi_n^{\ddagger}-\delta\Psi/\delta\chi^n$.

The transformation \eqref{eq:15.9.23} is a special case of what are usually
called canonical transformations, and will here be called ``anticanonical
transformations'' to distinguish them from the canonical transformations of
Chapter 7. An anticanonical transformation is any transformation of fields and
antifields, finite or infinitesimal, that leaves unchanged the fundamental
antibracket relations:
\begin{equation}
(\chi^n,\chi_m^{\ddagger})=\delta^n_m,
\qquad
(\chi^n,\chi^m)=(\chi_n^{\ddagger},\chi_m^{\ddagger})=0.
\tag{15.9.25}\label{eq:15.9.25}
\end{equation}
For instance, consider the infinitesimal anticanonical transformation
generated by an infinitesimal fermion generator $\delta F$, under which any
bosonic or fermionic functional $G$ is transformed into
\begin{equation}
G\longrightarrow G'=G+(\delta F,G).
\tag{15.9.26}\label{eq:15.9.26}
\end{equation}
It is easy to show that this does not change the fundamental antibrackets
\eqref{eq:15.9.25}. For this purpose, note that the antibracket $(G,H)$ of two
functionals $G$ and $H$ is transformed into $(G',H')$, which to first order in
infinitesimals is
\[
(G',H')=(G,H)+((\delta F,G),H)+(G,(\delta F,H)).
\]
Using the Jacobi identity \eqref{eq:15.9.21}, this is
\begin{equation}
(G',H')=(G,H)\pm(\delta F,(G,H)),
\tag{15.9.27}\label{eq:15.9.27}
\end{equation}
with the sign $+$ if $G$ and $H$ are both bosonic, and $-$ otherwise. In
particular, if $(G,H)$ is a c-number then it is unchanged by an anticanonical
transformation. (This is another way of seeing that the transformation
\eqref{eq:15.9.23} leaves the master equation unchanged.) The fields and
antifields have c-number antibrackets \eqref{eq:15.9.25}, so the same must be
true for the transformed fields and antifields.

To calculate the $S$-matrix we must give some definite value to the
antifields. As in the simple case of closed gauge algebras where $S$ is of the
linear form \eqref{eq:15.9.5}, we can do this by taking the antifields in the
form \eqref{eq:15.9.5}; that is, we calculate the $S$-matrix using the
``gauge-fixed'' action
\begin{equation}
I_\Psi[\chi]=S\left[\chi,\frac{\delta\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi}\right],
\tag{15.9.28}\label{eq:15.9.28}
\end{equation}
where $\Psi[\chi]$ is a fermionic functional of ghost number $-1$. According
to the remarks following Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.24}, this is the same as taking
the canonically transformed antifields $\chi^{\ddagger\prime}$ equal to zero.

The gauge-fixed action is invariant under a BRST transformation that acts on
fields $\chi^n$ alone:
\begin{equation}
\delta_\theta\chi^n=\theta s\chi^n,
\qquad
s\chi^n=\left.
\frac{\delta_R S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}.
\tag{15.9.29}\label{eq:15.9.29}
\end{equation}
To check this, note that
\begin{align*}
sI_\Psi[\chi]
={}&\left.
\left(
\frac{\delta_R S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_L S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}{\delta\chi^n}
\right)\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}
\\
&+\left.
\left(
\frac{\delta_R S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}{\delta\chi_m^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_L^2\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi^m\delta\chi^n}
\frac{\delta_L S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\right)\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}.
\end{align*}
The first term on the right-hand side vanishes as a result of the master
equation, and the second because the summand is antisymmetric
\footnote{For $\chi^m$ and $\chi^n$ both bosonic this is because
$\delta S/\delta\chi_m^{\ddagger}$ and
$\delta S/\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}$ anticommute. For one of $\chi^m$ and
$\chi^n$ fermionic and the other bosonic, this is because the right- and
left-derivatives of $S$ with respect to whichever $\chi$ is fermionic have
opposite signs. The terms with $\chi^m$ and $\chi^n$ both fermionic are
antisymmetric because for these terms
$\delta_L^2\Psi/\delta\chi^m\delta\chi^n$ is antisymmetric.}
in $m$ and $n$.

For closed algebras with $S$ of the form \eqref{eq:15.9.1}, the transformation
\eqref{eq:15.9.29} is the original BRST transformation
$\delta_\theta\chi^n=\theta s\chi^n$. But for general open algebras, the
transformation \eqref{eq:15.9.29} is not the same as the original BRST
transformation, and in general is not even nilpotent unless the field equations
are satisfied. Instead, from the terms in the master equation of first order
in the shifted antifields
$\chi_n^{\ddagger\prime}=\chi_n^{\ddagger}-\delta\Psi[\chi]/\delta\chi^n$,
we find
\begin{equation}
s^2\chi^m
=\mp\left.
\left(
\frac{\delta_L}{\delta\chi_m^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_R}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}I[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]
\right)\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}
\frac{\delta I_\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi^n},
\tag{15.9.30}\label{eq:15.9.30}
\end{equation}
with the sign $-$ or $+$ if $\chi^m$ is bosonic or fermionic, respectively.
Again, we see that the dependence on the field equations characteristic of
open gauge algebras is associated with terms in $S$ quadratic in the
antifields.

Until now we have been considering only the formulation of a classical field
theory based on an open or closed gauge algebra. Now we must consider how
quantum mechanical calculations are done in such theories. Physical matrix
elements may be calculated by functional integrals weighted with
$\exp(iI_\Psi[\chi])$, where as explained above, $I_\Psi[\chi]$ is obtained
from $S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$ by setting
$\chi_n^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi[\chi]/\delta\chi^n$, or in other words
$\chi_n^{\ddagger\prime}=0$. We wish to evaluate the effect of a change in
$\Psi[\chi]$ on these matrix elements. First consider the vacuum--vacuum
amplitude
\begin{equation}
Z_\Psi=\int\left[\prod d\chi\right]\exp(iI_\Psi[\chi]).
\tag{15.9.31}\label{eq:15.9.31}
\end{equation}
Under a shift $\delta\Psi[\chi]$ in $\Psi[\chi]$, this changes by an amount
\begin{equation}
\delta Z=i\int\left[\prod d\chi\right]\exp(iI_\Psi[\chi])
\left.
\left(\frac{\delta_R S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}
{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}\right)
\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}
\left(\frac{\delta(\delta\Psi[\chi])}{\delta\chi^n}\right).
\tag{15.9.32}\label{eq:15.9.32}
\end{equation}
Integrating by parts in field space, this becomes
\begin{align}
\delta Z={}&\int\left[\prod d\chi\right]\exp(iI_\Psi[\chi])
\left.
\left\{
\frac{\delta_R S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]}
{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_L I_\Psi[\chi]}{\delta\chi^n}
-i\Delta S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]
\right\}\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}
\delta\Psi[\chi],
\tag{15.9.33}\label{eq:15.9.33}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
\Delta\equiv
\frac{\delta_R}{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}
\frac{\delta_L}{\delta\chi^n}.
\tag{15.9.34}\label{eq:15.9.34}
\end{equation}
We see that the condition for the $\Psi$ independence of the vacuum--vacuum
amplitude is in general \emph{not} the master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.2}, but
rather what is called the \emph{quantum master equation}
\begin{equation}
(S,S)-2i\Delta S=0
\qquad\text{at}\qquad
\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi.
\tag{15.9.35}\label{eq:15.9.35}
\end{equation}
In cgs units a factor $1/\hbar$ would accompany each factor of
$S[\chi,\chi^{\ddagger}]$, so the second term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.35} would
have a coefficient $-2i\hbar$ in place of $-2i$. Thus whenever the quantum
master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.35} is satisfied, the term in $S$ of zeroth
order in $\hbar$ satisfies the original master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.2}.
Usually it is easy to construct an action that satisfies the classical master
equation, so that the first term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.35} vanishes, and the
question is then whether the second term also vanishes. The case where the
quantum master equation is not satisfied by a local action is considered in
Chapter 22, on anomalies.

Assuming the quantum master equation \eqref{eq:15.9.35} to be satisfied, the
change in the vacuum expectation value of an operator $\mathcal O[\chi]$ due
to a change $\delta\Psi$ in $\Psi$ is
\begin{equation}
\delta\langle\mathcal O\rangle
=-\frac{i}{Z_\Psi}\int\left[\prod d\chi\right]
\exp(iI_\Psi[\chi])
\frac{\delta_R\mathcal O[\chi]}{\delta\chi^n}
\left.
\left(-\frac{\delta_R S(\chi,\chi^{\ddagger})}
{\delta\chi_n^{\ddagger}}\right)
\right|_{\chi^{\ddagger}=\delta\Psi/\delta\chi}
\delta\Psi[\chi].
\tag{15.9.36}\label{eq:15.9.36}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of the exponential in the integrand in Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.36}
is just $s\mathcal O[\chi]$. We see that expectation values of operators that
are invariant
\footnote{For open gauge theories there are generally no operators other than
constants that are invariant under the transformations
\eqref{eq:15.9.16}--\eqref{eq:15.9.17}. Instead one should consider operators
$\mathcal O(\chi,\chi^{\ddagger})$ that are invariant under the nilpotent
``quantum BRST operator'' $\sigma$, defined by
$\sigma\mathcal O=(\mathcal O,S)-i\Delta\mathcal O$. Where $\mathcal O$ depends
only on $\chi$, this condition reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:15.9.36}. If
$\sigma\mathcal O=0$ then the expectation value of
$\mathcal O(\Psi,\delta\Psi/\delta\chi)$ is unaffected by a small change in
the gauge-fixing fermion $\Psi$~\hyperref[ch15-ref-24]{[24]}.}
under the generalized BRST transformation
\eqref{eq:15.9.16}--\eqref{eq:15.9.17} are unaffected by a change in the
gauge-fixing fermion $\Psi$. Corresponding results hold for vacuum expectation
values of two or more operators.
