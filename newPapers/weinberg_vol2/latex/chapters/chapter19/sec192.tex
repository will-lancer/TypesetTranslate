\subsection{Goldstone Bosons}
\label{sec:19.2}

We now specialize to the case of a spontaneously broken \emph{continuous}
symmetry. In this case there is a theorem, that (with one important exception,
to be considered in Chapter 21) the spectrum of physical particles must
contain one particle of zero mass and spin for each broken symmetry. Such
particles, known as Goldstone bosons (or Nambu--Goldstone bosons) were first
encountered in specific models by Goldstone~\hyperref[ch19-ref-3]{[3]} and
Nambu~\hyperref[ch19-ref-4]{[4]}; two general proofs of their existence were
then given by Goldstone, Salam, and myself~\hyperref[ch19-ref-5]{[5]}. This
section will present both of these proofs, and then go on to consider the
properties of the Goldstone bosons.

Suppose that the action and measure are invariant under a continuous
symmetry, under which a set of Hermitian scalar fields $\phi_n(x)$ (either
elementary or composite) are subjected to the linear infinitesimal
transformations
\begin{equation}
 \phi_n(x)\longrightarrow\phi_n(x)
 +i\epsilon\sum_m t_{nm}\phi_m(x),
 \tag{19.2.1}\label{eq:19.2.1}
\end{equation}
with $it_{nm}$ a finite real matrix. As we found in Section 16.4, the
effective action is then also invariant under this transformation
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n,m}\int
 \frac{\delta\Gamma[\phi]}{\delta\phi_n(x)}
 t_{nm}\phi_m(x)\,d^4x=0.
 \tag{19.2.2}\label{eq:19.2.2}
\end{equation}
We shall specialize to the case of a translationally invariant theory with
constant fields $\phi_n$, where as we saw in Section 16.2, the effective
action takes the form
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma[\phi]=-\mathcal V V(\phi),
 \tag{19.2.3}\label{eq:19.2.3}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal V$ is the spacetime volume and $V(\phi)$ is known as the
effective potential. Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.2} may then be written
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n,m}\frac{\partial V(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n}
 t_{nm}\phi_m=0.
 \tag{19.2.4}\label{eq:19.2.4}
\end{equation}
We will use this symmetry requirement in a form obtained by differentiating
again with respect to $\phi_\ell$:
\begin{equation}
 \sum_n\frac{\partial V(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n}t_{n\ell}
 +\sum_{n,m}\frac{\partial^2V(\phi)}
 {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_\ell}t_{nm}\phi_m=0.
 \tag{19.2.5}\label{eq:19.2.5}
\end{equation}
Now specialize to the case where $\phi_n$ is at the minimum of $V(\phi)$,
that is, at the vacuum expectation value $\bar\phi_n$. Since $V(\phi)$ is
stationary at its minimum, the first term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.5} vanishes,
so
\begin{equation}
 \left.
 \sum_{n,m}\frac{\partial^2V(\phi)}
 {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_\ell}
 \right|_{\phi=\bar\phi}
 t_{nm}\bar\phi_m=0.
 \tag{19.2.6}\label{eq:19.2.6}
\end{equation}
The general results of Section 16.1 show that the second derivative in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.6} is just the sum of all connected
one-particle-irreducible momentum-space Feynman diagrams with external lines
labelled $n$ and $\ell$ and carrying zero four-momentum. As shown at the end
of Section 16.1, it is related to the reciprocal of the momentum-space
propagator by
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial^2V(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_\ell}
 =\Delta^{-1}_{n\ell}(0),
 \tag{19.2.7}\label{eq:19.2.7}
\end{equation}
so Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.6} gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n,m}\Delta^{-1}_{n\ell}(0)t_{nm}\bar\phi_m=0.
 \tag{19.2.8}\label{eq:19.2.8}
\end{equation}
Thus, if the symmetry is broken, so that $\sum_m t_{nm}\bar\phi_m$ is
non-zero, then this is an eigenvector of $\Delta^{-1}_{n\ell}(0)$ with
eigenvalue zero. The existence of such an eigenvector means that
$\Delta_{n\ell}(q)$ has a pole at $q^2=0$. The rank of the residue of the pole
at $q^2=0$ is equal to the dimensionality of the space of the vectors
$t\bar\phi$, with $t$ running over all the generators of continuous
symmetries of the theory. Roughly speaking, there is one massless boson for
every independent broken symmetry.

In the classic example of a broken symmetry, the Lagrangian involves a set
of $N$ real scalar fields $\phi_n$, and takes the form
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_n
 \partial_\mu\phi_n\partial^\mu\phi_n
 -\frac{\mathcal M^2}{2}\sum_n\phi_n\phi_n
 -\frac{g}{4}\left(\sum_n\phi_n\phi_n\right)^2.
 \tag{19.2.9}\label{eq:19.2.9}
\end{equation}
This is invariant under the group $O(N)$, consisting of rotations of the
$N$-vector with components $\phi_n$. For constant fields the effective
potential in the tree approximation is given simply by minus the
non-derivative terms in the Lagrangian density
\begin{equation}
 V(\phi)\simeq\frac{\mathcal M^2}{2}\sum_n\phi_n\phi_n
 +\frac{g}{4}\left(\sum_n\phi_n\phi_n\right)^2.
 \tag{19.2.10}\label{eq:19.2.10}
\end{equation}
As usual we suppose that $g$ is positive-definite. (Otherwise the minimum,
if any, of $V(\phi)$ lies outside the range of validity of perturbation
theory.) If $\mathcal M^2$ is also positive, the minimum of $V(\phi)$ is at
the point $\phi=0$, which is invariant under $O(N)$. On the other hand, for
$\mathcal M^2<0$ the minimum is at points $\bar\phi_n$ at which
\begin{equation}
 \sum_n\bar\phi_n\bar\phi_n=-\mathcal M^2/g.
 \tag{19.2.11}\label{eq:19.2.11}
\end{equation}
The mass matrix in the tree approximation is then
\begin{align}
 M^2_{nm}
 &=
 \left.\frac{\partial^2V(\phi)}
 {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m}\right|_{\phi=\bar\phi}
 \notag\\
 &=\mathcal M^2\delta_{nm}
 +g\delta_{nm}\sum_\ell\bar\phi_\ell\bar\phi_\ell
 +2g\bar\phi_n\bar\phi_m
 \notag\\
 &=2g\bar\phi_n\bar\phi_m.
 \tag{19.2.12}\label{eq:19.2.12}
\end{align}
This has one eigenvector $\bar\phi_n$ with a non-zero eigenvalue:
\begin{equation}
 m^2=2g\sum_n\bar\phi_n\bar\phi_n=2\lvert\mathcal M^2\rvert,
 \tag{19.2.13}\label{eq:19.2.13}
\end{equation}
and $N-1$ eigenvectors perpendicular to $\bar\phi$ with eigenvalue zero. The
reason for the appearance of only $N-1$ Goldstone bosons is just that $O(N)$
is broken down to $O(N-1)$ (the subgroup of $O(N)$ that leaves $\bar\phi$
invariant), and so the number of independent broken symmetries is the
dimensionality of $O(N)$ minus the dimensionality of $O(N-1)$, or
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1}{2}N(N-1)-\frac{1}{2}(N-1)(N-2)=N-1.
 \tag{19.2.14}\label{eq:19.2.14}
\end{equation}

Here is another proof of the existence of Goldstone bosons, one that makes no
use of the effective action formalism. As we saw in Chapter 7, any continuous
symmetry of the action leads to the existence of a conserved current $J^\mu$:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial J^\mu(x)}{\partial x^\mu}=0,
 \tag{19.2.15}\label{eq:19.2.15}
\end{equation}
with a charge $Q$ that induces the associated symmetry transformation
\begin{equation}
 Q=\int d^3x\,J^0(\mathbf x,0),
 \tag{19.2.16}\label{eq:19.2.16}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 [Q,\phi_n(x)]=-\sum_m t_{nm}\phi_m(x).
 \tag{19.2.17}\label{eq:19.2.17}
\end{equation}
Operator relations like Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.2.15}--\eqref{eq:19.2.17} are
unaffected by spontaneous symmetry breaking, which is manifested in the
properties of physical \emph{states}. Now, consider the vacuum expectation
value of the commutator of the current and field. Summing over intermediate
states, this is
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}[J^\lambda(y),\phi_n(x)]\ket{\Omega}
 =(2\pi)^{-3}\int d^4p
 \left[\rho_n^\lambda(p)e^{ip\cdot(y-x)}
 -\widetilde\rho_n^\lambda(p)e^{ip\cdot(y-x)}\right],
 \tag{19.2.18}\label{eq:19.2.18}
\end{equation}
where, using translation invariance,
\begin{equation}
 (2\pi)^{-3}i\rho_n^\lambda(p)
 =\sum_N
 \bra{\Omega}J^\lambda(0)\ket{N}
 \bra{N}\phi_n(0)\ket{\Omega}
 \delta^4(p-p_N),
 \tag{19.2.19}\label{eq:19.2.19}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 (2\pi)^{-3}i\widetilde\rho_n^\lambda(p)
 =\sum_N
 \bra{\Omega}\phi_n(0)\ket{N}
 \bra{N}J^\lambda(0)\ket{\Omega}
 \delta^4(p-p_N).
 \tag{19.2.20}\label{eq:19.2.20}
\end{equation}
We usually take $J^\lambda$ as well as $\phi_n$ to be Hermitian operators, in
which case Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.2.19} and \eqref{eq:19.2.20} are complex
conjugates
\begin{equation}
 \rho_n^\lambda(p)=-\widetilde\rho_n^{\lambda *}(p),
 \tag{19.2.21}\label{eq:19.2.21}
\end{equation}
but this will not be assumed here.

Lorentz invariance tells us that $\rho$ and $\widetilde\rho$ must take the
forms
\begin{equation}
 \rho_n^\lambda(p)=p^\lambda\rho_n(-p^2)\theta(p^0),
 \tag{19.2.22}\label{eq:19.2.22}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde\rho_n^\lambda(p)
 =p^\lambda\widetilde\rho_n(-p^2)\theta(p^0).
 \tag{19.2.23}\label{eq:19.2.23}
\end{equation}
(The factor $\theta(p^0)$, which is $+1$ for $p^0>0$ and zero otherwise, is
required by the fact that $p_N$ is the four-momentum of a physical state.)
This gives
\begin{align}
 \bra{\Omega}[J^\lambda(y),\phi_n(x)]\ket{\Omega}
 =\frac{\partial}{\partial y_\lambda}\int d\mu^2
 \Bigl[&
 \rho_n(\mu^2)\Delta_+(y-x;\mu^2)
 \notag\\
 &+\widetilde\rho_n(\mu^2)\Delta_+(x-y;\mu^2)\Bigr],
 \tag{19.2.24}\label{eq:19.2.24}
\end{align}
where $\Delta_+$ is the familiar function
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_+(z;\mu^2)=(2\pi)^{-3}\int
 d^4p\,\theta(p^0)\delta(p^2+\mu^2)e^{ip\cdot z}.
 \tag{19.2.25}\label{eq:19.2.25}
\end{equation}
As remarked in Chapter 5, Lorentz invariance allows
$\Delta_+(z;\mu^2)$ to depend only on $z^2$, $\mu^2$, and $\theta(z^0)$, and
for $z^2>0$ only on $z^2$ and $\mu^2$. Hence
$\Delta_+(x-y;\mu^2)$ and $\Delta_+(y-x;\mu^2)$ are equal for $x-y$
spacelike, and so in this case
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}[J^\lambda(y),\phi_n(x)]\ket{\Omega}
 =\frac{\partial}{\partial y_\lambda}\int d\mu^2
 \left[\rho_n(\mu^2)+\widetilde\rho_n(\mu^2)\right]
 \Delta_+(y-x;\mu^2).
 \tag{19.2.26}\label{eq:19.2.26}
\end{equation}
But for $x-y$ spacelike all commutators must vanish, so
\begin{equation}
 \rho_n(\mu^2)=-\widetilde\rho_n(\mu^2),
 \tag{19.2.27}\label{eq:19.2.27}
\end{equation}
and therefore Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.24} gives for general $x$ and $y$:
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}[J^\lambda(y),\phi_n(x)]\ket{\Omega}
 =\frac{\partial}{\partial y_\lambda}\int d\mu^2\,\rho_n(\mu^2)
 \left[\Delta_+(y-x;\mu^2)-\Delta_+(x-y;\mu^2)\right].
 \tag{19.2.28}\label{eq:19.2.28}
\end{equation}
Where Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.21} applies, Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.27} also shows that
$\rho_n(p)$ is \emph{real}.

Now let us use the fact that $J^\lambda(y)$ is conserved. Applying the
derivative $\partial/\partial y^\lambda$ to both sides of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.28}, and using the familiar equation
\begin{equation}
 (\Box_y-\mu^2)\Delta_+(y-x;\mu^2)=0,
 \tag{19.2.29}\label{eq:19.2.29}
\end{equation}
we find, for \emph{all} $x$ and $y$
\begin{equation}
 0=\int d\mu^2\,\mu^2\rho_n(\mu^2)
 \left[\Delta_+(y-x;\mu^2)-\Delta_+(x-y;\mu^2)\right],
 \tag{19.2.30}\label{eq:19.2.30}
\end{equation}
and so (since $\Delta_+(x-y)$ is \emph{not} even for $x-y$ timelike or
lightlike)
\begin{equation}
 \mu^2\rho_n(\mu^2)=0.
 \tag{19.2.31}\label{eq:19.2.31}
\end{equation}
Normally we would conclude from this that $\rho_n(\mu^2)$ vanishes for all
$\mu^2$. However, this is not possible in the case of broken symmetry. Set
$\lambda=0$ and $x^0=y^0=t$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.28}:
\begin{align*}
 \bra{\Omega}[J^0(\mathbf y,t),\phi_n(\mathbf x,t)]\ket{\Omega}
 ={}&2i(2\pi)^{-3}\int d\mu^2\,\rho_n(\mu^2)
 \\
 &\quad{}\cdot\int d^4p\,
 \sqrt{\mathbf p^2+\mu^2}\,
 e^{i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)}
 \delta(p^2+\mu^2)
 \\
 ={}&i\delta^3(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)
 \int d\mu^2\,\rho_n(\mu^2).
\end{align*}
Integrating and using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.2.16} and \eqref{eq:19.2.17} gives
\begin{equation}
 -\sum_m t_{nm}\bra{\Omega}\phi_m\ket{\Omega}
 =i\int\rho_n(\mu^2).
 \tag{19.2.32}\label{eq:19.2.32}
\end{equation}
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.2.31} and \eqref{eq:19.2.32} can be reconciled only if
\begin{equation}
 \rho_n(\mu^2)=i\delta(\mu^2)
 \sum_m t_{nm}\bra{\Omega}\phi_m(0)\ket{\Omega}.
 \tag{19.2.33}\label{eq:19.2.33}
\end{equation}
(This is real for Hermitian fields $\phi_n$ because in this case
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.1} requires $t_{nm}$ to be imaginary.) Thus as long as the
symmetry is broken, $\rho_n(\mu^2)$ cannot vanish, but rather consists
entirely of a term proportional to $\delta(\mu^2)$. Such a term can obviously
only arise in a theory that has massless particles, because otherwise the
spectrum of center-of-mass squared energies $-p_N^2$ would not extend down to
zero. Furthermore a delta-function $\delta(\mu^2)$ can only arise from single
particle states of zero mass; multiparticle states would contribute a
continuum extending down to $\mu^2=0$. The state
$\phi_n(0)\ket{\Omega}$ is rotationally invariant, so
$\bra{N}\phi_n(0)\ket{\Omega}$ must vanish for any state $N$ of non-zero
helicity. Also $\bra{\Omega}J^0\ket{N}$ vanishes for any state $N$ that has
different intrinsic parity or (unbroken) internal quantum numbers from
$J^0$. We conclude then that \emph{a broken symmetry with
$t_{nm}\bra{\Omega}\phi_m(0)\ket{\Omega}\ne0$ requires the existence of a
massless particle of spin zero and the same parity and internal quantum
numbers as $J^0$.} These are our Goldstone bosons.

The above argument breaks down when the spontaneously broken symmetry is a
local rather than a global symmetry. Either we choose a Lorentz-invariant
gauge, such as the Landau gauge with $\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$, in which case as
shown in Section 15.7 the positivity assumptions of quantum mechanics are
violated, or we adopt a gauge like the axial gauge with $A^\mu_3=0$, in which
case the ordinary rules of quantum mechanics apply but manifest Lorentz
invariance is lost. As we shall see in Chapter 21, this exception is not just
a technicality; spontaneously broken local symmetries do not lead to
Goldstone bosons.

It will be useful to look in a little more detail at how the coefficient of
the delta-function in $\rho_N(\mu^2)$ is related to the properties of the
Goldstone boson. For a spin zero boson $B$ of four-momentum $q^\mu$, Lorentz
invariance requires the matrix element of the current between the vacuum and
one-particle states to take the form
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}J^\lambda(x)\ket{B}
 =i\frac{Fp_B^\lambda e^{ip_B\cdot x}}
 {(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_B^0}},
 \tag{19.2.34}\label{eq:19.2.34}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf p_B$ is the momentum of $B$, $p_B^0=\lvert\mathbf p_B\rvert$
and $F$ is a constant coefficient with the dimensions of energy. (This is
consistent with current conservation because $p_{B\lambda}p_B^\lambda=0$.)
Also, the matrix element of the scalar field $\phi_n(y)$ between a
single-particle state and the vacuum is of the form
\begin{equation}
 \bra{B}\phi_n(y)\ket{\Omega}
 =\frac{Z_ne^{-ip_B\cdot y}}
 {(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_B^0}},
 \tag{19.2.35}\label{eq:19.2.35}
\end{equation}
where $Z_n$ is a dimensionless constant. From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:19.2.34} and
\eqref{eq:19.2.35}, we have then
\begin{align*}
 (2\pi)^{-3}i\rho_n(-p^2)p^\lambda\theta(p^0)
 &=
 \int d^3p_B\,
 \bra{\Omega}J^\lambda(0)\ket{B}
 \bra{B}\phi_n(0)\ket{\Omega}\delta^4(p-p_B)
 \\
 &=
 \delta(p^0-\lvert\mathbf p\rvert)(2\pi)^{-3}
 (2p^0)^{-1}p^\lambda FZ_n
 \\
 &=
 \theta(p^0)\delta(-p^2)(2\pi)^{-3}p^\lambda iFZ_n,
\end{align*}
so
\begin{equation}
 \rho_n(\mu^2)=FZ_n\delta(\mu^2).
 \tag{19.2.36}\label{eq:19.2.36}
\end{equation}
Comparing with Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.33}, this gives
\begin{equation}
 iFZ_n=-\sum_m t_{nm}\bra{\Omega}\phi_m(0)\ket{\Omega}.
 \tag{19.2.37}\label{eq:19.2.37}
\end{equation}

More generally, we may have several broken symmetries with generators $t_a$
and currents $J^\mu_a$, which we can take independent in the sense that no
linear combination of the $t_a$ is unbroken. For each of these, there is a
Goldstone boson $\ket{B_a}$, and we define $Z_{an}$ and $F_{ab}$ by
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}J^\lambda_a(x)\ket{B_b}
 =i\frac{F_{ab}p_B^\lambda e^{ip_B\cdot x}}
 {(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_B^0}},
 \tag{19.2.38}\label{eq:19.2.38}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \bra{B_a}\phi_n(y)\ket{\Omega}
 =\frac{Z_{an}e^{-ip_B\cdot y}}
 {(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_B^0}}.
 \tag{19.2.39}\label{eq:19.2.39}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.37} applies for each $a$, so
\begin{equation}
 i\sum_b F_{ab}Z_{bn}
 =-\sum_m[t_a]_{nm}\bra{\Omega}\phi_m(0)\ket{\Omega}.
 \tag{19.2.40}\label{eq:19.2.40}
\end{equation}
For instance, in the $O(N)$ example discussed earlier, we can adapt our basis
so that the vacuum expectation value points in the one-direction
\begin{equation}
 \bar\phi_m\equiv\bra{\Omega}\phi_m(0)\ket{\Omega}
 =v\delta_{m1}.
 \tag{19.2.41}\label{eq:19.2.41}
\end{equation}
The $N-1$ broken symmetry generators $t_a$ (with $a=2\cdots N$) can be
defined as those for infinitesimal rotations in the $1$--$a$ plane. With a
convenient choice of normalization, these have the non-zero elements
\begin{equation}
 [t_a]_{1a}=-[t_a]_{a1}=i
 \tag{19.2.42}\label{eq:19.2.42}
\end{equation}
(with no summation over $a$). The unbroken $O(N-1)$ symmetry, under which the
$N-1$ Goldstone bosons transform according to the vector representation,
tells us that
\begin{equation}
 F_{ab}=\delta_{ab}F,\qquad Z_{a1}=0,\qquad Z_{ab}=Z\delta_{ab}.
 \tag{19.2.43}\label{eq:19.2.43}
\end{equation}
Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.40} requires
\begin{equation}
 FZ=v.
 \tag{19.2.44}\label{eq:19.2.44}
\end{equation}
It is conventional to adopt the field renormalization prescription that
$Z=1$, so $F=v$. Thus $F$ is a measure of the strength of the symmetry
breaking. As we shall now see, the parameter $1/F$ determines the strength
with which the Goldstone bosons interact with each other and with other
particles.

A broken symmetry tells us more about the Goldstone bosons than just that
they have zero mass; it also tightly constrains their interactions at low
energy. To see this in the simplest case, consider the matrix element of

\input{chapters/chapter19/figures/fig19-01.tex}

the current $J^\mu(x)$ associated with a broken symmetry. Between arbitrary
states $\alpha,\beta$:
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\beta}J^\mu(x)\ket{\alpha}
 =e^{iq\cdot x}\bra{\beta}J^\mu(0)\ket{\alpha},
 \tag{19.2.45}\label{eq:19.2.45}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
 q^\mu\equiv p_\alpha^\mu-p_\beta^\mu.
 \tag{19.2.46}\label{eq:19.2.46}
\end{equation}
We know that $J^\mu(x)$ has a non-zero matrix element between the vacuum and
a one-Goldstone-boson state $\ket{B,\mathbf q}$, given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.34}. It follows by the usual rules of polology (see
Chapter 10) that the matrix element \eqref{eq:19.2.45} has a pole at
$q^2\to0$, with\footnote{Where the Goldstone boson corresponds to an
elementary field, Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.47} may be obtained by inspection of
the class of Feynman diagrams shown in Figure~\ref{fig:19.1}. The factor
$i(2\pi)^4$ in the S-matrix element for $\alpha\to\beta+B$ is cancelled by
the factor $-i(2\pi)^{-4}$ associated with the $B$ propagator. The general
rules of polology tell us that the same holds even where the Goldstone boson
is a composite particle.}
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\beta}J^\mu(0)\ket{\alpha}
 \longrightarrow \frac{iFq^\mu}{q^2}M_{\beta\alpha},
 \tag{19.2.47}\label{eq:19.2.47}
\end{equation}
where
\[
 \frac{i(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p_\alpha-p_\beta-q)M_{\beta\alpha}}
 {(2\pi)^{3/2}(2p^0)^{1/2}}
\]
is the S-matrix element for emitting a Goldstone boson of four-momentum $q$
in the transition $\alpha\to\beta$. Let us therefore write
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\beta}J^\mu(0)\ket{\alpha}
 \equiv N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}
 +\frac{iFq^\mu}{q^2}M_{\beta\alpha},
 \tag{19.2.48}\label{eq:19.2.48}
\end{equation}
where $N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ is defined as the non-pole contributions to the
matrix element of the current.

\input{chapters/chapter19/figures/fig19-02.tex}

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.45}, we see that the conservation law
$\partial_\mu J^\mu=0$ for the current $J^\mu$ requires
\eqref{eq:19.2.48} to vanish when contracted with $q_\mu$, and so
\begin{equation}
 M_{\beta\alpha}=\frac{i}{F}q_\mu N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}.
 \tag{19.2.49}\label{eq:19.2.49}
\end{equation}
One immediate consequence is that unless $N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ has a pole at
$q\to0$, the matrix element $M_{\beta\alpha}$ for emitting a Goldstone boson
in a transition $\alpha\to\beta$ vanishes as $q\to0$. This is called an
``Adler zero''~\hyperref[ch19-ref-6]{[6]}.

In fact, it often happens that $N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ \emph{does} have a pole
at $q=0$. This is because the vertex for the current $J^\mu(x)$ might be
attached to an external line of the process $\alpha\to\beta$. (See
Figure~\ref{fig:19.2}.) For instance, if a four-momentum $q^\mu$ is carried
away by a current $J^\mu$ inserted into an outgoing or incoming particle
line of four-momentum $p$ and mass $m$, then the internal line that connects
this vertex to the rest of the diagram will carry a four-momentum $p^\mu
+q^\mu$ or $p^\mu-q^\mu$ respectively, and its propagator will therefore
contribute to $N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ a factor
\begin{equation}
 \big[(p\pm q)^2+m^2\big]^{-1}
 =\big[\pm2p\cdot q+q^2\big]^{-1}
 \longrightarrow \pm\frac{1}{2p\cdot q}.
 \tag{19.2.50}\label{eq:19.2.50}
\end{equation}
For a fixed direction of $\mathbf q$, the factor $1/\lvert\mathbf q\rvert$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.50} cancels the factor $\lvert\mathbf q\rvert$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.49}, yielding a finite (though
$\mathbf q$-direction-dependent) result in the limit
$\lvert\mathbf q\rvert\to0$. On the other hand, the contribution to
$N^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ of diagrams in which the current $J^\mu$ is attached
to an internal line of the process $\alpha\to\beta$ has no singularity for
$\lvert\mathbf q\rvert\to0$, and therefore is cancelled by the factor
$\lvert\mathbf q\rvert$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.49}. Thus
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.49} can be interpreted to mean that \emph{the amplitude
for emitting a very soft Goldstone boson in a process $\alpha\to\beta$ can
be calculated from graphs in which the Goldstone boson is emitted only from
the external lines of the process, with the vertex for emitting the
Goldstone boson given by applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.49} to the transition
between single-particle states.}

The effective action formalism can be used to derive interesting results for
the interactions of the Goldstone bosons with each other and with other
scalars. For this purpose, we note that if we define a set of renormalized
Goldstone boson fields $\pi_a$ for which
\begin{equation}
 \bra{B_a}\pi_b(x)\ket{\Omega}
 =\frac{e^{-ip_B\cdot x}\delta_{ab}}
 {(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p_B^0}},
 \tag{19.2.51}\label{eq:19.2.51}
\end{equation}
then Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.39} tells us that
\begin{equation}
 \phi_n(x)=\sum_a Z_{an}\pi_a(x)+\cdots,
 \tag{19.2.52}\label{eq:19.2.52}
\end{equation}
where ``$\cdots$'' indicates fields that do not create Goldstone bosons. But
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.40} gives
$Z_{an}=\sum_bF^{-1}_{ab}(it_b\bar\phi)_n$. Thus the amplitude for any
reaction among $N$ zero-four-momentum Goldstone bosons
$\pi_{a_1},\ldots,\pi_{a_N}$ is the same as would be calculated in the tree
approximation from the effective interaction
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal H_{\mathrm{eff}}
 =\frac{1}{N!}g_{a_1\cdots a_N}\pi_{a_1}\cdots\pi_{a_N},
 \tag{19.2.53}\label{eq:19.2.53}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
 g_{a_1\cdots a_N}
={}&\sum_{b_1\cdots b_N}
 F^{-1}_{a_1b_1}\cdots F^{-1}_{a_Nb_N}
 (it_{b_1}\bar\phi)_{n_1}\cdots(it_{b_N}\bar\phi)_{n_N}
 \notag\\
 &{}\left.
 \frac{\partial^N V(\phi)}
 {\partial\phi_{n_1}\cdots\partial\phi_{n_N}}
 \right|_{\phi=\bar\phi}.
 \tag{19.2.54}\label{eq:19.2.54}
\end{align}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.4} at $\phi=\bar\phi$ then implies that the sum of all
``tadpole'' graphs for a single Goldstone boson line disappearing into the
vacuum vanishes, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.6} tells us that the amplitudes vanish
for Goldstone bosons to make transitions at zero four-momentum into any
other scalars. To go beyond these results, we can continue to differentiate
with respect to the scalar fields. For instance, the derivative of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.5} gives for any symmetry generator $t$:
\begin{align}
 \sum_n\frac{\partial^2V(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m}t_{n\ell}
 &+\sum_n\frac{\partial^2V(\phi)}{\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_\ell}t_{nm}
 \notag\\
 &+\sum_{n,k}
 \frac{\partial^3V(\phi)}
 {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell}
 t_{nk}\phi_k=0.
 \tag{19.2.55}\label{eq:19.2.55}
\end{align}
Taking $t$ as one of the broken symmetry generators $t_a$, setting
$\phi=\bar\phi$, contracting with
$(t_b\bar\phi)_m(t_c\bar\phi)_\ell$, and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:19.2.6} gives
\begin{equation}
 \left.
 \sum_{n,m,\ell}
 \frac{\partial^3V(\phi)}
 {\partial\phi_n\partial\phi_m\partial\phi_\ell}
 \right|_{\phi=\bar\phi}
 (t_a\bar\phi)_n(t_b\bar\phi)_m(t_c\bar\phi)_\ell=0,
 \tag{19.2.56}\label{eq:19.2.56}
\end{equation}
so the sum of all graphs with three external zero-four-momentum Goldstone
boson lines vanishes. In particular, this means that in general processes,
to leading order in small Goldstone boson energies, low energy Goldstone
bosons are not emitted from external low energy Goldstone boson lines.
