\subsection{Degenerate Vacua}
\label{sec:19.1}

We do not have to look far for examples of spontaneous symmetry breaking.
Consider a chair. The equations governing the atoms of the chair are
rotationally symmetric, but a solution of these equations, the actual chair,
has a definite orientation in space. Here we will be concerned not so much
with the breaking of symmetries by \emph{objects} like chairs, but rather with
the symmetry breaking in the ground state of any realistic quantum field
theory, the vacuum.

A spontaneously broken symmetry in field theory is always associated with a
degeneracy of vacuum states. For instance, consider a symmetry transformation
of the action, and of the measure used in integrating over fields, that acts
linearly on a set of scalar fields $\phi_n(x)$:
\begin{equation}
 \phi_n(x)\longrightarrow\phi'_n(x)
 =\sum_m L_{nm}\phi_m(x).
 \tag{19.1.1}\label{eq:19.1.1}
\end{equation}
(The $\phi_n$ need not be elementary fields; they can be composite objects,
like $\bar\psi\Gamma_n\psi$.) As we saw in Section 16.4, the quantum effective
action $\Gamma[\phi]$ will then have the same symmetry
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma[\phi]=\Gamma[L\phi].
 \tag{19.1.2}\label{eq:19.1.2}
\end{equation}
For the vacuum the expectation value of $\phi(x)$ must be at a minimum of the
vacuum energy $-\Gamma[\phi]$, say at $\phi(x)=\bar\phi$ (a constant). But if
$L\bar\phi\ne\bar\phi$, then this vacuum is not unique; $-\Gamma[\phi]$ has
the same value at $\phi=L\bar\phi$ as it does at $\bar\phi$. In the simple
special case where the symmetry transformation \eqref{eq:19.1.1} is a
reflection, $\phi\to-\phi$, if $-\Gamma(\phi)$ has a minimum at a non-zero
value $\bar\phi$ of $\phi$, then it has two minima, at $\bar\phi$ and
$-\bar\phi$, each corresponding to a state of broken symmetry.

We are not yet ready to conclude that in such cases the symmetry is broken,
because we have not yet ruled out the possibility that the true vacuum is a
linear superposition of vacuum states in which $\phi_m$ has various
expectation values, which would respect the assumed symmetry. For instance,
in a theory with a symmetry $\phi\to-\phi$, even if $\Gamma(\phi)$ has a
minimum for some non-zero value $\bar\phi$ of $\phi$, how do we know that the
true vacuum is one of the states $\ket{\Omega_\pm}$ for which $\Phi$ has
expectation values $\bar\phi$ and $-\bar\phi$, and not some linear combination
like $\ket{\Omega_+}+\ket{\Omega_-}$ that \emph{would} respect the symmetry
under $\phi\to-\phi$? The assumed symmetry under the transformation
$\phi\to-\phi$ tells us that the vacuum matrix elements of the Hamiltonian
are
\[
 \bra{\Omega_+}H\ket{\Omega_+}
 =\bra{\Omega_-}H\ket{\Omega_-}=a
\]
(with $a$ real) and
\[
 \bra{\Omega_+}H\ket{\Omega_-}
 =\bra{\Omega_-}H\ket{\Omega_+}=b
\]
(with $b$ real), so the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian are
$\ket{\Omega_+}\pm\ket{\Omega_-}$, with energies $a\pm\lvert b\rvert$.
These energy eigenstates are invariant (or invariant up to a sign) under the
symmetry $\phi\to-\phi$. In fact, the same issue also arises for chairs. The
quantum mechanical ground state of an isolated chair is actually rotationally
invariant; it is a state with zero angular momentum quantum numbers, and
hence with no definite orientation in space.

Spontaneous symmetry breaking actually occurs only for idealized systems that
are infinitely large. The appearance of broken symmetry for a chair arises
because it has a macroscopic moment of inertia $I$, so that its ground state
is part of a tower of rotationally excited states whose energies are
separated by only tiny amounts, of order $\hbar^2/I$. This gives the state
vector of the chair an exquisite sensitivity to external perturbations; even
very weak external fields will shift the energy by much more than the energy
difference of these rotational levels. In consequence, any rotationally
asymmetric external field will cause the ground state or any other state of
the chair with definite angular momentum numbers rapidly to develop
components with other angular momentum quantum numbers. The states of the
chair that are relatively stable with respect to small external perturbations
are not those with definite angular momentum quantum numbers, but rather
those with a definite orientation, in which the rotational symmetry of the
underlying theory is broken.

For the vacuum also, the possibility of spontaneous symmetry breaking is
again related to the large size of the system, specifically to the large
volume of space. In the above example of a reflection symmetry, the
off-diagonal matrix element $b$ of the Hamiltonian involves an integration
over field configurations that tunnel from the minimum at $\phi=\bar\phi$ to
the one at $\phi=-\bar\phi$, so it is smaller than the diagonal matrix element
$a$ by a barrier penetration factor that for a spatial volume $\mathcal V$ is
of the form $\exp(-C\mathcal V)$, where $C$ is a positive
constant\footnote{For instance, by analogy with the classic wave mechanical
problem of barrier penetration, for a Lagrangian density of the form
$-\frac{1}{2}\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi-V(\phi)$, we have
$C=\int_{-\bar\phi}^{+\bar\phi}\sqrt{2V(\phi)}\,d\phi$. We will not bother to
calculate the off-diagonal matrix element $b$ here, because we shall soon give
a general argument that shows that it vanishes for infinite volume.}
depending on the microscopic parameters of the theory. The two energy
eigenstates $\ket{\Omega_+}\pm\ket{\Omega_-}$ are thus essentially degenerate
for any macroscopic volume, and so are strongly mixed by any perturbation
that is an odd functional of $\phi$. Even if such a perturbation $H'$ is very
weak, its diagonal elements $\bra{\Omega_\pm}H'\ket{\Omega_\pm}$ will differ
by much more than the exponentially suppressed off-diagonal elements of
either $H$ or the perturbation. Thus the vacuum eigenstates of the perturbed
Hamiltonian will be very close to either one of the broken symmetry states
$\ket{\Omega_\pm}$ which diagonalize the perturbation, and \emph{not} to the
invariant states $\ket{\Omega_+}\pm\ket{\Omega_-}$. Which one of the states
$\ket{\Omega_\pm}$ is the true vacuum for very small perturbations? This
depends on the perturbation, but since these two states are related by a
symmetry transformation of the original Hamiltonian, it doesn't matter; if
the perturbation is sufficiently small, no observer will be able to tell the
difference.

The vanishing of matrix elements between vacuum states with different field
expectation values becomes exact in a space of infinite
volume~\hyperref[ch19-ref-1]{[1]}. For infinite volume, a general vacuum state
$\ket{\Omega_v}$ may be defined as a state with zero momentum
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf P\ket{\Omega_v}=0
 \tag{19.1.3}\label{eq:19.1.3}
\end{equation}
for which this is a \emph{discrete} momentum eigenvalue. (This excludes
single-particle or multiparticle states, for which the momentum value zero is
always part of a continuum of momentum values in a space of infinite
volume.) In general there may be a number of such states. They can usually be
expanded in a discrete set, and our notation will treat them as if they were
discrete. They will be chosen to be orthonormal
\begin{equation}
 \braket{\Omega_u}{\Omega_v}=\delta_{uv}.
 \tag{19.1.4}\label{eq:19.1.4}
\end{equation}
Any matrix element of a product of local Hermitian operators at equal times
between these states may be expressed as a sum over states:
\begin{align}
 \bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf x)B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}
 ={}&\sum_w
 \bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_w}
 \bra{\Omega_w}B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}
 \notag\\
 &+\int d^3p\sum_N
 \bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf 0)\ket{N,\mathbf p}
 \bra{N,\mathbf p}B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}
 e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x},
 \tag{19.1.5}\label{eq:19.1.5}
\end{align}
where $\ket{N,\mathbf p}$ are a set of orthonormalized continuum states of
definite three-momentum $\mathbf p$ that together with the
$\ket{\Omega_v}$ span the whole physical Hilbert space. (Here $N$ may include
continuous as well as discrete labels. Also, we are dropping time
arguments.) We assume without proof that because the $\ket{N,\mathbf p}$
belong to the continuous spectrum of the momentum operator $\mathbf P$, the
dependence of matrix elements on $\mathbf p$ is smooth enough (that is,
Lebesgue integrable) to allow the use of the Riemann--Lebesgue
theorem~\hyperref[ch19-ref-2]{[2]}, so that the integral over $\mathbf p$
vanishes as $\lvert\mathbf x\rvert\to\infty$. In this limit, we have then
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf x)B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}
 \xrightarrow{\lvert\mathbf x\rvert\to\infty}
 \sum_w\bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_w}
 \bra{\Omega_w}B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}.
 \tag{19.1.6}\label{eq:19.1.6}
\end{equation}
Likewise,
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega_u}B(\mathbf 0)A(\mathbf x)\ket{\Omega_v}
 \xrightarrow{\lvert\mathbf x\rvert\to\infty}
 \sum_w\bra{\Omega_u}B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_w}
 \bra{\Omega_w}A(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}.
 \tag{19.1.7}\label{eq:19.1.7}
\end{equation}
But causality tells us that the equal-time commutator
$[A(\mathbf x),B(\mathbf 0)]$ vanishes for $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$ (see
Section 5.1), so the matrix elements \eqref{eq:19.1.6} and
\eqref{eq:19.1.7} are equal, and thus the Hermitian matrices
$\bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}$,
$\bra{\Omega_u}B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}$, etc., must all commute with one
another. It follows that they can all be simultaneously diagonalized.
Changing if necessary to this basis, we have then for every Hermitian local
operator $A(x)$ of the theory
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega_u}A(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega_v}=\delta_{uv}a_v
 \tag{19.1.8}\label{eq:19.1.8}
\end{equation}
with $a_v$ a real number, the expectation value of $A$ in the state
$\ket{\Omega_v}$. So for infinite volume any Hamiltonian constructed from
local operators will have vanishing matrix elements between the different
vacua $\ket{\Omega_v}$. In the absence of off-diagonal terms in the
Hamiltonian, any two $\ket{\Omega_v}$s connected by a symmetry operation
will be degenerate. A symmetry-breaking perturbation built out of such local
operators will be diagonal in the same basis, and will therefore yield a
ground state that is one of the $\ket{\Omega_v}$s, rather than a linear
combination of them.

It is reassuring that the vacuum states $\ket{\Omega_v}$ which are stable
against small field-dependent perturbations are also vacuum states in which
the cluster decomposition condition (see Chapter 4) is satisfied. This
principle requires that for the physical vacuum state $\ket{\Omega}$
\begin{equation}
 \bra{\Omega}A(\mathbf x)B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega}
 \xrightarrow{\lvert\mathbf x\rvert\to\infty}
 \bra{\Omega}A(\mathbf x)\ket{\Omega}
 \bra{\Omega}B(\mathbf 0)\ket{\Omega}.
 \tag{19.1.9}\label{eq:19.1.9}
\end{equation}
This condition is satisfied if we take the vacuum state $\ket{\Omega}$ to be
any one of the states $\ket{\Omega_v}$ in the basis defined by
\eqref{eq:19.1.8}, but not if we take it to be a general linear combination
of several of the $\ket{\Omega_v}$s.
